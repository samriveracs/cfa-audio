LEWIS: Reading 1 covers what an interest rate is made of, how to measure a return, the three kinds of average, time weighted versus money weighted returns, and adjusting returns for compounding, fees, taxes, inflation and leverage. Reading 2 covers valuing bonds and stocks from their cash flows, backing a return out of a price, and the no arbitrage idea behind forward rates, currency forwards and options.

## Module 1.1, Interest Rates and Return Measurement

HEART: An interest rate is one number doing three jobs, and the exam expects you to recognize all three. It is a required return, the least a lender will accept to hand money over. It is a discount rate, the tool for translating money due in the future into money today. And it is an opportunity cost, what you give up by spending now instead of saving.

LEWIS: The opportunity cost version is the one people forget. Pay cash for a new car instead of leaving that money in savings, and the car really costs you the sticker price plus the interest that money would have earned. The rate is the price of having it now.

HEART: So what is a rate made of? Think of it as a bill the lender hands you, with one line for each thing the lender has to put up with.

HEART: The first line is the real risk free rate. That is pure payment for waiting. No inflation, no chance of default, just compensation for putting off what you could enjoy today. Economists call it time preference. Most people would rather have a concert ticket for this weekend than the same ticket a year from now, and the real risk free rate is the price of that impatience.

LEWIS: The second line is for inflation, because the dollars you get back will buy less than the dollars you lent. Add expected inflation to the real risk free rate and you have the nominal risk free rate.

HEART: That is roughly what a short term Treasury bill pays, and it is a favorite trick question. A T bill is risk free, but its rate is not a real rate. Expected inflation is built into it. Risk free and real are two different ideas.

LEWIS: One precision point. Adding the real rate and inflation is a shortcut. Strictly, the two compound together, so when both are positive the true nominal rate is a touch higher than the simple sum.

HEART: Then come three lines for risk. Default risk is the chance the borrower does not pay on time or in full. Liquidity risk is the chance you have to sell in a hurry and take less than fair value. Maturity risk is the bigger price swings that longer bonds suffer when rates move. Each one adds its own premium.

LEWIS: Picture three loans. One to the federal government for three months. One to a small private company for three months. And one to that same company for twenty years, written in a way that hardly anyone would ever buy it from you. The government loan pays the nominal risk free rate. The short company loan adds a default premium. The long loan nobody wants to buy adds a maturity premium and a liquidity premium on top of that. Every extra thing the lender has to tolerate adds a line to the bill.

HEART: So the full stack is the real risk free rate, plus the inflation premium, plus the default risk premium, plus the liquidity premium, plus the maturity premium. When a question asks why two bonds yield different amounts, walk down that list.

LEWIS: Next, measuring a return. The holding period return is the simplest measure there is. Everything you have at the end, including any cash you were paid along the way, compared with what you started with.

HEART: The income counts. If you own a rental house for a few years, your return is not just the sale price compared with the purchase price. It includes the rent you collected. For a stock, that means the price change plus the dividends, measured against the starting price.

LEWIS: And a holding period has no fixed length. It can be a day or a decade. It is simply start to finish.

HEART: To combine several periods, you compound rather than add, because each period's return builds on whatever balance the last one left behind. The cleanest illustration: a stock that rises 10 percent and then falls 10 percent does not leave you even. The loss comes out of a bigger balance, so you finish a little behind where you started.

LEWIS: It gets dramatic at the extremes. Lose half your money and you need to double what is left just to get back to even. Gains and losses are not symmetric, and that one fact is why we need more than one kind of average.

HEART: The arithmetic mean is the everyday average. Add up the returns and divide by how many there are. It treats each period as a separate draw, and statistically it is an unbiased estimate of the true average. So if someone asks what return to expect next year, the arithmetic mean is the answer.

LEWIS: The geometric mean answers a different question. What one steady rate, compounded every period, would have taken you from your starting value to your ending value? Think of a race run in uneven splits. The geometric mean is the single even pace that would have produced the same finish time.

HEART: Here is the example that makes the difference stick. A stock doubles one year and halves the next. The arithmetic mean says you averaged 25 percent a year. Your account says you are exactly where you started. The geometric mean is zero, and that is the honest description of what happened to your money.

LEWIS: Which gives you the rule. The geometric mean is never above the arithmetic mean. They are equal only when every return is identical, and the more the returns bounce around, the wider the gap between them.

HEART: One subtle point the exam likes. The geometric mean comes out in whatever unit of time you feed it. Feed it quarterly returns and you get a quarterly rate. If you want an annual return from those same quarterly returns, you spread the total growth over the number of years, not the number of periods.

LEWIS: So ask what the question wants. The average per period, or the rate per year? Same data, two different answers, and each is correct for its own question.

HEART: The third average is the harmonic mean, and you already use it without knowing it. Run one mile fast and the next mile slow. Your average speed is not halfway between the two speeds. You spent more time on the slow mile, so the slow speed counts for more, and your true average lands below the simple midpoint. That true average is the harmonic mean.

LEWIS: Investing has exactly the same shape. Put the same dollar amount into a fund every month. When the price is low, your money buys more shares. When the price is high, it buys fewer. You end up owning more of the cheap shares, so your average cost per share comes out below the simple average of the prices. That average cost is the harmonic mean, and it is the whole argument for investing a steady amount every month, often called cost averaging.

HEART: The harmonic mean only works with positive values. To use it on returns that include losses, do what you do for the geometric mean. Work with one plus each return, then subtract one at the end.

LEWIS: And the ordering is worth knowing cold. When the values are not all the same, the harmonic mean is the smallest, the geometric mean sits in the middle, and the arithmetic mean is the largest. You may also see them tied together by saying the arithmetic mean times the harmonic mean equals the geometric mean squared. That holds exactly for two values and approximately for longer lists.

HEART: Last piece of this module, outliers. One wild year can drag an average around, and the arithmetic mean includes every value, extremes and all. Two fixes come back in the statistics reading. A trimmed mean throws out a small share of the highest and lowest values, the way some judged sports drop the top and bottom scores. A winsorized mean keeps the same number of values but pulls the extremes in, replacing them with the most extreme values that remain.

LEWIS: So, which average for which job. Arithmetic when you want every observation included, or an estimate for a single period. Geometric for compound growth over several periods. Harmonic for the average cost of equal dollar purchases. Trimmed or winsorized when outliers distort the picture.

HEART: And the trap. A question gives you several years of returns and asks for the expected return next year. The geometric mean sounds more sophisticated, so it tempts you. But for one future period, it is the arithmetic mean. Ask instead for the compound annual return over the whole stretch, and now it is the geometric mean.

## Module 1.2, Time-Weighted and Money-Weighted Returns

LEWIS: This module is about fairness. A fund manager runs money for clients who add and withdraw whenever they like. At the end of the year, how do you grade the manager?

HEART: It depends whose result you are measuring. One question is how good the manager's decisions were. A different question is how the investor actually did, given when they chose to put money in and take it out. Each question gets its own measure.

LEWIS: The time weighted return grades the manager. The money weighted return grades the investor's wallet.

HEART: Start with money weighted. It is the internal rate of return, the IRR, on the investor's cash flows. Here is the plain English version. Imagine the account had been a savings account paying one steady rate. Which single rate would have turned your exact deposits, on their exact dates, into your exact withdrawals and your ending balance? That rate is your money weighted return. Formally, it is the rate that makes the present value of the money going in equal the present value of the money coming out.

LEWIS: Every flow counts at its real size and on its real date: the starting balance, each deposit, each withdrawal and what is left at the end. Because it is built from dollars, a stretch when you had a lot of money invested counts for far more than a stretch when you had very little.

HEART: Time weighted works differently. It asks how one dollar would have grown if it had sat in the account the whole time, untouched by anyone's deposits or withdrawals. You cut the period into pieces at every deposit or withdrawal, measure the return of each piece, and chain the pieces together by compounding. If the whole stretch is longer than a year, you take the geometric mean to state it per year.

LEWIS: Every piece counts the same no matter how much money was in it, so the client's timing drops out. That is the whole point.

HEART: Here is the story that shows the difference. A client keeps a small amount with a manager through a great year. Pleased, the client sends in a large deposit right at the end of it. The next year is poor. The manager made the same kind of decisions both years. But the investor had little money working in the good year and a lot working in the bad year.

LEWIS: So the money weighted return comes out low, because the bad year carried most of the dollars. The time weighted return gives the good year and the bad year equal weight, so it comes out higher, and it is the fairer grade for the manager.

HEART: Flip the story and the result flips. Add money just before a strong stretch, and the money weighted return beats the time weighted return. The test is always the same question. When was the most money at work, and was that a good stretch or a bad one?

LEWIS: One mechanical trap. When you cut the time weighted pieces, value the account just before each deposit or withdrawal. The new money belongs at the start of the next piece, not at the end of the old one.

HEART: So which measure do you use? The time weighted return is the industry standard for judging managers, because clients, not managers, usually decide when money comes and goes. Blaming a manager for a client's timing would be unfair.

LEWIS: But when the manager does control the timing, the money weighted return is the better measure. A private equity fund is the classic case. The manager decides when to call money from investors and when to hand it back, so that timing is part of the skill being judged.

HEART: The takeaway. Money weighted is the IRR of the investor's own cash flows, so timing matters. Time weighted chains the returns of each piece, so the client's timing drops out, and that is why it is the standard for grading managers.

## Module 1.3, Common Measures of Return

LEWIS: Returns only compare well once they are on the same footing. This module is the toolkit for that: annualizing, compounding frequency, continuous compounding, and the adjustments for fees, taxes, inflation and leverage.

HEART: Start with annualizing. Rates are quoted per year almost everywhere, however long the investment actually lasted. So you ask how many of your holding periods fit into a year, and you compound the return that many times. Count a year as 365 days.

LEWIS: A return earned over a few months gets compounded up to a full year, so the annual figure ends up a bit more than simply scaling it up. A return earned over several years gets spread back down, so the annual figure is smaller than the total.

HEART: The trap with short periods is the hidden assumption. Annualizing says you could repeat that return again and again for a whole year. Annualize one great week in the market and you get an absurd number nobody will ever earn.

LEWIS: Next, compounding frequency. A bank quotes a stated annual rate, but it might credit interest monthly, or even daily. The more often interest is credited, the sooner that interest starts earning interest of its own. So the same stated rate produces a higher effective annual rate the more often it compounds.

HEART: The extra gain shrinks as you go, though. Moving from once a year to twice a year adds a noticeable amount. Moving from monthly to daily adds very little. There is a ceiling, and that ceiling is continuous compounding, interest credited every instant.

LEWIS: Then the direction to remember. More frequent compounding means faster growth, so future values go up. And if money grows faster, you need less of it today to reach the same future amount, so present values go down. Higher future value, lower present value.

HEART: Continuous compounding gives us the continuously compounded return, also called the log return. It answers a simple question. If growth compounded every instant, what rate would produce the change you actually saw? Because instant compounding is the most powerful kind, it takes a smaller rate to get there. So for a gain, the log return is always a little below the holding period return. For a loss, it is a little more negative.

LEWIS: Mechanically, it is the natural log of the ending value divided by the starting value. That ratio, ending over starting, is called the price relative.

HEART: And the reason analysts love log returns is that they add. Simple returns have to be compounded, period by period. Log returns just add up, the way mile splits add up to a race time. The log return for a year is simply the sum of the monthly log returns.

LEWIS: Now fees. Gross return versus net return, and the definitions hide a trap. Trading costs, like commissions, come out of both. They are part of the cost of producing the return in the first place. The only difference is the management and administration fees. Gross is before those fees. Net is after them.

HEART: Gross is the fairer number for comparing managers' skill, because fee schedules differ. Net is what the investor actually keeps.

LEWIS: Taxes. The pretax nominal return is the return before taxes. The after tax nominal return is what is left once the tax bill is paid. In practice, different pieces of a return can face different rates. Dividends, interest, and gains on investments held for a short time or a long time can each be taxed differently. And notice the word nominal. An after tax return is still not adjusted for inflation.

HEART: Which brings us to the real return, the one that measures purchasing power. Imagine you could buy a stack of pizzas today, and instead you invest the money for a year. Your money grows, but pizza prices rise too. Your real return is how many more pizzas you can buy at the end. Roughly, it is your nominal return minus inflation.

LEWIS: But only roughly. The extra money you earned also gets spent at the new, higher prices, so when your return beats inflation, the exact real return is a bit lower than the simple subtraction. The exact version divides. Grow your money, then divide by the growth in prices. When answer choices are close together, that difference decides the question.

HEART: You can also build the real return from the Module 1.1 pieces. Grow by a risk free rate and a risk premium, then divide out inflation. Same idea, assembled from parts.

LEWIS: Last, leverage. A leveraged return is your gain or loss measured against the cash you personally put in, when part of the position is bought with borrowed money. A house bought with a mortgage is the everyday example. The whole house rises or falls in value, but only your down payment absorbs the result.

HEART: So a modest move in the house's value becomes a big move in your equity. Put down a fifth of the price, and every change in the house's value hits your own money about five times as hard, before you count the interest you owe.

LEWIS: In words, a leveraged return is what the whole position earned, minus the interest on the loan, divided by your own money. And that interest is the catch. Leverage helps only when the asset earns more than the borrowing costs. If the asset earns less than the loan rate, you can lose money on an investment that went up. And losses get magnified just as surely as gains.

HEART: Futures work the same way, because you post only a small deposit against a much larger position. And a bank or credit union is a leveraged portfolio too. Its return on equity comes from the spread between what its assets earn and what its funding costs, magnified by how much it borrows relative to its capital.

LEWIS: The takeaway. Annualizing compounds a return to a full year. More frequent compounding raises future values and lowers present values. Log returns add. The real return divides inflation out. And leverage helps only when the asset beats the borrowing rate.

## Module 2.1, Discounted Cash Flow Valuation

HEART: Reading 2 rests on one idea. Every investment is a list of dated payments, and its value today is each payment translated back to today, then added up.

LEWIS: Translating a future payment back to today is called discounting. The intuitive way to hear it: how much would you need to put in the bank today, at the going rate, to have that payment waiting for you on that date? The further away the payment, or the higher the rate, the less you need today.

HEART: Growing money forward and discounting it back are the same relationship read in two directions. And with continuous compounding the logic is identical. Only the growth engine changes, from compounding once a period to compounding every instant.

LEWIS: Start with bonds. The simplest is a zero coupon bond, also called a pure discount bond. It pays one lump, its face value, at maturity, and nothing before. You buy it for less than face value, and the gap between what you pay and what you get back is your interest.

HEART: Two forces set its price, the yield and the time to maturity. A higher yield or a longer wait both push the price down, as long as the yield is positive. It also works in reverse. Know the price, the face value and the time, and you can back out the yield.

LEWIS: Here is a twist. Yields can be negative. In the 2010s, parts of Europe and Japan saw government bond yields below zero. A zero coupon bond with a negative yield costs more than its face value. You knowingly pay more today than you will get back.

HEART: Why would anyone do that? Think of it as a fee for very safe storage. When investors badly want safety, some will accept a small, certain loss to park money somewhere they trust completely.

LEWIS: Next, the fixed coupon bond. It pays a set interest payment every period, plus the face value at the end. And here is the distinction to lock in. The coupon rate only sets the size of each payment. The yield to maturity is the discount rate the market applies to those payments. They are two different numbers.

HEART: Think of a long lease. The coupon rate is the rent written into it the day it was signed. The yield is the return a buyer of that lease would demand today. Nothing forces the two to match.

LEWIS: And comparing them tells you the price. When the market yield equals the coupon rate, the bond sells at its face value, called par. When the market yield is above the coupon rate, the bond's payments are smaller than the market demands, so it sells below par, at a discount. When the market yield is below the coupon rate, it sells above par, at a premium.

HEART: A perpetual bond, or perpetuity, pays a fixed amount forever and never matures. Its value is simply the payment divided by the rate.

LEWIS: The bank account picture makes that obvious. How much would you need to deposit, at the going rate, to collect that payment every year forever without ever touching the principal? That deposit is the perpetuity's value. It also tells you something useful. Double the rate and the value halves.

HEART: One detail to file away. That value sits one period before the first payment. It matters for stocks in a moment.

LEWIS: The last bond shape is the amortizing bond. It pays the same amount every period, including the final one, and each payment covers that period's interest plus a slice of principal. By the last payment, the balance is zero. A car loan or a mortgage works this way, and a level stream of payments like that is called an annuity. The payment is set so that the present values of all the payments, at the loan rate, add up to exactly the amount borrowed.

HEART: Contrast that with a fixed coupon bond, where every coupon is pure interest and the whole principal comes back at the end. With an amortizing loan, the principal comes back a piece at a time. Each payment carries a little less interest and a little more principal than the one before it. On a long mortgage, the early payments are mostly interest.

LEWIS: Now equities. Same principle, value is the present value of future cash flows. Two things change. Stocks never mature, and their cash flows are not promised. So instead of a yield, we talk about the required return, the return investors need before they will hold the stock. It plays the role of the discount rate.

HEART: Preferred stock pays a fixed dividend, set as a percentage of its par value, and it has no maturity. So it is a perpetuity, and its value is the dividend divided by the required return. Same warning as with bonds. The stated dividend rate decides the size of the payment, and the required return is what you discount it at. Mixing them up is the trap.

LEWIS: Common stock is a residual claim. Shareholders get what is left after everyone else is paid, and the company can raise, cut or skip the dividend as it chooses. Nothing is promised, so analysts value it with the dividend discount model, which comes in three versions.

HEART: Version one, assume the dividend stays the same forever. Then common stock is valued just like preferred stock, as a perpetuity.

LEWIS: Version two, assume the dividend grows at a steady rate forever. That is the Gordon growth model, also called the constant growth model. Value today is next year's dividend divided by the gap between the required return and the growth rate.

HEART: It is the perpetuity idea with a twist. The payment keeps growing, so it is as if you divide by a smaller rate, the required return minus growth. And notice what that implies. When the gap is narrow, small changes in growth or in the required return swing the value a lot.

LEWIS: The model needs the required return to be above the growth rate. If dividends grew as fast as you discount them, every future dividend would be worth as much today as the next one, forever, and the value would be infinite. The math only works with a positive gap.

HEART: Now the trap that costs the most points in this reading. The model wants next year's dividend. If the question says the company just paid a dividend, that dividend is in the past. Grow it by one year first. Plug in the dividend just paid and you undervalue the stock, and that wrong answer will be sitting in the choices.

LEWIS: So listen for the wording. Just paid, most recent or last means the dividend already happened, so grow it once. Expected, next or coming means it is already next year's dividend, so use it as it is.

HEART: Version three is the multistage model, for a company that grows fast for a while and then settles into steady growth. The strategy is to value the bumpy early dividends one at a time, then collapse the entire steady part, all the way to forever, into one lump using the Gordon model.

LEWIS: And here is where that timing detail pays off. The Gordon model, applied to a dividend, gives a value one period before that dividend. So you apply it to the first dividend of the steady growth phase, and the lump you get sits one period earlier. Then you bring that lump back to today, along with the early dividends.

HEART: The classic mistake is discounting the lump for one period too many or one too few. Before you discount it, always ask on what date the Gordon value sits.

LEWIS: The takeaway. Every asset is the sum of its discounted payments. A perpetuity is the payment divided by the rate. And the Gordon model divides next year's dividend by the gap between the required return and growth, landing one period before that dividend.

## Module 2.2, Implied Returns and Cash Flow Additivity

HEART: Module 2.1 started with a rate and found a price. This module runs it in reverse. Start with the price the market shows you, and back out the return that price implies.

LEWIS: For a zero coupon bond, the implied return is the steady annual rate that grows today's price into the face value by maturity. For a coupon bond, it is the yield to maturity, the single discount rate that makes all the promised payments add up to today's price. In other words, the bond's internal rate of return.

HEART: That gives you a relationship you will use for the rest of the course. Bond prices and yields move in opposite directions. The payments are fixed, so if the market starts demanding a higher return, the only way to deliver it is a lower price. When the price rises, the yield falls.

LEWIS: For stocks, you cannot look up the required return. Nobody publishes it. But you can see the price. So you rearrange the Gordon model.

HEART: And the result reads naturally. The required return equals the dividend yield plus the growth rate. The dividend yield is next year's dividend divided by today's price. So your return comes from two sources: the cash the stock pays relative to what you paid for it, and the growth of that cash over time.

LEWIS: Turn it around and you get implied growth. If you know the return investors require, subtract the dividend yield, and what is left is the growth rate the market is pricing in. That makes a good reality check. If a price only makes sense with growth no company could sustain forever, the price is telling you something.

HEART: Now the principle the rest of the course leans on. Cash flow additivity says the present value of a stream of payments equals the sum of the present values of its pieces. Cut a stream into pieces and the pieces add back to the value of the whole. Merge two streams date by date and the result is worth the two values added together.

LEWIS: Think of Lego. A fixed coupon bond is really two simpler pieces snapped together, a level annuity of coupons plus a single lump at maturity, which is just a zero coupon bond. Value the two pieces separately, add them, and you get exactly the value of the bond.

HEART: Building one set of cash flows out of others like that is called replication. Any uneven stream can be broken into a level annuity plus a few single payments, which is often easier to value.

LEWIS: Here is why it matters so much. If two investments pay exactly the same cash flows in every possible outcome, they must cost the same today. That is the law of one price, also called the no arbitrage condition.

HEART: Because if they did not, you would buy the cheap one, sell the expensive one, and collect the difference with no risk and none of your own money. That free lunch is called arbitrage. Traders pile in, and their buying and selling pushes the prices together until it disappears.

LEWIS: The simplest way to hold it: two routes to the same destination must cost the same toll. This reading uses it to pin down three prices: forward interest rates, forward exchange rates and option values.

HEART: First, interest rates. A spot rate is the rate on a loan that starts today. A forward rate is a rate agreed today for a loan that starts later. The notation gives you both facts, the start first and the length second. One y one y is a one year loan starting one year from now. Two y five y is a five year loan starting in two years.

LEWIS: Now the two routes. You want to invest for two years. Route one, lock in the two year spot rate today. Route two, invest for one year at the one year spot rate, and agree today to reinvest for the second year at the one y one y forward rate. Both routes are certain and cover the same two years, so they must end with the same amount of money.

HEART: That is how forward rates are inferred from the spot rates you can see in the market. And it chains. Any mix of spot and forward rates covering the same stretch of time has to produce the same total growth.

LEWIS: A useful mental picture. The two year spot rate is roughly the average of the first year's rate and the forward rate for the second year. So when spot rates rise with maturity, the forward rate has to be above both spot rates, not between them. The forward is what pulls that average up.

HEART: Second, exchange rates. An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. In a quote of dollars per euro, the dollar is the price currency and the euro is the base currency. The quote tells you how many dollars one euro costs.

LEWIS: The two routes here. Keep your money in dollars and earn the dollar interest rate. Or convert to euros today, earn the euro interest rate, and lock in a forward rate today to convert back in a year. Both routes are riskless, so they must end with the same number of dollars.

HEART: Which means the forward rate exists to cancel the interest rate difference. In percentage terms, the forward differs from the spot rate by roughly the difference between the two interest rates. The currency with the higher interest rate trades at a forward discount. Its extra interest is given back when you convert at the forward rate.

LEWIS: If that were not true, people would borrow in the low rate currency, invest in the high rate one, lock in the forward, and pocket a riskless profit. That trade is exactly what keeps the forward rate in line.

HEART: Two traps. The forward rate is not a forecast of where the exchange rate is headed. It is simply the price that removes the free lunch. And keep each interest rate with its own currency. In a dollars per euro quote, when the dollar rate is above the euro rate, the forward sits above the spot. When the euro rate is higher, the forward sits below it.

LEWIS: Third, options. An option is a right, not an obligation. A call is the right to buy an asset at a set price, called the exercise price. A put is the right to sell at a set price. If using the option would not pay, the owner simply lets it expire. An option that would pay to exercise is in the money. One that would not is out of the money.

HEART: That walk away feature is what makes options different to value. But the no arbitrage idea still works, using a one period binomial model. Binomial just means two outcomes. By the end of the period, the stock sits at either a higher price or a lower price.

LEWIS: You need four inputs. The stock's value today, the option's exercise price, the size of the up move and the down move, and the risk free rate for the period.

HEART: Then comes the clever part. Sell a call option, and buy just enough of the stock that your combined position is worth the same whether the stock goes up or down. When the stock rises, your shares gain, but the call you sold costs you. When the stock falls, your shares lose value, but the call expires worthless. With the right amount of stock, those effects offset exactly.

LEWIS: That right amount is the hedge ratio, the number of shares per option. It is set by how far the option's payoff swings between the two outcomes, compared with how far the stock's price swings.

HEART: And now the logic closes. A position worth the same in every outcome has no risk. A riskless position must earn the risk free rate, or there would be an arbitrage. So you know what the hedged position is worth today. The option's price is the difference between what the shares cost and what that hedged position is worth.

LEWIS: You will do this properly in Derivatives. For now, notice the pattern is the same in all three examples. Build something riskless out of pieces, insist that it earns the risk free rate, and the unknown price falls out.

HEART: The takeaway. A market price implies a return, the yield to maturity for a bond and the dividend yield plus growth for a stock. Bond prices and yields move in opposite directions. And identical cash flows must have identical prices, which is what sets forward rates, currency forwards and option values.

## Recall check

LEWIS Q: A Treasury bill is about as close to risk free as it gets. Is its rate a real rate or a nominal rate?

HEART: Answer. Nominal. Here is why. Risk free only means no default risk. It says nothing about inflation. Lenders expect each dollar they get back to buy a little less than each dollar they lent, so the T bill rate carries an inflation premium to cover that. Real means inflation has been stripped out, and a T bill rate has not had that done.

HEART Q: You have ten years of annual returns. Which average estimates next year's return, and which describes the compound growth over the full ten years?

LEWIS: Answer. The arithmetic mean for next year, the geometric mean for the ten years. Here is why. Next year is a single draw, and the arithmetic mean is the best estimate of a typical single period. Ten years is a chain of returns where each one builds on the last, and only the geometric mean captures that compounding. The double then halve example shows the gap. The arithmetic mean says you did well, the geometric mean says you went nowhere, and your account agrees with the geometric mean.

LEWIS Q: You invest the same dollar amount every month. Which mean gives your average cost per share, and is it above or below the simple average of the prices?

HEART: Answer. The harmonic mean, and it is below the simple average. Here is why. A fixed dollar amount buys more shares when the price is low and fewer when it is high. So you own more of the cheap shares, and the low prices count for more in your true cost. The simple average treats every price as if you bought the same number of shares, which you did not.

HEART Q: A client makes a large deposit right before the portfolio's best year. Which is likely higher, the money weighted return or the time weighted return?

LEWIS: Answer. The money weighted return. Here is why. Money weighted counts dollars, so the period with the most money at work dominates, and here that was the best year. Time weighted gives every period equal say no matter how much money was in it, so the client's lucky timing does not lift it. Good timing helps money weighted, bad timing hurts it, and time weighted ignores timing altogether.

LEWIS Q: The stated annual rate stays the same, but interest now compounds monthly instead of once a year. What happens to the present value of a payment due in a year?

HEART: Answer. It falls. Here is why. Monthly compounding means interest starts earning interest sooner, so money grows faster and the effective annual rate rises. If money grows faster, you need to set aside less today to reach the same amount in a year. Faster growth forward means a smaller value today.

HEART Q: Why are continuously compounded returns so convenient across many periods?

LEWIS: Answer. They add. Here is why. Simple returns have to be compounded, because each period builds on the balance the last one left. Continuously compounded returns are log returns, and a log turns multiplying into adding. So the log return for a whole year is just the sum of its monthly log returns, the way mile splits add up to a finish time.

LEWIS Q: You borrow money to buy an asset. Under what condition does the leverage raise your return?

HEART: Answer. When the asset earns more than the interest rate on the loan. Here is why. Every borrowed dollar has to pay its interest before you keep anything. If the asset earns more than that rate, each borrowed dollar leaves you a profit, and your return on your own money rises. If it earns less, each borrowed dollar costs you money. And because the whole position rests on your small slice of equity, losses get magnified just like gains.

HEART Q: A bond's coupon rate is below the market yield. Does it trade at a premium, at par or at a discount?

LEWIS: Answer. At a discount. Here is why. The coupon is fixed. If the market now demands a higher return than those coupons provide, the only way a buyer can earn the market yield is by paying less up front. The lower price makes up for the smaller payments.

LEWIS Q: A question gives you the dividend a company just paid and asks for the Gordon growth value. What do you do with that dividend first?

HEART: Answer. Grow it by one period to get next year's dividend. Here is why. The Gordon model values the stream of dividends still to come, starting with the next one. The dividend just paid already went to someone else. Plug it in as it is and you start the stream too low, so you undervalue the stock, and that wrong answer will be sitting in the choices.

HEART Q: Working backward from a stock's price, what two pieces make up the return the market implies?

LEWIS: Answer. The dividend yield plus the growth rate. Here is why. A shareholder earns in two ways. Cash arrives as dividends, and next year's dividend measured against today's price is the dividend yield. The dividends themselves grow over time, and in the constant growth model the share price grows at that same rate. Add the two and you have the total return the price implies.

LEWIS Q: Spot rates rise with maturity. Is the forward rate for the second year above, below or between the one year and two year spot rates?

HEART: Answer. Above both. Here is why. Investing for two years at the two year rate has to match investing for one year at the one year rate and then rolling at the forward rate. So the two year rate is roughly the average of the first year's rate and the forward. Since the two year rate is above the one year rate, the forward has to sit above the two year rate to pull the average up that far.

HEART Q: In a dollars per euro quote, the dollar interest rate rises while the euro rate stays put. Does the forward rate rise or fall relative to the spot rate?

LEWIS: Answer. It rises. Here is why. Holding dollars now earns more interest. If the forward stayed put, you could borrow euros, swap them into dollars, earn the higher dollar rate and lock in the old forward to swap back, keeping a riskless profit. To remove that, the dollar has to be worth less in the forward market, which means more dollars per euro.

## Key takeaways

HEART: First, a few memory hooks.

LEWIS: For the parts of an interest rate: real rate, inflation, default, liquidity, maturity. Rarely Is Debt Lent Mindlessly. Every nominal rate has the first two. The last three get added only when the loan carries that risk.

HEART: For the three averages, whenever the values differ, alphabetical order runs from biggest to smallest. Arithmetic, then geometric, then harmonic. And each has a job. Arithmetic looks ahead one period. Geometric measures growth over time. Harmonic is the average cost when you invest the same dollar amount each time.

LEWIS: For the two performance measures: time weighted grades the talent, money weighted follows the money. One exception. If the manager decides when money goes in and out, money weighted is the fairer grade.

HEART: Compound more often, and future values rise while present values fall. Log returns add, simple returns compound. For real return, subtract to estimate and divide to be exact. And commissions come out of both gross and net returns, while management fees come out of net only.

LEWIS: For bonds: price and yield sit on a seesaw. Coupon above the yield means a price above par. For the Gordon model: it wants next year's dividend, and its answer lands one period before that dividend. Required return is dividend yield plus growth.

HEART: For forwards: when spot rates rise with maturity, the forward sits on top. The currency with the higher interest rate trades at a forward discount, giving back its extra interest. And underneath all of it sits the law of one price. Same cash flows, same price, or arbitrage closes the gap.

LEWIS: Now, how you would say the big ideas to a senior leader who does not live in this material.

HEART: On interest rates: every rate is a reward for waiting, plus cover for expected inflation, plus a charge for each risk the lender takes. When two rates differ, ask which risk the higher one is paying for. The jargon is the real risk free rate plus premiums for inflation, default, liquidity and maturity.

LEWIS: On performance: the time weighted return tells you how good the manager was. The money weighted return tells you how the investor actually did, including the effect of when they put money in.

HEART: On leverage: borrowing magnifies results in both directions, and it only lifts the return on our own capital when the assets earn more than the funding costs, while adding risk either way. That is the same logic behind a bank's return on equity.

LEWIS: And on valuation: anything is worth the cash it will pay you, adjusted for when you get it and how risky it is. And two things that pay the same must cost the same, or traders will force their prices together. The jargon is discounted cash flow valuation and the law of one price.
