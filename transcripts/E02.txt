LEWIS: Reading 3 covers how to describe a set of returns: where the center is, how spread out they are, whether they lean to one side, how fat the tails are, and how two variables move together. Reading 4 covers expected values from probability models, probability trees, conditional expectations, and updating a forecast with Bayes' formula.

## Module 3.1, Central Tendency and Dispersion

HEART: Every dataset raises two questions first. Where is the middle, and how spread out is everything around it? The middle is called central tendency. The spread is called dispersion. In investing, the middle stands for reward and the spread stands for risk, so these two ideas run through the rest of the course.

LEWIS: Start with the middle. There are three main measures, and they answer slightly different questions.

HEART: The arithmetic mean is the everyday average. Add up the values and divide by how many there are. When the data are a sample drawn from a bigger population, that average is called the sample mean, and we use it to estimate the mean of the whole population.

LEWIS: The median is the middle value once you line everything up in order. Half the observations sit above it and half below. With an even number of values there is no single middle one, so you take the average of the two in the middle.

HEART: And the reason the median matters is outliers. Picture ten people having coffee in a small cafe. A billionaire walks in. The average wealth in the room just jumped to tens of millions of dollars a head, but nobody else in the room got richer. The median barely moves, because it only cares about order, not size. When a few extreme values distort the picture, or might even be data errors, the median is the better measure of what is typical.

LEWIS: The mode is the value that shows up most often. A shoe store cares about the mode, because it wants to stock the size most people wear. A dataset can have one mode, which is called unimodal, two modes, bimodal, three, trimodal, or no mode at all.

HEART: There is a catch with investment returns. They are continuous, so no two returns are likely to match exactly, and a single most common value means nothing. Instead you sort the returns into ranges, like buckets, and the bucket that collects the most observations is called the modal interval.

LEWIS: Module 1.1 introduced two ways to tame outliers, and this module adds the detail. A trimmed mean throws away a stated percentage of the most extreme values, split evenly between the top and the bottom, and averages what is left. A winsorized mean keeps every observation but caps the extremes. Anything below a chosen low percentile is replaced by that percentile's value, and anything above a chosen high percentile is replaced by that one.

HEART: So trimming cuts the extremes out, and winsorizing pulls them in. Same goal, less influence from the wild values.

LEWIS: Now measures of location. Line the data up and pick a share, like a quarter or a tenth. The quantile is the cutoff with that share of the data at or below it. Quartiles cut the data into four equal parts. Quintiles into five. Deciles into ten. Percentiles into a hundred.

HEART: Think of test scores. Score in the top tenth and you are in the top decile. Every quantile has a percentile twin. The first quartile is the twenty fifth percentile, the second is the median, and the third, with three quarters of the data at or below it, is the seventy fifth.

LEWIS: The gap between the third quartile and the first is the interquartile range. It is the spread of the middle half of the data, and because it ignores the top and bottom quarters, extreme values cannot stretch it.

HEART: The standard picture is a box and whisker plot. The box usually spans the middle half of the data, from the first quartile to the third, and a whisker runs from each end toward the smallest and largest values. If one whisker is much longer than the other, the data stretch further on that side, which hints at outliers in that direction.

LEWIS: That brings us to dispersion, the measures of risk. The simplest is the range, the largest value minus the smallest. It is quick, but it depends entirely on two observations, so a single extreme value can make it misleading.

HEART: Next is the mean absolute deviation, or MAD. Measure how far each observation sits from the mean, ignore whether it is above or below, and average those distances. In plain words, it is the average distance from the average.

LEWIS: Why ignore the direction?

HEART: Because if you keep the signs, the distances above the mean and below the mean cancel out exactly. The deviations from the arithmetic mean always add up to zero. That is not a coincidence, it is what the mean is. So you take absolute values to keep the distances from erasing each other.

LEWIS: The variance takes a different route around the same problem. Instead of dropping the signs, it squares each deviation. Squaring makes every deviation positive, and it also punishes big misses far more than small ones. The sample variance adds up those squared deviations and divides by the number of observations minus one.

HEART: That minus one is a favorite exam point, so here is the intuition. A sample's values tend to sit closer to their own sample mean than to the true population mean, because the sample mean was calculated from those very values. So the squared deviations come out a little too small. Divide by the full count and you would systematically underestimate the population's variance. Dividing by one less nudges the answer up just enough to fix that. The result is called an unbiased estimator.

LEWIS: The trouble with variance is its units. Square percentages and you get percent squared, which nobody can picture. So we take the square root and get the standard deviation, which is back in the same units as the data. In plain words, the standard deviation is how far a typical observation lands from the mean.

HEART: Now a comparison problem. Say a utility stock has a modest average return and modest swings, and a biotech stock has a much higher average return with much bigger swings. Which is riskier for the reward you get? Comparing standard deviations alone is unfair, because the biotech is aiming for so much more.

LEWIS: That is what the coefficient of variation is for. It is a measure of relative dispersion, spread judged against the size of the average. It is the standard deviation divided by the mean. It tells you how much risk you take on for each unit of return. Lower is better, because it means less variability for every unit of reward. It lets you compare dispersion across datasets with very different averages.

HEART: Last in this module, downside risk. Standard deviation counts surprises on both sides of the mean, but most investors do not lose sleep over returns that beat their expectations. Picture a retiree who needs a certain return each year to cover spending. Only the years that fall short of that target hurt.

LEWIS: The measure for that is target downside deviation, also called target semideviation. You pick a target, look only at the observations that fall below it, square how far short each one fell, add those up, divide, and take the square root.

HEART: And here is the trap. Even though only the shortfalls go into the top of the calculation, the bottom still uses the full sample size minus one. You do not shrink the denominator to the number of shortfalls.

LEWIS: The takeaway. Mean, median and mode describe the middle, and the median resists outliers. Range, MAD, variance and standard deviation describe the spread. The coefficient of variation scales risk by return, and target downside deviation counts only the bad side.

## Module 3.2, Skewness, Kurtosis, and Correlation

HEART: The mean and standard deviation tell you a lot, but not the shape. Two sets of returns can share the same mean and the same standard deviation and still behave very differently. This module is about shape, and then about how two variables move together.

LEWIS: Start with symmetry. Fold a symmetrical distribution at its mean and the two halves match, like the classic bell curve. In return terms, falling short of the average by a given amount is just as likely as beating it by the same amount.

HEART: Skewness measures how far a distribution departs from that symmetry. It comes from outliers sitting mostly on one side.

LEWIS: A positively skewed distribution, also called skewed right, has a long tail stretching out to the right. Most outcomes are modest, with an occasional huge winner. Think of a portfolio of startup investments. Most companies return little or nothing, but once in a while one becomes enormous.

HEART: A negatively skewed distribution, skewed left, has the long tail on the left. Most outcomes are steady and pleasant, with an occasional big loss. A bond with credit risk behaves like this. Year after year you collect the coupons, and then once in a long while the issuer defaults and you take a heavy hit. Strategies that sell insurance against rare events look like this too.

LEWIS: Skew moves the three measures of the middle apart, and the exam tests the order. In a symmetrical distribution, the mean, median and mode are all the same. In a skewed one with a single peak, the mean gets pulled toward the long tail, because it feels the size of every outlier. The mode stays at the peak. The median sits in between.

HEART: So with positive skew, the mean is the largest, then the median, then the mode. With negative skew, it flips. The mean is the smallest, then the median, then the mode. A handy way to hear it: start at the tail and say them in alphabetical order. Mean, median, mode.

LEWIS: You will not need to compute skewness, but you should know how it is built. The sample measure averages the cubed deviations from the mean and scales by the standard deviation cubed. Cubing keeps the sign, so if the big deviations sit above the mean the result is positive, and if they sit below it is negative. Scaling by the standard deviation makes it comparable across datasets. As a rule of thumb, a skewness beyond one half in either direction is considered significant.

HEART: Now kurtosis. Kurtosis describes how peaked a distribution is and, more importantly, how fat its tails are, compared with a normal distribution.

LEWIS: There are three names to know. Leptokurtic means more peaked than normal, with fatter tails. Platykurtic means flatter than normal, with thinner tails. Mesokurtic means the same as normal.

HEART: A leptokurtic distribution is the interesting one for investors. Compared with a bell curve of the same standard deviation, more of its outcomes cluster near the middle, and more of them land far out in the tails. What it has less of is the in between. Daily stock market returns look like this. Most days are quiet, and every so often there is a day that a normal distribution says should almost never happen.

LEWIS: Kurtosis has an anchor. Every normal distribution scores three. Many analysts subtract that three and report excess kurtosis, so the bell curve scores zero, a fat tailed leptokurtic distribution scores above zero, and a thin tailed platykurtic one scores below. The sample measure is built like skewness, but with deviations raised to the fourth power, which makes the extreme observations count enormously.

HEART: And this is why risk managers care. A bell curve model treats a market crash in a single day as practically impossible, yet markets keep producing them. Real returns usually show some skew and fatter tails than the bell curve allows, so a normal model understates how often big losses arrive. Risk work therefore centers on the tails. The exam rule: negative skew and positive excess kurtosis each mean more risk.

LEWIS: Now, two variables at once. The first tool is a scatter plot. Put one variable on each axis and plot each pair of observations as a point. A cloud with no pattern means no clear relationship. Points hugging a straight line mean a strong linear relationship.

HEART: And scatter plots catch something the statistics can miss: curved relationships. Think of electricity demand against temperature. Demand rises on very cold days and on very hot days, and it is lowest in the mild middle. Plot it and you get a clear U shape, a strong relationship. Yet the correlation can come out near zero, because correlation only measures straight line relationships.

LEWIS: The statistics themselves. Covariance measures how two variables move together. Positive covariance means they tend to move in the same direction, negative means opposite directions. Like the sample variance, the sample covariance divides by the sample size minus one.

HEART: Covariance has two problems. Its units are the product of the two variables' units, so it is hard to interpret, and its size depends on how you measure things. Record the same prices in cents instead of dollars and the covariance balloons, even though nothing real has changed. So covariance gives you direction but tells you nothing useful about strength.

LEWIS: The fix is correlation, the correlation coefficient. You take the covariance and divide by the two standard deviations. That strips out the units and leaves a pure number between minus one and plus one.

HEART: Plus one is perfect positive correlation. The two move in lockstep, in proportion, in the same direction. Minus one is perfect negative correlation, in lockstep but in opposite directions. Zero means no linear relationship, so you cannot predict one from the other with a straight line. That does not rule out a curved relationship, as the electricity example showed.

LEWIS: Then come the warnings. Correlation is not causation. Stock prices and consumer confidence tend to rise together, but the number cannot tell you whether rising prices cheer people up, confident people buy stocks, or something else drives both. So the careful word is association.

HEART: A classic example. Ice cream sales and drownings rise and fall together. Ice cream does not cause drowning. Hot weather drives both. That is spurious correlation, which is correlation that arises by pure chance, or because both variables are linked to some third variable.

LEWIS: And check the outliers. Two stocks that barely move together can show a strong correlation because of one crash day when both plunged. If dropping a few extreme points changes the answer a lot, find out whether they reflect something real or just noise.

HEART: The takeaway. Skewness describes which side the long tail is on, and the mean follows the tail. Kurtosis describes fat tails, and positive excess kurtosis means extreme outcomes are more common than a normal distribution predicts. Covariance gives direction, correlation gives direction and strength, but only for straight line relationships, and never proof of cause.

## Module 4.1, Probability Models, Expected Values, and Bayes' Formula

LEWIS: Reading 3 described data that already happened. Reading 4 looks forward. Instead of a sample of past returns, you start with a probability model: a list of possible outcomes and the probability of each.

HEART: The expected value is the probability weighted average of those outcomes. Think of how an analyst builds a forecast. A bull case, a base case and a bear case, each with a probability. The expected value weights each scenario by its probability and adds them up. It is the center of the forecast.

LEWIS: One thing to notice. The expected value does not have to be a possible outcome. Flip a coin for a dollar or nothing, and the expected value is fifty cents, an amount you can never actually receive. It is the long run average, not a prediction of any single result.

HEART: The spread around that expected value is measured the same way as before, with one twist. The variance is the probability weighted average of the squared deviations from the expected value. The standard deviation is its square root.

LEWIS: And here is the distinction to lock in. There is no dividing by the sample size minus one here. The minus one repairs the bias from measuring spread around a mean estimated from the same data. A probability model estimates nothing. It is a complete list of outcomes and their probabilities, so it already is the whole population, and the probabilities do the weighting. Sample statistics look back at data and divide by one less than the count. Probability models look forward and weight by probability.

HEART: Forecasts often unfold in stages, and a probability tree lays them out. The first branches might split on the central bank raising rates or holding. Then each branch splits again on whether a homebuilder's sales rise or fall, given that decision.

LEWIS: The probabilities on the second set of branches are conditional probabilities, the chance of sales rising given a rate hike, for example. To get the probability of any full path through the tree, you multiply along its branches. That gives the joint probability of both things happening. The probabilities of all the endpoints add up to one.

HEART: Then the expected value of, say, the homebuilder's profit is just the endpoint outcomes, each weighted by its joint probability. Multiply along the branches, then add across the endpoints.

LEWIS: That leads to conditional expected values. A conditional expected value is the expected value given that some event has happened. Think of an airline. Its expected earnings, given that jet fuel prices spike, are lower than its expected earnings given that fuel stays cheap.

HEART: That is exactly how analysts revise forecasts when news arrives. Before you know what fuel prices will do, the overall expected value is a blend of the conditional expectations, each weighted by the probability of its scenario, as long as the scenarios cover every possibility without overlapping. When the news lands, you drop the scenarios that did not happen and move to the conditional expectation for the one that did.

LEWIS: Which brings us to Bayes' formula, the disciplined way to update a probability when new information arrives. You start with a prior probability, what you believed before the news. You end with an updated probability, also called a posterior.

HEART: In words, the updated probability equals the prior probability times a ratio. On top of the ratio is the probability of seeing this new information if the event is true. On the bottom is the overall, or unconditional, probability of seeing that information at all. If the evidence is more likely when the event is true than it is in general, the ratio is above one and your probability goes up. If it is less likely, your probability goes down.

LEWIS: The unconditional probability on the bottom comes from the total probability rule. You add up every way the information could have shown up: the joint probability that the event is true and you see the information, plus the joint probability that it is false and you still see the information.

HEART: Picture it on a tree. Find every branch that ends in what you actually observed. Bayes says the updated probability is the branch you care about divided by all the branches that lead to what you saw.

LEWIS: Here is a picture with simple counts, because Bayes surprises people. A lender has a model that flags risky borrowers. Out of every hundred borrowers, five will default. The model catches four of those five. But it also flags ten of the ninety five good borrowers.

HEART: So out of every hundred borrowers, fourteen get flagged, and only four of those fourteen actually default. Given a flag, the chance of default is under a third. The flag raised it a lot from the starting point of five in a hundred, but it is nowhere near certain.

LEWIS: Why so low when the model catches most defaulters? Because defaults are rare. There are so many more good borrowers that even a small error rate among them produces more false alarms than true ones. That is the classic lesson of Bayes. Never ignore the prior, the base rate.

HEART: The takeaway. Expected value is a probability weighted average, and its variance is weighted by probabilities, not divided by the sample size minus one. On a tree, multiply along the branches and add across the endpoints. Conditional expectations let you revise a forecast as news arrives. And Bayes updates a prior by how much more likely the evidence is under one explanation than overall.

## Recall check

LEWIS Q: A billionaire walks into a small cafe. Which moves more, the mean wealth in the room or the median, and which better describes the typical person there?

HEART: Answer. The mean jumps, the median barely moves, and the median better describes the typical person. Here is why. The mean adds up every value, so one enormous value drags it a long way. The median only depends on which value sits in the middle of the line, so one extreme value at the end of the line barely shifts it.

HEART Q: What percentile is the third quartile, and what does the interquartile range measure?

LEWIS: Answer. The seventy fifth percentile, and the interquartile range measures the spread of the middle half of the data. Here is why. Quartiles cut the data into four equal parts, so three quarters of the data sit below the third quartile. The interquartile range runs from the first quartile to the third, which leaves out the bottom and top quarters, so extreme values cannot stretch it.

LEWIS Q: Why does the mean absolute deviation use absolute values instead of the raw deviations?

HEART: Answer. Because the raw deviations from the mean always add up to zero. Here is why. The mean is the balance point of the data, so the distances above it exactly offset the distances below it. Dropping the signs keeps those distances from cancelling, so you get a true measure of spread.

HEART Q: Why does the sample variance divide by the sample size minus one instead of the full sample size?

LEWIS: Answer. To avoid underestimating the population's variance. Here is why. The sample mean is calculated from the sample itself, so the sample's values sit closer to it than they would to the true population mean. That makes the squared deviations a little too small. Dividing by one less makes up for it, and the result is an unbiased estimator.

LEWIS Q: Two funds have very different average returns and very different standard deviations. Which measure compares their risk fairly, and is higher or lower better?

HEART: Answer. The coefficient of variation, and lower is better. Here is why. It divides the standard deviation by the mean, so it measures risk per unit of return. That puts a high return fund and a low return fund on the same footing, and a lower number means less variability for each unit of reward.

HEART Q: A retiree only cares about years that fall short of the return she needs. Which risk measure fits, and what is the trap in calculating it?

LEWIS: Answer. Target downside deviation, also called target semideviation. Here is why. It counts only the observations below the target, so good surprises do not count as risk. The trap is the denominator. It still uses the full sample size minus one, not just the number of shortfalls. The years that beat the target are still part of the record. They simply count as a shortfall of zero.

LEWIS Q: A return distribution has a long tail to the left. What is the sign of its skewness, and how do the mean, median and mode line up?

HEART: Answer. Negative skewness, with the mean below the median and the median below the mode. Here is why. The mean feels the size of every outlier, so the big losses in the left tail drag it down the most. The mode stays at the peak of the distribution, and the median sits in between.

HEART Q: A return distribution is leptokurtic. What does that say about its tails, what is the sign of its excess kurtosis, and why does a risk manager care?

LEWIS: Answer. Fat tails and positive excess kurtosis. Here is why. Leptokurtic means more outcomes near the middle and more far out in the tails than a normal distribution. A model that assumes normal returns will therefore underestimate how often extreme losses happen, which is exactly the risk a risk manager is paid to see.

LEWIS Q: Two variables have a correlation close to zero, but their scatter plot shows a clear U shape. Is there a relationship?

HEART: Answer. Yes, a strong curved one. Here is why. Correlation only measures straight line relationships. In a U shape, one variable rises at both ends and falls in the middle, so the straight line effects cancel out and the correlation looks like nothing. The scatter plot shows what the number hides.

HEART Q: Why is correlation more useful than covariance for judging how strongly two returns move together?

LEWIS: Answer. Because correlation has no units and is bounded between minus one and plus one. Here is why. Covariance depends on the units of measurement, so its size can balloon or shrink without anything real changing. Dividing by both standard deviations removes the units, which turns direction into a measure of strength you can compare.

LEWIS Q: Why does a variance calculated from a probability model not divide by one less than the number of observations?

HEART: Answer. Because a probability model has no sample to correct for. Here is why. The minus one repairs the bias from measuring spread around a mean estimated from the same data. A probability model estimates nothing. It already lists every outcome with its probability, so it describes the whole distribution, and the probabilities do the weighting.

HEART Q: New evidence is much more likely when an event is true, but the event is rare to begin with. After you see the evidence, does the probability go up, and can it still be low?

LEWIS: Answer. It goes up, and yes, it can still be low. Here is why. Bayes multiplies the prior by how much more likely the evidence is when the event is true than it is overall. That ratio pushes the probability up. But if the event is rare, there are far more cases where it is false, so even a small rate of false signals among them can outnumber the true signals.

## Key takeaways

HEART: First, a few memory hooks.

LEWIS: The three middles: mean is the average, median is the middle, mode is the most common. When outliers distort the picture, reach for the median.

HEART: For outliers in an average: trimming cuts the extremes out, winsorizing caps them.

LEWIS: For quantiles: quartiles, quintiles, deciles and percentiles slice the data into four, five, ten and a hundred pieces. The third quartile is the seventy fifth percentile.

HEART: For the spread, think of a ladder. Range is the crude first step. MAD is the average distance from the average. Variance squares the distances and speaks in squared units. Standard deviation brings it back to the data's own units. The coefficient of variation is risk per unit of return, where lower is better. And downside deviation counts only the bad side, but still divides by the full sample size minus one.

LEWIS: Why minus one for a sample: samples hug their own mean, so the raw spread understates the truth.

HEART: For skew: the mean chases the tail. Start at the tail and say them in alphabetical order: mean, median, mode.

LEWIS: For kurtosis: lepto leaps, a tall peak with fat tails. Platy is a plateau, flat with thin tails. Meso is the middle, like the normal curve. Normal kurtosis is three, and excess kurtosis subtracts the three.

HEART: Two warning lights for risk: negative skew and fat tails.

LEWIS: For moving together: covariance gives direction only, correlation gives direction and strength from minus one to plus one. Correlation only sees straight lines, so check the scatter plot, and it never proves cause.

HEART: For probability: expected value is a probability weighted average. On a tree, multiply along the branches, add across the endpoints. And for Bayes: the branch you care about, divided by every branch that leads to what you saw. Never ignore the base rate.

LEWIS: Now, how you would say the big ideas to a senior leader.

HEART: On averages: when results are lopsided, quote the median, because one extreme value can drag the average a long way. The jargon is central tendency, outliers and skewness.

LEWIS: On risk: standard deviation is how far a typical result lands from the average. If two options promise different returns, compare the risk you take per unit of return. The jargon is standard deviation and the coefficient of variation.

HEART: On tail risk: our models often assume a bell curve, but real returns have fatter tails and a heavier downside, so severe losses come more often than the model says. That is a big reason risk teams run stress tests. The jargon is negative skewness and excess kurtosis.

LEWIS: On relationships: correlation tells us how closely two things move together, not whether one causes the other. The jargon is the correlation coefficient and spurious correlation.

HEART: And on forecasting: start with what we believed, then adjust in proportion to how much more likely the new evidence is under one explanation than in general, and never forget how rare the event was to begin with. The jargon is Bayes' formula, prior probability and the base rate.
