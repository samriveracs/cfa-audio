LEWIS: Reading 5 covers building a portfolio: its expected return, how covariance and correlation drive its risk, and choosing a portfolio with Roy's safety first rule. Readings 6 and 7 cover why prices are modeled as lognormal, simulation with Monte Carlo and the bootstrap, the main ways to draw a sample, and the central limit theorem with the standard error of the sample mean.

## Module 5.1, Probability Models for Portfolio Return and Risk

HEART: This module puts assets together into a portfolio and asks two questions. What return should the whole portfolio earn, and how much will it swing? The first answer is simple. The second is where diversification comes from.

LEWIS: Start with the weights. A portfolio weight is the share of the portfolio's current market value that sits in one asset. If a third of your account's value is in a stock fund, that fund's weight is one third. The weights always add up to one.

HEART: And it is market value today, not what you originally paid. If your stock fund doubles while everything else sits still, its weight grows, even though you never bought another share.

LEWIS: The expected return of a portfolio is the weighted average of the expected returns of the assets in it. Each asset's expected return times its weight, all added up. Think of a fruit smoothie. Its sweetness is just a blend of each fruit's sweetness, in proportion to how much of each fruit went in.

HEART: So for expected return, it makes no difference whether the assets move together or in opposite directions. Only the weights and each asset's own expected return count.

LEWIS: Risk is different. Picture an ice cream stand and an umbrella stand on the same boardwalk. On sunny days the ice cream stand is mobbed and the umbrella stand is quiet. On rainy days it is the reverse. Own both, and your total income is far steadier than either one alone. Neither business got any safer. The combination did, because their bad days do not land on the same days.

HEART: So to measure a portfolio's risk, you need to know how the assets move relative to each other. The measure for that is covariance. Covariance is the expected value of the product of two deviations: how far asset A lands from its own expected return, times how far asset B lands from its own expected return.

LEWIS: Why multiply them?

HEART: Because the sign of the product tells you whether they moved together. If both land above their means, or both below, the product is positive. If one is above while the other is below, the product is negative. Average those products and you learn the tendency. Positive covariance says the two tend to sit on the same side of their averages at the same time. Negative covariance says they tend to sit on opposite sides.

LEWIS: Three properties to know. Pair an asset with itself and you are multiplying each deviation by itself, which is squaring it, so an asset's covariance with itself is just its variance. Covariance has no built in limits. It can run anywhere from minus infinity to plus infinity. And order does not matter. The covariance of A with B is the same number as the covariance of B with A.

HEART: When you estimate covariance from a sample of past returns, you multiply the paired deviations from each sample mean, add them up, and divide by the number of observations minus one. That is the same minus one correction you met with the sample variance in Reading 3, for the same reason.

LEWIS: Now a whole set of assets. Their covariances are organized in a covariance matrix, a grid with the same list of assets running down the side and across the top. Each cell holds the covariance between the asset in its row and the asset in its column.

HEART: The picture I use is the mileage chart in an old road atlas. Every city runs down the side and across the top, and each cell gives the distance between two cities. The chart is symmetric, because Chicago to Denver is the same distance as Denver to Chicago, and a covariance matrix is symmetric for the same reason.

LEWIS: The difference is the diagonal. On the mileage chart, a city's distance to itself is zero. In the covariance matrix, an asset's covariance with itself is its variance. So the diagonal holds the variances, and everything off the diagonal is a covariance, each one appearing twice, once on each side.

HEART: Which raises a counting point. There is one variance per asset. The number of distinct covariances is the number of distinct pairs: take the number of assets, multiply by one fewer than that, and halve it. Three assets give three pairs. Ten assets give forty five. A hundred assets give nearly five thousand.

LEWIS: Now the portfolio variance itself. The general recipe is to go through every cell of the covariance matrix, multiply the covariance in that cell by the weights of its row asset and its column asset, and add up every cell. That is the whole rule.

HEART: For two assets, the grid has four cells. The two diagonal cells give each asset's own variance, scaled by its weight squared. The two off diagonal cells hold the same covariance, A with B and B with A. So the two asset portfolio variance has three pieces: A's own risk, B's own risk, and one interaction piece equal to twice the two weights times their covariance. That two is simply the mirror image cell, and leaving it out is the classic wrong answer.

LEWIS: Why is each weight squared?

HEART: Because variance is measured in squared units. Hold half as much of an asset and its share of the portfolio's swings is half as big, but its own variance piece, which squares those swings, falls to a quarter. With three assets, the same logic gives three own risk pieces and three interaction pieces, one for each pair, and each interaction piece again carries a two.

LEWIS: Now the direction that matters. The interaction pieces are the only place where the way the assets move together enters. For a portfolio that owns both assets, hold the weights and each asset's own variance fixed, and the lower the covariances, the lower the portfolio variance. A smaller positive covariance lowers the risk, and a negative covariance lowers it further, because a negative interaction piece actually subtracts from the total.

HEART: The trouble with covariance, as Reading 3 showed, is its units. Work in whole percentages and it comes out in percent squared, and its size depends on the scale of the data. So we convert it to correlation, the covariance divided by the product of the two standard deviations. Correlation is a pure number between minus one and plus one.

LEWIS: And you can run that backward. Covariance equals the correlation times the two standard deviations. So the portfolio variance can be written with correlation instead. The interaction piece becomes twice the two weights, times the correlation, times the two standard deviations. A correlation matrix is laid out just like a covariance matrix, but on its own it is not enough. You still need each asset's standard deviation to turn its correlations back into covariances. Its diagonal is all ones, because every asset is perfectly correlated with itself.

HEART: Here is the payoff. Suppose two assets have a correlation of exactly plus one. Then the portfolio's standard deviation is exactly the weighted average of the two standard deviations. There is no diversification at all, because the two assets are really one bet.

LEWIS: At any correlation below plus one, the portfolio's standard deviation comes out below that weighted average. The lower the correlation, the bigger the gap. At minus one, with the right weights, you can drive the risk all the way to zero.

HEART: That gap is the diversification benefit, and notice what did not change. The expected return is still the plain weighted average. So mixing assets that are less than perfectly correlated gives you the average return with less than the average risk. A bank or credit union sees the same thing on its balance sheet. Its total interest rate risk is not simply the sum of each book's risk measured on its own, because some exposures partly offset others.

LEWIS: The traps here. Averaging the standard deviations is only right when the correlation is exactly plus one. Putting a correlation where the formula needs a covariance, or the reverse, gives a wrong answer that will be sitting in the choices. And variance is in squared units, so if the question asks for risk as a standard deviation, remember the square root at the end.

HEART: Next, where do the inputs come from? Sometimes from past data, as we just said. Sometimes from a probability model, a set of scenarios, each with a probability and a return for each asset. When it gives the probability of each pair of returns happening together, it is called a joint probability function.

LEWIS: Picture an airline and an oil producer, and a handful of scenarios for the price of oil. When oil spikes, the producer does well and the airline suffers. When oil slumps, it is the reverse.

HEART: The method is the expected value logic from Reading 4. First find each company's expected return, the probability weighted average across the scenarios. Then, in each scenario, multiply the airline's deviation from its expected return by the producer's deviation from its expected return. Weight each product by that scenario's probability, and add them up.

LEWIS: In the spike, the producer lands above its mean and the airline below, so the product is negative. In the slump, the producer is below and the airline above, so the product is negative again. The covariance therefore comes out negative, just as intuition says. And because this is a probability model, not a sample, there is no dividing by the number of observations minus one. The probabilities do the weighting. For the correlation, find each company's standard deviation the same probability weighted way, and divide the covariance by the product of the two.

HEART: The last idea in this module is a rule for choosing among portfolios. Plenty of investors care less about volatility in general than about one specific bad outcome, ending up below some minimum. Shortfall risk is the chance that, over some stated horizon, the portfolio ends up below that minimum, whether the minimum is a return or a dollar value. The minimum is called the threshold level, or the target return.

LEWIS: Picture catching a train that leaves at a fixed time. Route one is the highway. It is faster on average, but traffic makes it wildly unpredictable. Route two is the back roads. A little slower on average, but almost the same every day. If all you care about is not missing the train, the average travel time is not the question. The question is how much cushion each route leaves before the deadline, measured against how unpredictable it is.

HEART: That is the idea behind Roy's safety first criterion. It says to pick the portfolio with the smallest chance of landing below the threshold. When returns are normally distributed, there is a neat shortcut. Compute the safety first ratio for each portfolio: the expected return minus the threshold return, divided by the portfolio's standard deviation. Then choose the portfolio with the largest ratio.

LEWIS: Read the ratio as a count. The top is the cushion, how far the expected return sits above the threshold. Dividing by the standard deviation turns that cushion into a number of standard deviations, so the ratio tells you how many standard deviations below the mean the threshold sits. On a normal curve, the further out the threshold sits, the less of the curve lies beyond it. So a larger safety first ratio means a smaller probability of a shortfall. In fact, the shortfall probability is exactly that tail, the share of a standard normal curve lying more than that many standard deviations below its center.

HEART: The trap is choosing by gut. The highest expected return is tempting, and so is the lowest standard deviation. Neither decides. Only the ratio does. A portfolio with a modest expected return can win if its swings are narrow enough, and a high return portfolio can lose if its swings are wide enough.

LEWIS: Three more details. A goal is often stated in money, like a minimum ending value. Convert it to a threshold return first, by comparing the required amount with what the portfolio is worth today. If the threshold happens to be the risk free rate, the safety first ratio becomes the Sharpe ratio, the excess return per unit of risk. And the criterion itself, minimize the chance of a shortfall, does not need normality. Only the shortcut of ranking by the ratio does, because it relies on the normal curve to turn standard deviations into probabilities.

HEART: The takeaway. Portfolio expected return is a weighted average, but portfolio risk depends on the covariances, and anything below perfect correlation pulls risk below the average. When the goal is to avoid falling below a threshold, choose the portfolio with the highest safety first ratio.

## Module 6.1, Lognormal Distributions and Simulation Techniques

LEWIS: This module answers two questions. What distribution should describe an asset's future price? And when a problem is too tangled for a formula, how do you get an answer anyway?

HEART: Start with the price. A normal distribution is symmetric and runs forever in both directions. That is a problem for a stock price. A price can rise a very long way, but it cannot fall below zero. The worst case is losing everything. So the distribution of future prices should have a floor at zero and a long tail to the right.

LEWIS: The lognormal distribution has exactly that shape. A variable is lognormal if its natural logarithm is normally distributed. Turn that around. Take a normally distributed variable, push it through exponential growth, and what comes out is lognormal.

HEART: Why exponential growth? Because of the continuously compounded return from Reading 1. A future price is today's price grown continuously, at the continuously compounded return, over the period. So the future price equals today's price times the exponential of that return.

LEWIS: And here is the clever part. A continuously compounded return can be any number at all, from hugely negative to hugely positive, which is exactly the range a normal distribution allows. But growing at even a hugely negative continuous rate leaves you with a small positive price, never a negative one. Exponential growth squeezes every possible return into a positive price. That is where the floor at zero comes from. And because large positive returns compound into very large prices, the right tail stretches out.

HEART: Since the shape leans to the right, a lognormal variable's mean sits above its median, the same pattern you saw with positive skew in Reading 3. So keep the roles straight. Continuously compounded returns are modeled as normal. Prices, and price relatives, the ending price over the starting price, are modeled as lognormal.

LEWIS: Why should the return be normal? Because continuously compounded returns add up over time. Split a year into months, and the year's continuously compounded return is the sum of the monthly ones. If each monthly return is normal, their sum is normal. And even if the monthly returns are not normal, the central limit theorem, which is coming in Module 7.1, says a sum of many independent pieces tends toward normal anyway.

HEART: That argument leans on an assumption you will see throughout pricing models: returns are independent and identically distributed. Independent means past returns tell you nothing useful about future returns. A strong month does not make next month any more or less likely to be strong. Identically distributed means the mean and variance stay the same over time. The rules of the game do not drift. That stability is called stationarity, and it gets proper treatment in time series work at Level II.

LEWIS: Those same assumptions tell you how risk grows with the horizon. Under independent and identically distributed returns, the variance of the multi period continuously compounded return grows in proportion to the number of periods. So the standard deviation grows only with the square root of the number of periods. Four times the horizon means twice the standard deviation, not four times.

HEART: And that is the trap. Variance scales with time. Standard deviation scales with the square root of time.

LEWIS: Now simulation. Some valuation problems have no tidy formula. Think of a mortgage backed security. Its cash flows depend on how fast homeowners refinance, and that depends on the whole path interest rates take over many years, not just where rates end up.

HEART: Monte Carlo simulation handles it by brute force. It is named after the casino, because it runs on random numbers the way a roulette wheel does. You choose the risk factors that drive the value, here the path of interest rates and how quickly borrowers prepay. For each one you pick a probability distribution and set its parameters, the mean and variance, and the skewness if the factor is lopsided.

LEWIS: Then the software rolls the dice. It picks a random value for every risk factor, feeds that one imagined world into a pricing model, and records the security's value. That is one trial. Repeat it many thousands of times and you have a whole distribution of possible values. Its average is your estimate of the security's value, and its spread tells you how much that value could vary.

HEART: You have probably met the everyday version. A retirement planning tool tells you there is a high chance your savings will last thirty years. Behind that number, the tool ran thousands of possible market paths and counted how many still had money left at the end.

LEWIS: The investment uses are broad. Projecting a pension plan's assets and its promised benefits years ahead, to see how widely the gap between them could swing. Estimating value at risk, how much a portfolio of assets and liabilities could lose in a bad period. Testing a trading strategy by simulating its profits and losses across many possible markets. Valuing portfolios whose returns are not normal, where the bell curve shortcuts break down. And valuing complex securities, like that mortgage security, or anything with options built in.

HEART: Its big advantage is that it can imagine what history has not shown. Because you pick the distributions, you can stress the model with a rate shock bigger than anything on record.

LEWIS: And the limitations, which are just as testable. It takes real work to build. It can never be better than the distributions you chose and the pricing model you plugged them into. Garbage in, garbage out, with a precise looking answer on top. And because it estimates by repetition rather than by solving an equation, it is a statistical method, not an analytic one. It hands you a number without the insight a formula gives into why the value is what it is.

HEART: The second technique starts from data instead of assumptions. It is called resampling. We rarely get to see a whole population, so we treat the data we have as a sample from it. A fund's track record, for example, is just one of the many histories the market could have dealt it. Resampling draws new samples, again and again, out of that one observed sample, and uses them to learn about the population, such as its mean and variance.

LEWIS: The main version is bootstrap resampling. Picture writing each historical month's return on a marble and dropping all the marbles into a bag. Draw one, note it, and put it back before you draw again. Keep going until you have a new sample the same size as the original. Because each marble goes back in, the same month can be drawn more than once, or not at all. That is called sampling with replacement.

HEART: Do that thousands of times and you have thousands of new samples. Compute the mean of each one, and the standard deviation of all those means is your estimate of the standard error of the sample mean. We come back to that use in Module 7.1, with the standard error.

LEWIS: Once you have the inputs, a bootstrap simulation runs the same engine as Monte Carlo. Only the fuel is different. It is real history rather than assumed distributions, and that history also caps how extreme any input can be. Monte Carlo draws from distributions you assumed. The bootstrap draws from outcomes that actually happened.

HEART: That cuts both ways. The bootstrap needs no assumption about the shape of the distribution, so whatever skew or fat tails are in the history come along automatically. But it can only ever replay what actually happened. Every draw is a month that really occurred, so no simulated month can be worse than the worst month on record. And like every simulation, it is no better than its assumptions, here the assumption that the past is a fair picture of the future.

LEWIS: The trap is telling them apart. If a question describes random draws from distributions an analyst specified, that is Monte Carlo. If it describes drawing past observations with replacement, that is the bootstrap.

HEART: The takeaway. Continuously compounded returns add up and are modeled as normal, which makes prices lognormal, with a floor at zero and a long right tail. Monte Carlo simulates from assumed distributions and can go beyond history. The bootstrap resamples history with replacement and cannot.

## Module 7.1, Sampling Techniques and the Central Limit Theorem

LEWIS: Every input we have used so far, an expected return, a standard deviation, a covariance, has to be estimated, and almost always from a sample rather than the whole population. This module is about how to draw that sample, and how far a sample mean is likely to land from the truth.

HEART: Two terms first. A sample statistic, like a sample mean, is computed from the sample. A population parameter, like the population mean, is the true value for the whole group. The gap between them is sampling error. It is not a mistake anyone made. It is simply the price of looking at part of the population instead of all of it.

LEWIS: Think of tasting soup. One spoonful tells you about the pot, but not perfectly. If the soup is well stirred, one spoonful is a good guide. If the salt has settled to the bottom and you skim from the top, it is a poor guide. How you take the spoonful matters as much as how big it is.

HEART: Sampling methods come in two families. In probability sampling, chance decides who gets in, and every member's chance of being selected is known. In non probability sampling, convenience or the researcher's judgment decides. Take chance out of the selection, and sampling error tends to grow.

LEWIS: The plainest probability method is simple random sampling, where every member of the population has the same chance of being picked. Give every account a number and let a computer draw numbers at random, the way a lottery machine draws balls.

HEART: A practical shortcut is systematic sampling, taking every tenth name on a list, say. That gives an approximately random sample, with one trap. The list must not have a hidden pattern. If a staff roster happens to list a manager every tenth name and you take every tenth name, you sample nothing but managers.

LEWIS: Next is stratified random sampling. First, sort the population into subgroups, called strata, using characteristics that matter for what you are measuring. Then draw randomly within every stratum, taking from each one in proportion to its share of the population, and combine those draws into one sample.

HEART: Picture a lender estimating the average balance across all its loans. Mortgages, auto loans and credit cards have wildly different balances. A simple random sample might, by bad luck, catch too few mortgages and badly understate the average. Stratify by loan type instead, draw from each type in proportion to how many loans of that type there are, and every type is guaranteed its proper place in the sample.

LEWIS: That guarantee is the point. The sample ends up with the same mix of the key characteristics as the population. Simple random sampling offers no such guarantee. You might draw too many from one group, or none at all from another.

HEART: Then cluster sampling, which also divides the population into subgroups, but with a different idea behind them. Each cluster is assumed to be a small version of the whole population. Clusters are often natural groupings, like locations, and are not necessarily built around the characteristic you are studying.

LEWIS: Picture a grocery chain with hundreds of stores that wants to know how its customers feel. It treats each store as a mini version of its whole customer base. In one stage cluster sampling, it randomly picks some stores and surveys every customer in them. In two stage cluster sampling, it randomly picks some stores, then randomly samples customers within each chosen store.

HEART: The appeal is that it is cheaper and faster, because you only visit a handful of stores, which makes it a good choice for a small trial run, called a pilot study. The weakness is that the chosen stores may not be truly typical. If they are not, the estimate tends to miss by more than a simple random sample of customers would. And the two stage version tends to miss by more than the one stage version, because sampling only part of each chosen store adds a second layer of chance.

LEWIS: Here is the trap that matters most in this module. Stratified and cluster sampling both start by splitting the population into groups, so they sound alike. The difference is which groups you sample from. Stratified sampling draws from every group. Cluster sampling draws from only some groups.

HEART: And the groups are built for opposite reasons. Strata gather members that share a characteristic, so each stratum differs from the others. Clusters are each meant to look like the whole population, so a few of them can stand in for the rest.

LEWIS: Now the two non probability methods. Convenience sampling uses whatever data are easiest to get. An analyst who wants to know how investors feel about the economy and simply asks the people in her own office is using convenience sampling. It is quick and cheap, but chance plays little part in who gets picked, so its sampling error tends to be larger. Its fair use is a quick early feel for the data, and then you switch to a more random method when the answer matters.

HEART: Judgmental sampling lets the researcher hand pick each observation using experience and judgment. A veteran analyst might choose the dozen regional banks she believes best represent the whole sector. The danger is that her blind spots become the sample's blind spots, so a biased or careless pick can miss badly. The upside is that real expertise, used without bias, can assemble a more representative set than chance would, or zero in on the observations with the most useful information.

LEWIS: One more consideration applies to every method. The data you sample have to come from one consistent population. If something fundamental changed partway through the period, pooling the data mixes two different populations. Think of online brokers. For years they charged a commission on every stock trade, and then many of them cut those commissions to zero. An average commission per trade pooled across both eras describes neither one.

HEART: Now the most important idea in this module, the central limit theorem. Start with a picture. Roll one die, and every face from one to six is equally likely. That distribution is flat, nothing like a bell. Now roll thirty dice and take their average. Do it over and over. Those averages pile up around the middle, near three and a half, and thin out toward the extremes, and the pile looks very much like a bell curve.

LEWIS: That is the theorem. Take simple random samples of a given size from any population that has a mean and a finite variance. As the sample size gets large, the distribution of the sample mean approaches a normal distribution. The usual rule of thumb is that a sample of thirty or more is large enough.

HEART: Listen carefully to what becomes normal. Not the population. Not the individual observations in your sample. It is the sampling distribution of the sample mean, the distribution you would get if you drew sample after sample of the same size and recorded each sample's mean. Each sample mean is itself a random variable, and those means are what pile up into a bell. The individual stock returns in a sample can stay skewed and fat tailed while their averages settle into a bell shape.

LEWIS: Three properties come with it. First, the sample means are approximately normal once the sample is large enough. Second, the mean of all those sample means equals the population mean, so the sample mean is centered on the truth. Third, the variance of the sample means equals the population variance divided by the sample size. Bigger samples, tighter pile.

HEART: And here is why the theorem earns its fame. Bell curve probabilities are easy to use. So, with a large enough sample, you can put a confidence interval around a sample mean, or test a claim about the population mean, without ever learning the population's shape. Testing claims that way is the next episode.

LEWIS: The standard deviation of that sampling distribution has its own name, the standard error of the sample mean. When you know the population standard deviation, the standard error is that standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample size. In practice you almost never know it, so you use the sample standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample size instead.

HEART: Hold on to the square root. Because of it, precision gets expensive. To cut the standard error in half you need four times as many observations. To cut it to a third, nine times as many.

LEWIS: And the direction is the key idea. More data pulls the typical sample mean in toward the population mean, so the standard error shrinks as the sample grows. A bigger sample shrinks sampling error, but it never removes it unless you measure the entire population.

HEART: Picture a fair where people guess how many jellybeans fill a jar. Any one person's guess can be wildly off. That spread across individual guesses is the standard deviation. But the average of a hundred guesses wobbles far less from one fair to the next. That smaller wobble is the standard error.

LEWIS: Which is the trap. Standard deviation describes how spread out individual observations are. Standard error describes how spread out sample means are. The first does not shrink as you collect more data. The second does.

HEART: Last, two resampling methods for estimating the standard error. That square root formula leans on an unbiased sample and on sample means that really are close to normal. Resampling offers a second route that leans less on the normal shape.

LEWIS: The first is the jackknife. You compute the sample mean over and over, each time leaving out a different single observation. So a sample of forty observations gives forty slightly different means, each from the other thirty nine. The spread of those leave one out means gives you an estimate of the standard error.

HEART: The name comes from the folding pocket knife, a cheap tool you can always reach for. The method dates from an era when computer time was costly. It copes with fairly small samples, and it can strip some bias out of an estimate.

LEWIS: The second is the bootstrap, the marble bag from Module 6.1. Draw many samples the same size as the original, with replacement, and the standard deviation of their means is your estimate of the standard error.

HEART: It asks more of the computer than the jackknife does, and it gives more back. Many resampled samples can measure how uncertain an estimate is more accurately than leaning on the one sample and a formula alone. It is not limited to the mean, so it can put a confidence interval around a correlation or a tail percentile. And it can map out the distribution of a complicated statistic that has no formula at all.

LEWIS: So keep the two apart. The jackknife leaves one observation out at a time, and makes exactly as many new samples as there are observations. The bootstrap draws with replacement, and can make as many new samples as you like.

HEART: The takeaway. Stratified sampling draws from every subgroup, cluster sampling from only some. The central limit theorem makes the sample mean approximately normal for large samples, centered on the population mean, with a standard error equal to the standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample size. The jackknife and the bootstrap estimate that standard error by resampling.

## Recall check

LEWIS Q: Does the correlation between two assets change a portfolio's expected return?

HEART: Answer. No, it changes only the portfolio's risk. Here is why. Expected return is a weighted average of each asset's expected return, and nothing in that average depends on how the assets move together. Correlation enters only through the interaction pieces of the portfolio variance. So changing it changes how much the portfolio swings, not where it is expected to land.

HEART Q: In a covariance matrix, what sits on the diagonal, and why is the grid the same on both sides of it?

LEWIS: Answer. The variances sit on the diagonal, and the grid is symmetric because the covariance of A with B equals the covariance of B with A. Here is why. The diagonal pairs each asset with itself, and multiplying each deviation by itself gives the variance. Off the diagonal, swapping the two assets only swaps the order of the multiplication, which does not change the product. So each covariance appears twice, once on each side.

LEWIS Q: Why does the two asset portfolio variance have a two in front of the covariance piece?

HEART: Answer. Because the pair appears twice in the full grid. Here is why. Portfolio variance adds up the two weights times the covariance for every cell of the covariance matrix. For two assets there is one cell for A with B and another for B with A, and they hold the same number. Combining them gives twice the two weights times the covariance, and dropping the two is a classic wrong answer.

HEART Q: Two assets have a correlation of plus one. How does the portfolio's standard deviation compare with the weighted average of their standard deviations, and what happens as the correlation falls?

LEWIS: Answer. At plus one it equals the weighted average, and as the correlation falls it drops below it. Here is why. With perfect correlation the two assets always move together in proportion, so combining them is really one bet and nothing offsets. Below plus one, some of one asset's swings are cancelled by the other's, and the interaction piece of the variance shrinks. The lower the correlation, the more cancelling, and the bigger the diversification benefit.

LEWIS Q: Under Roy's safety first criterion, with normally distributed returns, which portfolio do you choose, and why not simply the one with the highest expected return?

HEART: Answer. The one with the largest safety first ratio. Here is why. The ratio measures the cushion between the expected return and the threshold in units of standard deviation. Under a normal distribution, more standard deviations of cushion leave less of the curve below the threshold, so the chance of a shortfall is lower. A high expected return with wide swings can leave less cushion in those units than a modest return with narrow swings.

HEART Q: Which is modeled as lognormal, an asset's continuously compounded return or its price?

LEWIS: Answer. The price. Here is why. Continuously compounded returns add up over time, so a sum of normal period returns is normal, and a sum of many independent ones is approximately normal. The future price is today's price times the exponential of that return, and exponential growth of a normal variable produces a lognormal one. That gives prices a floor at zero and a long right tail, which is how real prices behave.

LEWIS Q: What do independent and identically distributed mean for a series of returns?

HEART: Answer. Independent means past returns do not help predict future ones, and identically distributed means the mean and variance stay the same over time. Here is why. Independence lets you treat each period's return as a fresh draw, so the period returns add up into a sum whose variance is simply the sum of the period variances. Identical distribution means every period draws from the same distribution, so that variance grows in step with the number of periods. Together they justify modeling multi period returns as normal and prices as lognormal.

HEART Q: Which simulation method can produce a monthly loss worse than any month in the historical record, Monte Carlo or the bootstrap?

LEWIS: Answer. Monte Carlo. Here is why. Monte Carlo draws from distributions the analyst specifies, so its inputs are not limited to what has happened, and a wide enough distribution can produce a month worse than anything on record. The bootstrap draws only from observed history, with replacement, so every simulated month is one that actually happened. That is the bootstrap's strength and its weakness at once.

LEWIS Q: An analyst sorts companies by sector, then draws a random sample from every sector in proportion to its size. Which method is this, and how would cluster sampling differ?

HEART: Answer. Stratified random sampling, and cluster sampling would randomly pick only some of the groups. Here is why. Stratified sampling guarantees every subgroup shows up in its proper share, so the sample mirrors the population's mix. Cluster sampling assumes each group is a small copy of the population, so it samples a few groups and skips the rest. That saves cost and time, but when the chosen clusters do not really mirror the population, sampling error rises.

HEART Q: According to the central limit theorem, what becomes approximately normal as the sample size grows?

LEWIS: Answer. The distribution of the sample mean, not the population or the individual observations. Here is why. Each sample mean averages many independent draws, and averaging lets extremes in one direction offset extremes in the other. Repeat the sampling many times, and the means pile up around the population mean in a bell shape, as long as the population variance is finite and the sample is large, usually thirty or more. The population itself keeps whatever shape it had.

LEWIS Q: What happens to the standard error of the sample mean if you quadruple the sample size, and why does the standard deviation of the data not shrink the same way?

HEART: Answer. The standard error halves, while the standard deviation of the data stays about the same. Here is why. The sample size sits under a square root in the standard error, so four times the data only halves it. The standard deviation describes how spread out individual observations are, and collecting more of them does not make each one less variable. The standard error describes how much the sample mean wobbles, and averaging more observations steadies it.

HEART Q: The jackknife and the bootstrap both estimate a standard error by resampling. How does each one build its new samples?

LEWIS: Answer. The jackknife leaves out one observation at a time, and the bootstrap draws with replacement. Here is why. The jackknife recomputes the statistic once for each observation left out, so it makes exactly as many new samples as there are observations, which keeps it simple and cheap. The bootstrap draws full size samples with replacement as many times as you like. That takes more computing power, but it can handle statistics beyond the mean, including complex ones with no formula at all.

## Key takeaways

HEART: First, a few memory hooks.

LEWIS: For portfolios: returns average, risks do not. Expected return is a plain weighted average. Risk depends on how the assets move together.

HEART: For the covariance matrix, think of the mileage chart: every pair appears twice, mirrored across a diagonal of variances. That mirror image is the two in the two asset formula. Twice for the pair.

LEWIS: For diversification: at plus one, the portfolio's standard deviation is the weighted average. Below plus one, it falls below the average. The lower the correlation, the bigger the benefit, and the expected return does not budge.

HEART: For Roy's safety first: cushion over wobble. Expected return minus the threshold, divided by the standard deviation. With normal returns, the biggest ratio has the smallest chance of a shortfall. And when the threshold is the risk free rate, it is the Sharpe ratio.

LEWIS: For lognormal: the log is normal. Continuously compounded returns are normal and they add. Prices are lognormal, with a floor at zero and a long right tail.

HEART: For time: variance grows with time, standard deviation with the square root of time.

LEWIS: For simulation: Monte Carlo rolls the dice on your assumptions. The bootstrap puts each marble back in the bag, so it never leaves history. Both are only as good as what you feed them.

HEART: For sampling: stratified samples every stratum, cluster picks only some clusters. A stratum groups members that are alike. A cluster is a mini version of the whole population.

LEWIS: For the central limit theorem: thirty or more, and the means go normal, not the data. Centered on the population mean, with a variance equal to the population variance divided by the sample size.

HEART: For the standard error: deviation is for the data, error is for the averages. Quadruple the sample to halve the error.

LEWIS: For resampling: the jackknife leaves one out. The bootstrap puts each one back.

HEART: Now, how you would say the big ideas to a senior leader.

LEWIS: On diversification: combining assets that do not move in lockstep gives us the average return with less than the average risk. The jargon is covariance, correlation and portfolio variance.

HEART: On a minimum return: if what matters most is not falling below a floor, pick the option with the most cushion above that floor for each unit of volatility, not the one with the highest return. The jargon is shortfall risk, Roy's safety first criterion and the safety first ratio.

LEWIS: On modeling prices: a price cannot go below zero but can climb a long way, so we model returns with a bell curve and prices with a distribution that has a floor at zero and a long tail of big gains. The jargon is continuously compounded returns and the lognormal distribution.

HEART: On simulation: when a problem is too tangled for a formula, we run thousands of possible futures and study the range of outcomes, and the answer is only as good as the assumptions. The jargon is Monte Carlo simulation, or bootstrap resampling when we draw from history.

LEWIS: On sampling: how we choose a sample matters as much as how big it is, and a sample that misses a key group can mislead us at any size. The jargon is probability sampling, stratified and cluster sampling, and sampling error.

HEART: And on precision: an average from a bigger sample is steadier, but four times the data only halves the uncertainty in the average. The jargon is the central limit theorem and the standard error of the sample mean.
