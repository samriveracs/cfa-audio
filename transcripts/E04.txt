LEWIS: Reading 8 covers how a hypothesis test works, the two kinds of error, power and the p value, then the specific tests for means and variances and when a nonparametric test fits better. Reading 9 covers testing whether a correlation is real, the rank based Spearman version of that test, and testing whether two characteristics are independent using a table of counts.

## Module 8.1, Hypothesis Testing Basics

HEART: Every test in this episode answers one question. Is the pattern in my sample real, or could plain luck have produced it? A hypothesis test is the formal way to decide. A hypothesis is a statement about a population parameter, such as a population mean or a population variance. You almost never get to see the whole population, so you look at a sample and ask whether it is consistent with the statement.

LEWIS: Picture a bank that promises its mortgage applications will close in thirty days on average. Nobody can check every loan the bank will ever make. But you can pull a sample of recent loans, see how long each one took, and ask a simple question. If the true average really were thirty days, would a sample like this one be believable, or would it be a real surprise?

HEART: The process runs in seven steps. State the hypotheses. Pick the test statistic. Set the significance level. State the decision rule. Collect the sample and calculate the statistic. Decide whether to reject the hypothesis. And finally, make the real world decision that the result points to.

LEWIS: That last step is easy to skip, and it matters. A result can pass the statistical test and still be too small to act on once costs are counted. The statistics tell you whether an effect is probably real. You still have to judge whether it is big enough to matter.

HEART: Now the two hypotheses. The null hypothesis is the statement you actually test, and it is the one the researcher is hoping to reject. Think of a courtroom. The defendant is presumed innocent, and that presumption is the null. The prosecution's case is the alternative hypothesis, the conclusion you reach only if the evidence is strong enough to throw out the null.

LEWIS: Here is the rule the exam leans on. The null always contains the equal sign. It says equal to, less than or equal to, or greater than or equal to. The alternative is what the researcher really wants to show, and it gets the strict version: not equal to, less than, or greater than.

HEART: So if a fund manager wants to show that her average excess return is positive, the alternative says it is greater than zero, and the null says it is less than or equal to zero. What you hope to prove goes in the alternative. What you hope to knock down, with the equal sign attached, goes in the null.

LEWIS: Between them, the two hypotheses cover every possible value of the parameter, and no value falls in both. In the jargon, they are mutually exclusive and exhaustive. That is why rejecting one leaves you with the other.

HEART: The alternative also decides the shape of the test. A two tailed test asks whether the parameter differs in either direction. Is the true closing time anything other than thirty days, faster or slower? The null says equal to, the alternative says not equal to, and evidence against the null can show up in either tail of the distribution.

LEWIS: A one tailed test looks in one direction only. Is the true closing time longer than thirty days? Now the null says less than or equal to thirty, the alternative says greater than thirty, and only results in the upper tail count against the null. You choose the shape before you set the decision rule, because it changes where the cutoff sits.

HEART: Next, the test statistic. The basic recipe, and the one every test of a mean follows, has a simple shape. Take your sample statistic, subtract the value the null claims, and divide by the standard error of the sample statistic. In plain words, it counts how many standard errors your sample result sits away from what the null predicts.

LEWIS: Think of it as signal over noise. The gap between your sample and the claim is the signal. The standard error is the noise, how much a sample result would wobble from one sample to the next just by chance. A big gap measured against a small wobble is hard to blame on luck.

HEART: For a sample mean, the standard error is the standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample size. If you know the population standard deviation, you use it. Usually you do not, so you use the sample standard deviation in its place. Either way, notice the direction. A bigger sample means a smaller standard error, so the same gap becomes more convincing.

LEWIS: Which matches common sense. Average the closing times of five loans, and one stuck file can drag the average a long way. Average five hundred, and one odd file barely moves it.

HEART: When the null is true, the test statistic follows a known distribution, and which one depends on the test. This reading uses four: the z distribution, which is the standard normal, the t distribution, the chi square distribution and the F distribution. Each one supplies its own critical values.

LEWIS: The critical value is the cutoff. Every test compares two numbers, the test statistic you calculated from the sample and the critical value from the right distribution. If your statistic lands beyond the critical value, in what is called the rejection region, you reject the null. If it lands inside, you fail to reject.

HEART: Where the cutoff sits depends on the significance level, written as alpha. The significance level is the probability of rejecting the null when the null is actually true. You set it before you look at the data, typically at ten, five or one percent, and it is the total size of the rejection region.

LEWIS: In a two tailed test, that area is split evenly, half in each tail. In a one tailed test, all of it sits in a single tail. That is why the same significance level gives different cutoffs for the two shapes.

HEART: For the standard normal distribution, four cutoffs are worth knowing cold. One point six five is the cutoff for a one tailed test at five percent, and also for a two tailed test at ten percent, because both put five percent in a single tail. One point nine six is the cutoff for a two tailed test at five percent. Two point three three is a one tailed test at one percent. And two point five eight is a two tailed test at one percent.

LEWIS: Now the language of the decision, which the exam polices. You either reject the null or fail to reject it. You never accept the null. A jury that acquits does not declare the defendant innocent. It says the evidence was not strong enough to convict. Failing to reject works the same way. It only means the sample was not surprising enough.

HEART: And because you are deciding from a sample, you can be wrong in two ways. A Type one error is rejecting a null that is actually true. A Type two error is failing to reject a null that is actually false. In the courtroom, a Type one error convicts an innocent person, and a Type two error lets a guilty one walk free.

LEWIS: A kitchen smoke alarm makes the same point. A Type one error is the alarm shrieking because you burned the toast. There was no fire, but the alarm rejected the idea that all is well. A Type two error is the alarm staying silent while something really is burning.

HEART: The significance level is the probability of a Type one error. Set it at five percent, and when the null is true you will wrongly reject it about one time in twenty, purely by chance.

LEWIS: Its partner is power. The power of a test is the probability of correctly rejecting a null that is false. It equals one minus the probability of a Type two error. Power is the alarm's ability to catch a real fire. If two different test statistics could check the same claim, you pick the one with more power, because at the same significance level it catches more false nulls.

HEART: Here is the tradeoff. Hold the sample size fixed and make the significance level stricter. You get fewer false alarms, so Type one errors become rarer. But you have made the null harder to reject, so you also miss more real effects. The chance of a Type two error rises and power falls.

LEWIS: It runs the other way too. With a fixed sample, the only way to buy more power is to accept a higher chance of a Type one error. Turn up the sensitivity on the alarm and it catches more real fires, but it also goes off at more toast.

HEART: So how do you cut both errors at once? A bigger sample. More data shrinks the standard error, which sharpens the test in both directions. Leave the significance level where it is, and more data is the one lever that still lowers the Type two risk and lifts power. It is a better sensor, not just a different setting on the same one.

LEWIS: The same tension shows up whenever a bank validates a risk model. A test strict enough to rarely flag a sound model will more often wave a weak one through.

HEART: Last idea in this module, the p value. The p value is the smallest significance level at which you could reject the null. Another way to hear it: assuming the null is true, how likely is a test statistic at least as extreme as the one you got? A small p value means your sample would be a real surprise if the null were true.

LEWIS: That gives you a decision rule without looking up any cutoff. Reject the null when the p value is below the significance level. A p value of two percent rejects at five percent, but not at one percent.

HEART: And the trap. The p value is not the probability that the null is true. It is calculated by assuming the null is true. It tells you how surprising your data would be in that world, not how likely that world is.

LEWIS: The takeaway. The null holds the equal sign and is what you try to reject. The test statistic counts standard errors between your sample and the claim. The significance level is the Type one error rate, power is one minus the Type two error rate, and only a bigger sample cuts both errors at once. Reject when the p value is below the significance level.

## Module 8.2, Types of Hypothesis Tests

HEART: The first module built the machine. This one is the set of tests you plug into it, and the map is short. A single mean uses a t test. Two means use a t test, in one of two forms depending on whether the samples are independent or linked. A single variance uses a chi square test. Two variances use an F test. Each comes in a two tailed form and in one tailed forms, and in every one of them the null keeps the equal sign.

LEWIS: Start with a single population mean. Go back to the bank promising thirty day closings. You sample recent loans, find their average closing time, and compare it with thirty. The t statistic is the gap between the sample mean and thirty, measured in standard errors, using the sample standard deviation because you do not know the population's. Its degrees of freedom are the sample size minus one.

HEART: Why t rather than the normal distribution? Because the standard error is itself estimated from the sample, which adds a layer of uncertainty. The t distribution allows for that with fatter tails, so its critical values sit further out than the z values. A small sample needs a bigger statistic before you can reject.

LEWIS: But the gap closes as the sample grows. With a few hundred observations, the t and z critical values are almost identical, so a z test is acceptable for a large sample. The trap runs the other way. Use the z cutoff on a small sample and you reject too easily, because the true cutoff is further out than you think.

HEART: Next, comparing two population means. Here the first question is always the same. Are the two samples independent, or are they linked?

LEWIS: Start with independent samples. Independent means the two groups have nothing to do with each other. Say you compare the average time to approve a small business loan at a lender in Ohio with the average at an unrelated lender in Oregon. Different loans, different staff, different customers. Neither sample tells you anything about the other.

HEART: That calls for the difference in means test. It is a t test on the gap between the two sample means. It assumes both populations are normally distributed and the samples are independent. And in the version you need, the two population variances are unknown but assumed to be equal.

LEWIS: Because the variances are assumed equal, you combine the two sample variances into one better estimate, called the pooled variance. It is a weighted average of the two sample variances, with each sample weighted by its size minus one. The degrees of freedom are the two sample sizes added together, minus two, one lost for each sample's mean.

HEART: Picture what drives the answer. Suppose the two lenders' average approval times differ by a day, while individual loans at each lender swing by weeks. A one day gap is easy to blame on luck, so the statistic stays small and you fail to reject equal means. If the averages sit weeks apart while individual loans barely vary, luck is a poor explanation, the statistic is large, and you reject.

LEWIS: Now the linked case, the paired comparisons test, also called the test of mean differences. Use it when the samples are dependent, meaning both sets of observations are pushed around by some common factor.

HEART: Here is a picture for anyone who runs. Twenty runners each run the same course twice, once in their old shoes and once in a new pair. The fast runners will be fast in either pair and the slow ones slow, so the two sets of times are tied together, runner by runner.

LEWIS: So you do not compare the average old shoe time with the average new shoe time. You take each runner's own difference, new shoe time minus old shoe time, and now you have a single sample of differences. The test asks whether the mean of those differences equals zero, or some other hypothesized value.

HEART: Why bother pairing? Because the gaps between runners are huge compared with the effect of a shoe. In a difference in means test, all that runner to runner variation counts as noise, and it can bury a real shoe effect. Pairing compares each runner with themselves, so the shared factor drops out.

LEWIS: Markets hand you this situation constantly. Two stock funds measured over the same months are both pushed around by the overall market. When a set of companies is measured before and after some event, each company carries its own traits into both measurements. Whenever the observations are matched by company, by time period, or by the same subject before and after, treat the samples as dependent and pair them.

HEART: The paired statistic is the average difference minus the value the null claims, usually zero, measured in standard errors of that average difference. Degrees of freedom are the number of pairs minus one. And like the other t tests, it needs the data to be normally distributed.

LEWIS: Notice that once you pair the data, it really is a one sample t test on the differences. If you understand the single mean test, you already understand this one.

HEART: The trap is choosing between the two. Listen for a link. Same companies, same months, or before and after on the same subjects means paired. Two unrelated groups means difference in means. The choice also sets the degrees of freedom. Paired data use the number of pairs minus one, and two independent samples use the two sample sizes added together, minus two.

LEWIS: Now tests about variance, which in finance usually means tests about risk. Start with a single population variance. The test uses the chi square distribution, so lock in the first contrast. Means use t. A single variance uses chi square.

HEART: Picture a credit union's treasury team. For years, the day to day swings in deposit outflows have had a known variance, and the liquidity buffer is sized for it. The team wants to know whether those swings have grown. That is a one tailed test. The null says the variance is less than or equal to the old level, and the alternative says it is greater.

LEWIS: The chi square statistic is the sample variance divided by the hypothesized variance, scaled up by the degrees of freedom, which are the sample size minus one. A helpful way to hear it: if the sample variance came in exactly at the hypothesized level, the statistic would equal its degrees of freedom, which is the average value of that chi square distribution. The further the sample variance strays above the claim, the bigger the statistic.

HEART: One practical point. Claims about risk are often stated as a standard deviation. The test is about variance, so square the claimed standard deviation first.

LEWIS: The chi square distribution has a shape you need to know. It can never be negative, so it is bounded below by zero, and it is skewed to the right, with a long tail toward the high side. As the degrees of freedom rise, it becomes more symmetric and looks more and more like a normal distribution.

HEART: Because it is lopsided, a two tailed test needs two different critical values. There is no single plus or minus number the way there is with z. You look up a lower cutoff that leaves half the significance level in the left tail, and a separate upper cutoff that leaves the other half in the right tail. Reject if the statistic falls below the lower one or above the upper one.

LEWIS: And one caution. The chi square test for a variance is built for a normally distributed population, and it leans heavily on that assumption. With skewed or fat tailed data, its conclusions get shaky.

HEART: To compare two population variances, you use the F test. Go back to the two unrelated lenders in Ohio and Oregon. This time the question is not which one approves loans faster on average, but which one is less predictable, with approval times that are more spread out. Take a sample of loans from each lender and compare the variances of their approval times.

LEWIS: The F statistic is simply one sample variance divided by the other. Its degrees of freedom come in a pair: the size of the sample on top minus one, and the size of the sample on the bottom minus one. The test assumes both populations are normal and the two samples are independent.

HEART: If the two sample variances were identical, the ratio would be exactly one. So the question becomes whether the ratio is far enough from one to rule out chance.

LEWIS: The F distribution looks a lot like chi square. It is a ratio of two positive quantities, so it is bounded below by zero and skewed to the right, and its exact shape depends on both degrees of freedom.

HEART: Then comes the convention that saves you work. Always put the larger sample variance on top. That forces the ratio to be at least one, so you only ever need the upper critical value. Any lower tail cutoff is just the reciprocal of an upper tail cutoff with the two degrees of freedom swapped, so nothing is lost by checking only the top.

LEWIS: And here is the trap that convention creates. For a two tailed test at five percent, you use the cutoff that leaves two and a half percent in the upper tail, not five percent. The other half of the significance level still belongs to the lower tail. You simply arranged the ratio so you never have to visit it.

HEART: So the variance tests in one breath. Chi square for one variance, compared with a hypothesized value. F for two variances, as a ratio with the larger on top.

LEWIS: Everything so far has been a parametric test. A parametric test is about a parameter of the population, like a mean or a variance, and it relies on assumptions about the population's distribution. Even the z test needs either a normal population or a sample large enough for the central limit theorem to make the sample mean approximately normal. The t, chi square and F tests are all parametric.

HEART: A nonparametric test either is not about a particular parameter at all, or makes few assumptions about the population. Think of a parametric test as a precision instrument that needs the right conditions, and a nonparametric test as a sturdier tool that still works when conditions are rough.

LEWIS: Three situations call for one. First, the assumptions behind the parametric test fail. The classic case is a small sample from a population that is clearly not normal. The sample is too small for the central limit theorem to rescue you, and the population is not normal, so there is nothing left to justify a t test or a z test.

HEART: Second, the data are ranks rather than measured values. If all you know is that one fund placed first, another second and another third, you have an ordering, which is called an ordinal scale. The ranks do not tell you how far apart the funds were, so tests built on means and variances of the values do not fit.

LEWIS: Third, the question is not about a parameter at all. Is this variable normally distributed? Is this sequence random? Those are questions about the shape or behavior of the data, not about a mean or a variance.

HEART: The runs test is the example to know. Picture a record of whether a stock rose or fell each day, and look at the streaks. Each unbroken streak of up days or of down days is a run. If the moves are random, the number of runs should be close to what chance produces. Very few runs, meaning long streaks, or a great many runs, meaning the moves flip back and forth almost every day, both suggest the sequence is not random. The test works out how likely the observed pattern would be if the sequence really were random.

LEWIS: One balancing point. Nonparametric is not automatically better. When the parametric assumptions really do hold, the parametric test is generally more powerful, so it is more likely to catch a false null.

HEART: The takeaway. One mean uses t, with z acceptable for a large sample. Two means use t, as a difference in means test for independent samples or a paired comparisons test for dependent ones. One variance uses chi square, and two variances use F with the larger on top. Reach for a nonparametric test when the assumptions fail, the data are ranks, or the question is not about a parameter.

## Module 9.1, Tests for Independence

LEWIS: This module asks whether two variables are related at all. It gives you three tools: a test of whether the population correlation is zero, a rank based version of that test, and a test of independence for data sorted into categories.

HEART: Start with correlation. The correlation coefficient measures the strength of the linear relationship between two variables, from minus one to plus one, and a correlation of zero means no linear relationship. The ordinary version is called the Pearson correlation.

LEWIS: The problem is that a sample correlation is almost never exactly zero, even when the true correlation is. Take monthly returns on gold and on an index of bank stocks over a few years. The sample correlation comes out slightly positive. Is there a real relationship in the population, or is that just sampling noise?

HEART: So the null says the population correlation equals zero, and the alternative says it does not. The test statistic follows a t distribution with degrees of freedom equal to the sample size minus two, and the test assumes both variables are normally distributed.

LEWIS: Look at what drives the statistic. It rises as the sample correlation moves further from zero, which is no surprise. But it also rises with the sample size. With thirty observations, a correlation of one tenth is nowhere near significant. With a thousand observations, that same weak correlation clears the five percent bar easily.

HEART: Which is the trap. Statistically significant does not mean strong. A significant result says the true correlation is probably not zero. It says nothing about whether the relationship is big enough to be useful.

LEWIS: And watch the degrees of freedom. The single mean test uses the sample size minus one. The correlation test uses the sample size minus two. That difference is a favorite exam point.

HEART: One more limit. The Pearson correlation only measures how well the points fit a straight line. If one variable keeps rising with the other but along a curve, Pearson understates how tight the link is, and other measures, including the rank correlation coming next, capture it better.

LEWIS: Which brings us to the second tool, the Spearman rank correlation. It is a nonparametric test of whether two sets of ranks are correlated. Instead of using the raw values, you rank each variable from top to bottom, then measure how closely the two rankings agree.

HEART: Picture a list of stock mutual funds. Rank them by fees, cheapest first. Then rank the same funds by their returns, best first. If the cheapest funds also tend to rank near the top on returns, the rank correlation is positive. Spearman answers the question using nothing but the two orderings.

LEWIS: When do you use it? When the data are ranks to begin with, when the variables are clearly not normal, or when a few outliers would distort an ordinary correlation. Ranking tames an outlier, because the most extreme value is simply ranked first or last, however far out it sits.

HEART: Ties share a rank. If three runners cross the line together in what would have been fourth, fifth and sixth place, each gets the average of those three places, which is fifth.

LEWIS: The calculation rests on the gap between each item's two ranks. Square each gap, add them up, scale the total, and subtract it from one. You do not need the scaling to understand it. If the two rankings match exactly, every gap is zero and the rank correlation is plus one. If one ranking is the exact reverse of the other, it comes out at minus one.

HEART: To test whether a Spearman correlation is significant, you use the same t statistic as the ordinary correlation test. When the sample is larger than thirty, that statistic follows a t distribution with the sample size minus two degrees of freedom.

LEWIS: The third tool handles categories. A contingency table, also called a two way table, counts how many items in a sample share each combination of two characteristics. One characteristic runs down the rows, the other across the columns, and each cell holds a count.

HEART: Picture a bank sorting a sample of its customers two ways. Rows for age group: under thirty five, thirty five to sixty, and over sixty. Columns for how they mostly bank, on their phone or in a branch. Each cell counts the customers with that age and that habit. The question is whether age group and banking habit are independent.

LEWIS: The null says the two characteristics are independent. The alternative says they are related. The key move is to work out what each cell would hold if the null were true.

HEART: Here is the intuition. If the two traits are unrelated, every row should split across the columns in the same proportions as the whole sample. If four in ten of all the customers bank mostly on their phone, then under independence, four in ten of the young customers should too, and four in ten of the middle group, and four in ten of the oldest.

LEWIS: That is exactly what the expected count does. The expected count for a cell is its row total times its column total, divided by the grand total. In plain words, the size of the row times the column's share of the whole sample.

HEART: Then you compare. For every cell, take the gap between the observed count and the expected count, square it, and divide by the expected count. Add those up across every cell, and the sum is the chi square statistic.

LEWIS: Dividing by the expected count keeps each gap in proportion. Missing by twenty customers is a big miss in a cell that should hold thirty, and a small one in a cell that should hold three hundred.

HEART: The degrees of freedom are the number of rows minus one, times the number of columns minus one. Here is why. Once the row totals and the column totals are fixed, only that many cells are free. In the banking table, fill in the phone count for two of the age groups, and every other cell is forced by the totals.

LEWIS: And the decision. A big statistic means the observed counts stray far from what independence predicts, so this test rejects only in the upper tail. If the statistic is above the critical chi square value, you reject independence and conclude that the two characteristics are related.

HEART: Two traps. The degrees of freedom are rows minus one times columns minus one, not the number of cells minus one. And the statistic works on counts. If a table gives you percentages, turn them back into counts first.

LEWIS: Notice that the chi square distribution has now done two different jobs. Earlier it tested a single variance, and here it tests independence in a table of counts. Same distribution, different question.

HEART: The takeaway. To test whether a correlation is real, use a t statistic with the sample size minus two degrees of freedom, and remember that significance grows with the sample, so significant is not the same as strong. Spearman does the same job with ranks. And a contingency table tests independence by comparing observed counts with the counts independence predicts, using chi square in the upper tail.

## Recall check

LEWIS Q: A researcher wants to show that a strategy's average return is above zero. What goes in the null hypothesis, and what goes in the alternative?

HEART: Answer. The null says the average return is less than or equal to zero, and the alternative says it is greater than zero. Here is why. The null is the statement you are trying to knock down, and it always holds the equal sign. What the researcher hopes to show goes in the alternative, which you can only conclude when the evidence rejects the null. Because only high results count against this null, it is a one tailed test in the upper tail.

HEART Q: A test fails to reject the null. Can you say the null has been shown to be true?

LEWIS: Answer. No, you can only say you failed to reject it. Here is why. Failing to reject means the sample was not surprising enough under the null. That can happen because the null is true, or because the sample was too small to detect a real effect. Like a jury's not guilty verdict, it says the evidence fell short, not that innocence was proven.

LEWIS Q: You make the significance level stricter and keep the same sample size. What happens to the chance of a Type two error and to the power of the test?

HEART: Answer. The chance of a Type two error rises and power falls. Here is why. A stricter significance level pushes the cutoff further out, so the null is harder to reject. That cuts false alarms, but it also lets more false nulls slip through. With a fixed sample you trade one error for the other, and only a larger sample can reduce both.

HEART Q: A colleague says a p value of three percent means there is a three percent chance the null hypothesis is true. What is wrong with that?

LEWIS: Answer. The p value is calculated by assuming the null is true, so it cannot be the probability that the null is true. Here is why. The p value is the probability of a test statistic at least as extreme as yours if the null holds, so it measures how surprising your data would be in that world. Its proper use is to compare it with the significance level and reject when it is smaller. A p value of three percent rejects at five percent, but not at one percent.

LEWIS Q: Thirty runners each run the same course in old shoes and then in new ones. Which test compares the average times, and why not the difference in means test?

HEART: Answer. The paired comparisons test, also called the test of mean differences. Here is why. Both sets of times come from the same runners, so the samples are dependent, and a fast runner is fast in either pair of shoes. The difference in means test assumes independent samples and would treat all that runner to runner variation as noise. Pairing takes each runner's own difference, so the shared factor drops out, and the test runs on the mean difference with the number of pairs minus one degrees of freedom.

HEART Q: When you compare the means of two independent samples, what do you assume about their population variances, and what do you do with the sample variances?

LEWIS: Answer. You assume the population variances are unknown but equal, and you pool the two sample variances. Here is why. If both populations share one variance, each sample gives an estimate of it, and combining them gives a better estimate than either alone. The pooled variance weights each sample variance by its size minus one, and the degrees of freedom are the two sample sizes added together, minus two. The test also assumes both populations are normal.

LEWIS Q: Which distribution tests a single population variance, and why does a two tailed version need two different critical values?

HEART: Answer. The chi square distribution, and it needs two cutoffs because it is not symmetric. Here is why. Chi square is bounded below by zero and skewed to the right, so its lower and upper tails have different shapes. A single plus or minus cutoff only works for a symmetric distribution like z or t. So you look up a separate lower cutoff and upper cutoff, each leaving half the significance level in its own tail.

HEART Q: In an F test of two variances, why do you put the larger sample variance on top, and what does that mean for a two tailed test at five percent?

LEWIS: Answer. It means you only need the upper critical value, and for a two tailed test at five percent you use the one that leaves two and a half percent in the upper tail. Here is why. With the larger variance on top, the ratio is at least one, so it can only land in the upper rejection region. But the test is still two tailed, so the significance level is split between the two tails, and the upper tail holds only half of it.

LEWIS Q: All you have is a ranking of funds from first to last. Should you use a parametric or a nonparametric test, and why?

HEART: Answer. A nonparametric test, such as the Spearman rank correlation if you are comparing two rankings. Here is why. Ranks are an ordinal scale. They give you the order but not how far apart the items are, so you cannot build means and variances of the underlying values. Parametric tests rest on parameters and distribution assumptions that ranks cannot support, while rank based tests are built for orderings.

HEART Q: An analyst wants to know whether a sequence of daily up and down moves is random. Why is that a job for a nonparametric test?

LEWIS: Answer. Because the question is not about a parameter. Here is why. Randomness is a property of the sequence, not a mean or a variance of a population. A runs test counts the streaks of up days and down days and asks whether there are about as many as chance would produce. Too few runs or too many both suggest the moves are not random.

LEWIS Q: With a very large sample, a correlation of about one tenth turns out to be highly significant. Does that mean the two variables are strongly related?

HEART: Answer. No, it means the true correlation is probably not zero, but the relationship is still weak. Here is why. The t statistic for a correlation rises with the sample size as well as with the correlation itself. With enough observations, even a weak correlation clears the cutoff. Significance tells you an effect is probably real, not that it is large.

HEART Q: In a chi square test of independence, why are the degrees of freedom the rows minus one times the columns minus one, and in which tail do you reject?

LEWIS: Answer. Because once the totals are fixed, only that many cells are free, and you reject only in the upper tail. Here is why. The expected counts come from the row and column totals, so after you fill in all but one row and all but one column, the remaining cells are forced. A large statistic means the observed counts stray far from what independence predicts, while a small one means they match it, so only large values count against independence.

## Key takeaways

HEART: First, a few memory hooks.

LEWIS: The null holds the equal sign, and you either reject it or fail to reject it. Never accept it, the same way a jury never declares anyone innocent.

HEART: For the two errors: Type one cries wolf, Type two misses the wolf. The significance level is the chance of crying wolf when there is no wolf. Power is the chance of catching a wolf that really is there, which is one minus the Type two error rate. And only more data cuts both errors at once.

LEWIS: For the p value: if the p is low, the null must go, where low means below the significance level. And because the p value assumes the null is true, it is never the probability that the null is true.

HEART: For the z cutoffs: one point six five does double duty, one tail at five percent or two tails at ten. One point nine six is two tails at five. Two point three three is one tail at one, and two point five eight is two tails at one.

LEWIS: For the test statistic: signal over noise. The gap between your sample and the claim, measured in standard errors.

HEART: For the test map: means use t. One variance uses chi square. Two variances use F, which is a fraction, one variance over the other, with the bigger one on top and only the upper tail to check.

LEWIS: For two means: same subjects, same periods, or before and after, so pair them. Two unrelated groups, so compare their means with a pooled variance.

HEART: For degrees of freedom: one mean, one variance and paired data all use one less than the count, where the count for paired data is the number of pairs. Two independent means use the combined count minus two. Correlation and Spearman use the sample size minus two. F uses each sample's size minus one. A contingency table uses rows minus one times columns minus one.

LEWIS: For when to go nonparametric, think ranks, wrecks and runs. Ranks: the data are only an ordering. Wrecks: the parametric assumptions are wrecked, like a small sample from a clearly non normal population. Runs: the question is not about a parameter, such as whether a sequence is random.

HEART: For a contingency table: if the traits are unrelated, every row splits like the whole table. Big gaps from that pattern mean a big chi square, and you reject independence in the upper tail. Now, how you would say the big ideas to a senior leader.

LEWIS: On testing a claim: before we act on a pattern, we ask how often luck alone would produce a pattern this strong, and if that would be rare enough, we treat the pattern as real. The jargon is hypothesis testing, the null hypothesis and the p value.

HEART: On error tradeoffs: any screen strict enough to rarely raise a false alarm will miss more real problems, and the only way to improve on both counts is more data. The jargon is Type one and Type two errors, the significance level and power.

LEWIS: On size versus significance: with enough data, even a tiny effect can pass the test, so we also ask whether it is big enough to matter. The jargon is statistical significance, which is not the same thing as a strong relationship.

HEART: On before and after comparisons: when we measure the same branches before and after a change, we compare each branch with itself, so everything the two measurements share drops out. The jargon is a paired comparisons test, as opposed to a difference in means test.

LEWIS: On volatility: to check whether risk has changed, we test a variance against a benchmark, or compare two variances directly. The jargon is the chi square test for one variance and the F test for two.

HEART: And on whether two categories are linked: we compare the counts we actually see with the counts we would expect if the two had nothing to do with each other. The jargon is a contingency table and the chi square test of independence.
