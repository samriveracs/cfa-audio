## The big picture

This episode covers two short readings that sit between domestic economic policy and currencies. The first is geopolitics, which asks how countries deal with one another and how that creates risk for investors. The second is international trade, which asks why countries trade at all, who wins and who loses when a government restricts trade, and why countries form trading blocs.

On the exam, both readings are mostly about classification and direction of effect. You will be asked to match a description to a label, such as which archetype a country fits, which type of geopolitical risk an event represents, or how far a group of countries has gone toward economic integration. You will also be asked who gains and who loses from a tariff, a quota, a voluntary export restraint or an export subsidy. The arithmetic is light, but the vocabulary is precise, and the wrong answers are usually close neighbors of the right one.

The two readings fit together. Trade policy is one of the main tools of geopolitics. A country that cooperates tends to lower trade barriers and join trade agreements, while a country that turns inward tends to raise them.

## Module 16.1, Geopolitics

Start with a definition. Geopolitics is the study of how geography shapes politics and the relations between countries. Geography here includes where a country sits, what natural resources it holds, who its neighbors are and which trade routes it depends on.

Two kinds of actors take part. State actors are national governments, their leaders and the political bodies that act for them, and they control borders, armed forces and national resources. Non state actors are everyone else who takes part in global affairs, such as companies, non governmental organizations, charities and individuals. They have influence, but they do not control national security or a country's resources.

The first big idea is cooperation versus competition. A cooperative country works with others on a give and take basis. Its rules are fairly stable and predictable, it lets goods, capital and people cross its borders, and it shares technology. A noncooperative country acts on its own. Its policies can change abruptly, it limits flows across its borders, and it is quick to retaliate.

Why would a country cooperate? Two motives come first. One is national security, meaning protection of the country, its people and its institutions from outside threats. The other is economic interest, especially secure access to resources and markets. A country that lacks energy or farmland has a strong reason to stay on good terms with its suppliers, while a country rich in resources can afford to be more independent.

Three other forces make cooperation easier. First is standardization. When countries agree on common rules for things like shipping, product safety or financial reporting, the cost of doing business across borders falls. Second is shared culture and history, such as a common language or long standing ties. Third is soft power, which is the ability to influence other countries through attraction rather than force or payment, for example through culture, education, media and values.

The second big idea is globalization. Globalization is the process by which people, companies and governments around the world become more connected through flows of goods, services, capital, information and people. The opposite of globalization is nationalism, where a country promotes its own economic interests even at the expense of other countries.

Political cooperation is mainly a matter for state actors, while globalization is driven mostly by non state actors, especially companies and investors. Companies globalize for three broad reasons. First, profit, because they can sell to more customers and produce where costs are lower. Second, access to resources and markets they cannot find at home. Third, intrinsic gains, such as exposure to new ideas, talent and cultures.

Globalization also has costs. The gains are spread unevenly, both between countries and within them. Countries become dependent on each other, so a disruption in one place can travel along supply chains to many others. And companies can take on environmental, social and governance problems, or ESG problems, such as weak labor or environmental standards at foreign suppliers.

Now put the two big ideas on two axes and you get four archetypes. This is the most testable part of the module. One axis runs from cooperation to noncooperation. The other runs from globalization to nationalism.

Nationalist and noncooperative is autarky. An autarkic country aims to be self sufficient. It trades little, limits foreign capital, and the state often controls strategic industries through state owned companies. The appeal is control and independence, and the cost is that it gives up most of the gains from trade.

Globalized but noncooperative is hegemony. A hegemon is a regional or global leader that is deeply involved in the world economy but sets the terms on its own. It uses its economic, political or military weight to secure resources and influence, rather than bargaining as an equal. Countries that line up with the hegemon can share in the benefits it offers.

Nationalist but cooperative is bilateralism. Here a country cooperates through one to one agreements with chosen partners. It can have many such partners, but each deal is negotiated separately, and the country keeps a firm focus on its own interests.

Globalized and cooperative is multilateralism. A multilateral country takes part in many reciprocal agreements and shared rules with many countries at once. It reaps large gains from trade and investment, but it depends on that cooperation holding, which leaves it exposed when others pull back. A regional version, where a group of nearby countries cooperate closely, is often called regionalism.

Here is the trap. Hegemony sounds like the opposite of globalization, because people associate it with control. On these axes, though, a hegemon is globalized, and what makes it a hegemon is that it does not cooperate on equal terms. A simple memory hook is that both cooperative archetypes end in lateral. Bilateral means two at a time and leans nationalist, while multilateral means many at once and is the globalized one.

Countries pursue their goals with three kinds of tools. National security tools put pressure on another country by acting, directly or indirectly, on its resources, its people or its borders. Armed conflict is the most extreme, espionage is an indirect example, and military alliances are a cooperative example. Economic tools can also be cooperative or not. Free trade agreements, common markets and economic unions are cooperative, while tariffs, quotas and nationalization, which is the state taking ownership of private companies, are noncooperative.

Financial tools follow the same split. Letting currencies be exchanged freely across borders and welcoming foreign investment are cooperative. Limiting access to local currency markets, restricting foreign investment and imposing sanctions are noncooperative, where sanctions block financial dealings with targeted countries, companies or people. Tools are often used in combination, and a change in the tools a country reaches for is a clue that its archetype may be shifting.

International organizations exist to support cooperation in trade and finance. There are three to know, and the exam likes to swap their jobs. The International Monetary Fund, or I M F, looks after the stability of the international monetary system, meaning exchange rates and payments between countries. It monitors members' economic policies, gives advice and technical help, and lends to member countries that run into balance of payments problems, usually in return for commitments to change policy. Think of it as the short term crisis lender to countries.

The World Bank focuses on long term development and reducing poverty. It provides low interest loans, interest free credits and grants to developing countries for projects such as infrastructure, education and health. The World Trade Organization, or W T O, deals with the rules of trade between countries. It provides a forum for negotiating trade agreements, monitors trade policies and settles trade disputes between members.

The trap is mixing up the I M F and the World Bank. If the question is about a currency or payments crisis and short term financing, the answer is the I M F. If it is about funding roads, power plants or schools in a developing country, it is the World Bank. If it is about trade rules or tariff disputes, it is the W T O.

Next comes geopolitical risk, which is the risk that tensions or actions between actors disturb the normal, peaceful course of international relations. There are three types. Event risk revolves around dates known in advance, such as elections, referendums, new laws taking effect or treaty deadlines. You know when it will happen, even if you do not know the outcome.

Exogenous risk is sudden and unanticipated, such as an uprising, an invasion or the aftermath of a natural disaster. It can change a country's cooperative stance, the ability of companies to operate across borders, or both. Thematic risk is known and evolves over a long period, such as climate change, cyber threats and the ongoing threat of terrorism.

The trap here is the word known. Event risk and thematic risk are both known. The difference is that event risk is tied to a specific date, while thematic risk builds gradually with no single date. Exogenous risk is the one nobody scheduled.

To assess a geopolitical risk, an investor looks at three dimensions. First is likelihood, meaning how probable it is. Second is velocity, meaning how quickly its impact reaches markets and the economy. Third is the size and nature of the impact.

Velocity and impact deserve a moment. A fast shock, such as a sudden invasion, can move prices within hours. Its market effect is often sharp but short lived, so it matters most to short horizon investors. A slow risk, such as climate change, builds over years, is easy to underrate and matters more for long horizon investors. Size and nature of impact asks whether the effect is broad, hitting a whole economy or many markets at once, or concentrated in a single country, industry or company.

How does any of this reach a portfolio? An asset's value depends on its expected cash flows and the rate used to discount them. Geopolitical risk can lower expected cash flows by disrupting supply chains, raising costs or cutting off markets. It can also raise the discount rate, because greater uncertainty pushes up the risk premium investors demand. Either way, prices of risky assets tend to fall, and money often moves toward assets seen as safe.

Because geopolitical risks rarely unfold in a straight line, a single point forecast is not very useful. Instead, investors build scenarios and watch signposts. A signpost is a piece of data, an event or a threshold that tells you a scenario is becoming more or less likely. One common approach treats signposts like a traffic light, where green means no action is needed, amber means more caution and preparation, and red means it is time to act on the plan. In bank and credit union capital planning, the same scenario and signpost discipline shows up in stress testing, where a geopolitical shock is translated into paths for interest rates, credit losses and funding costs.

Here is a small worked example. A country has long belonged to several trade agreements and welcomes foreign capital, which makes it multilateral. An election is scheduled for next spring, which is event risk. The new government raises tariffs, caps foreign ownership and takes over the largest mining company. Those are noncooperative economic and financial tools, and together they signal a move from multilateralism toward autarky, so an investor's signposts should be flashing amber or red.

The rule to carry out of this module is the grid. Cooperation with globalization is multilateralism, and cooperation with nationalism is bilateralism. Noncooperation with globalization is hegemony, and noncooperation with nationalism is autarky. The three risk types are event risk on a known date, exogenous risk out of nowhere, and thematic risk building over time.

## Module 17.1, International Trade

A few terms first. An economy that does not trade is closed, and the price of a good inside a closed economy is called its autarkic price, the same autarky you met a few minutes ago. An economy that trades is open and faces the world price. Net exports are exports minus imports, so a trade surplus means exports exceed imports, and a trade deficit means the reverse.

The terms of trade measure what a country's exports can buy. The terms of trade equal an index of export prices divided by an index of import prices, multiplied by 100. If export prices rise relative to import prices, the terms of trade improve, because each unit of exports pays for more imports.

Why trade at all? The core reason is comparative advantage. A country has an absolute advantage in a good if it can produce it with fewer resources than another country. It has a comparative advantage if its opportunity cost of producing the good, measured in other goods given up, is lower. Gains from trade depend on comparative advantage, not absolute advantage.

Here is a quick example. In country A, one worker can make 10 units of cloth or 5 units of wine in a day. In country B, one worker can make 4 units of cloth or 4 units of wine. A is better at both, so it has the absolute advantage in both. Now look at opportunity cost. In A, each unit of wine costs 2 units of cloth, but in B, each unit of wine costs only 1 unit of cloth, so B has the comparative advantage in wine, and A has it in cloth.

Suppose they trade at one and a half units of cloth for each unit of wine. B gives up a unit of wine and gets one and a half units of cloth, which beats the 1 unit it could make at home. A gets a unit of wine for one and a half units of cloth instead of the 2 it would give up at home. Both come out ahead, even though B is less productive at everything.

Two classic models explain where comparative advantage comes from. In the Ricardian model, labor is the only factor of production, and comparative advantage comes from differences in technology, meaning labor productivity. In the Heckscher Ohlin model, there are two factors, capital and labor, and comparative advantage comes from differences in how much of each a country has. A country exports goods that make heavy use of its relatively abundant factor, so a labor rich country exports labor intensive goods. The Heckscher Ohlin model also predicts that trade shifts income within a country, with owners of the abundant factor gaining and owners of the scarce factor losing.

So what are the benefits of trade? Countries specialize in what they do relatively well, so total output and consumption rise. Larger markets let firms reach economies of scale. Consumers get lower prices and more variety. Competition from abroad pushes domestic firms to become more efficient, and trade spreads technology and know how.

The costs fall on specific groups. Workers and firms in industries that compete with imports can lose jobs and income, and moving those workers into new industries takes time and retraining. Trade can also widen income inequality within a country. The usual finding is that the gains to the winners exceed the losses to the losers, so the country as a whole gains. But the losers are concentrated and visible, which is why trade restrictions attract political support.

Now the restrictions. There are four to know, which are tariffs, import quotas, voluntary export restraints and export subsidies. The first three act on imports, and the last one acts on exports.

A tariff is a tax on imported goods. Assume first a small country, meaning one too small to affect the world price. Then a tariff raises the domestic price by the full amount of the tariff. Domestic producers expand because they get a higher price, consumers buy less because they pay more, and imports shrink from both sides.

Who wins and who loses? Consumers lose consumer surplus. Domestic producers gain producer surplus, and the government collects tariff revenue. Net, the country loses, and that net loss is called the deadweight loss.

Let me walk through numbers. The world price is 10 dollars and the country imports. At 10 dollars, consumers buy 100 units, domestic firms make 40, and imports fill the gap of 60. Now a 2 dollar tariff raises the domestic price to 12 dollars. Consumers cut back to 90 units, domestic firms raise output to 50, and imports fall to 40.

Tariff revenue is 2 dollars on each of 40 imported units, which is 80 dollars. Consumers pay 2 dollars more on each of the 90 units they still buy, which is 180 dollars, and they also lose 10 dollars of value on the 10 units they stop buying, for a total loss of 190 dollars. Producers earn 2 dollars more on the 40 units they already made, which is 80 dollars, plus 10 dollars on the 10 extra units, for a gain of 90 dollars.

Now add it up. Producers gain 90 and the government gains 80, which is 170 dollars, while consumers lose 190. The country is 20 dollars worse off, and that 20 is the deadweight loss. It comes from two triangles. The production loss is from the 10 extra units now made at home at a cost above the world price, and the consumption loss is from the 10 units that buyers valued above the world price but no longer buy.

Each triangle equals one half, times the tariff, times the change in quantity. Here that is one half, times 2 dollars, times 10 units, which is 10 dollars for each triangle.

The large country case is different. A large importer that buys less can push the world price down. That improves its terms of trade, and the gain can outweigh the deadweight loss, so national welfare can rise. But the gain comes at the trading partners' expense, world welfare falls, and partners may retaliate with tariffs of their own.

An import quota limits the quantity of a good that can be imported. Set the quota at 40 units in our example and you get the same result as the 2 dollar tariff, with a domestic price of 12 dollars, consumption of 90 and domestic output of 50. The difference is who gets the 80 dollars. With a quota, the gap between the domestic price and the world price on the 40 imported units becomes quota rent, earned by whoever holds the import licenses.

If the government auctions the licenses, it captures the rent, and the quota looks just like the tariff. If domestic importers receive the licenses for free, the rent stays in the country but goes to them. If foreign exporters hold the licenses, the rent leaves the country, and the national loss grows from 20 dollars to 100 dollars.

A voluntary export restraint is an agreement by the exporting country to limit how much it ships to the importing country, usually made under pressure to head off a tariff or quota. It works like a quota where the licenses belong to the foreign exporters. So the importing country suffers the deadweight loss plus the lost rent, and its government collects nothing. That is why a voluntary export restraint costs the importing country more than an equivalent tariff.

An export subsidy is a payment from the government to domestic firms for each unit they export. Since firms can earn the world price plus the subsidy abroad, they will not sell at home for less, so the domestic price rises. Domestic production rises, domestic consumption falls and exports grow. Producers gain, consumers lose, and taxpayers fund the subsidy, so national welfare falls.

For a large country, the extra exports push the world price down, which worsens its terms of trade and makes the loss bigger. Trading partners often respond with countervailing duties, which are tariffs aimed at subsidized imports.

Now here is the summary pattern, which is where questions cluster. All four restrictions raise the domestic price, raise domestic production, lower domestic consumption and make a small country worse off. Producers gain and consumers lose in every case.

What differs is the government. A tariff raises government revenue. A quota raises revenue only if the licenses are sold. A voluntary export restraint gives the government nothing, and an export subsidy costs the government money.

Here is the trap. Because producers gain and the government may collect revenue, a question may tempt you to say a tariff helps a small country. It does not, because consumers always lose more than producers and the government gain combined. The only case where a tariff can raise national welfare is a large country that improves its terms of trade.

Now trading blocs. A regional trading bloc is a group of countries that lowers barriers among its members, and there are five levels, each adding one feature to the level before. A free trade area removes barriers on trade among members, but each member keeps its own trade policy toward the rest of the world. The agreement among the United States, Canada and Mexico is the usual example.

A customs union adds a common trade policy toward nonmembers, including a common external tariff. A common market adds free movement of the factors of production, labor and capital, among members. An economic union adds common economic institutions and coordinated economic policy. A monetary union adds a single currency, which means a single central bank and a single monetary policy, and the euro area is the standard example.

To keep the order straight, listen for the last feature added. A common external tariff means at least a customs union, and free movement of workers and capital means at least a common market. Shared institutions and coordinated policy mean an economic union, and a shared currency means a monetary union.

Why form a bloc? The main economic gain is trade creation, which happens when high cost domestic production is replaced by cheaper imports from a partner. Larger markets bring economies of scale and more competition. Blocs also attract foreign investment, and closer economic ties tend to reduce political conflict, which links straight back to geopolitics.

The main economic cost is trade diversion. It happens when cheaper imports from a nonmember are replaced by more expensive imports from a member, only because the member no longer pays the tariff. Picture an outside producer that sells at 100 dollars and a partner that sells at 110 dollars, with a 20 percent tariff on every import. Before the bloc, the outsider's good costs buyers 120 dollars and the partner's costs 132, so the country buys from the outsider.

After the bloc removes the tariff on the partner only, the partner's good costs 110 dollars, below the outsider's 120, so buyers switch. Consumers pay less, but the country now uses 110 dollars of real resources per unit instead of 100, and it gives up the 20 dollars of tariff revenue. A bloc improves welfare when trade creation outweighs trade diversion.

Blocs face other challenges too. Members give up some national sovereignty, and in a monetary union a member can no longer set its own interest rates or let its currency adjust when its economy weakens. Gains and losses are spread unevenly among members, and cultural or political differences can strain the bloc.

The rule to carry out of this module is this. A tariff, a quota, a voluntary export restraint and an export subsidy all raise the domestic price, help producers, hurt consumers and reduce national welfare for a small country. What differs is who keeps the gap between the domestic price and the world price. The government keeps it under a tariff, the license holders keep it under a quota, and foreign exporters keep it under a voluntary export restraint. Under an export subsidy, the government pays that gap to exporters on every unit they ship abroad.

## Recall check

Q: A country is deeply involved in world trade and finance, but it sets terms on its own, using its economic and military weight rather than reciprocal agreements. Which archetype does it most likely fit?

Answer: Hegemony. It sits in the globalized but noncooperative quadrant. The tempting wrong answer is multilateralism, but a multilateral country cooperates through reciprocal rules with many partners.

Q: A national referendum on leaving a trade bloc is scheduled for six months from now. Which type of geopolitical risk is this?

Answer: Event risk, because it revolves around a date known in advance. It is not thematic risk, which builds gradually with no fixed date, and it is not exogenous risk, which arrives without warning.

Q: A country suddenly cannot pay for its imports, and its currency is collapsing. Which international organization is most likely to provide short term financing?

Answer: The I M F. It lends to member countries with balance of payments problems, usually in return for policy commitments. The World Bank funds long term development, and the W T O handles trade rules and disputes.

Q: In country A, a worker makes 8 shirts or 4 phones a day. In country B, a worker makes 3 shirts or 3 phones a day. Which country has the comparative advantage in phones?

Answer: Country B. A phone costs 2 shirts in A but only 1 shirt in B, so B has the lower opportunity cost. A has the absolute advantage in both goods, which is the tempting distraction, but trade follows comparative advantage.

Q: An importing country can either impose a tariff or accept a voluntary export restraint that holds imports to the same quantity. Which is most likely to cause the larger welfare loss for the importing country?

Answer: The voluntary export restraint. Both raise the domestic price by the same amount and create the same deadweight loss. Under the tariff, the government collects the gap between the domestic and world price on each import, but under the restraint that rent goes to foreign exporters.

Q: Several countries remove all trade barriers among themselves, set a common external tariff and let workers and capital move freely, but each keeps its own currency and its own economic policy. What type of bloc is this?

Answer: A common market. The common external tariff makes it at least a customs union, and free movement of labor and capital takes it to a common market. It lacks the shared institutions of an economic union and the shared currency of a monetary union.

## Recap

Geopolitics studies how geography shapes relations between countries. Crossing cooperation with globalization gives four archetypes. Autarky is noncooperative and nationalist, hegemony is noncooperative but globalized, bilateralism is cooperative but nationalist, and multilateralism is cooperative and globalized. The tools of geopolitics are national security, economic and financial, and each can be cooperative or not. The I M F protects monetary stability and lends in balance of payments crises, the World Bank funds long term development, and the W T O oversees trade rules and settles trade disputes. Event risk has a known date, exogenous risk is a surprise and thematic risk builds over time, and each is judged by likelihood, velocity and size of impact, using scenarios and signposts.

Gains from trade come from comparative advantage, meaning a lower opportunity cost, not from absolute advantage. Tariffs, quotas, voluntary export restraints and export subsidies all raise the domestic price, help producers, hurt consumers and lower national welfare in a small country, and the key difference is who keeps the gap between the domestic and world price, or under an export subsidy, who pays it. Trading blocs build up from free trade area to customs union, common market, economic union and monetary union, and a bloc helps when trade creation outweighs trade diversion.
