LEWIS: Reading 16 covers geopolitics: how countries cooperate or compete, the four archetypes that come out of that, the three big international organizations, and the kinds of geopolitical risk and how they reach a portfolio. Reading 17 covers why countries gain from trade, who wins and who loses when a government restricts it, and the ladder of trading blocs from a free trade area up to a monetary union.

## Module 16.1, Geopolitics

HEART: Geopolitics has two meanings, and both matter. The first is simply how countries deal with one another. The second is how geography shapes those dealings. Picture a landlocked country. Every container it sends abroad has to cross a neighbor's territory to reach a port, so its relationship with that neighbor is about far more than friendship. Geography has written part of its foreign policy for it.

LEWIS: The players come in two kinds. State actors are national governments and the people who act for them. Nonstate actors are everyone else with a stake across borders: companies, nongovernment organizations such as charities, and individuals. A carmaker deciding which country gets its next plant is taking part in geopolitics too, just without an army.

HEART: The first lens is cooperation versus competition. Countries can cooperate on diplomatic matters, military matters, economics and culture. On the economic side, cooperation means letting goods, services and capital move across borders, agreeing to harmonize tariffs, adopting common rules and standards, and sharing information and technology.

LEWIS: The key word is spectrum. No country is simply cooperative or noncooperative. Think of a neighbor who happily shares a fence line and lends you a ladder, but fights you over a parking space. A country can be open on trade and guarded on defense, and its stance can shift over time as its domestic politics change. That is why analysts watch who is making the decisions right now and how long the political cycle runs, because each change of leadership is a chance for a new direction.

HEART: So why would a country cooperate at all? Because it serves its national interests. And those interests come in a hierarchy, with survival at the top. A household is a good picture. Rent and groceries come before a new television. For a country, security and the basics of feeding and powering itself come before everything else.

LEWIS: Which is why natural resources shape priorities. A country that buys most of its fuel abroad has a survival level interest in keeping the tankers arriving and staying on good terms with its suppliers. A country with plenty of farmland, water and energy can afford to be more independent.

HEART: Nonstate actors push toward cooperation for their own reasons. Companies and investors want to send their money and resources wherever they earn the most, and sometimes that is abroad. Moving resources across borders is far easier when the rules match, and that is the argument for standardization. The shipping container is the classic example. Once the world settled on a few standard box sizes, the same box could move from a ship to a train to a truck anywhere, and the cost of moving goods between countries fell dramatically.

LEWIS: Culture matters as well. A shared language, or a long history of people migrating between two countries, makes cooperation more natural. So do institutions, both formal ones such as laws, courts and organizations, and informal ones such as customs and habits. Strong, stable institutions make cooperation easier, because a foreign partner can count on contracts being honored and on rules not changing overnight.

HEART: And culture is one route to soft power, the ability to influence other countries without using force or threatening it. A country whose universities draw students from around the world, or whose films and music travel everywhere, builds goodwill that can make other governments more willing to work with it.

LEWIS: The second lens is globalization versus nationalism. Globalization is the long term trend toward the world's economies and cultures becoming integrated. Think of a phone designed in one country, built with chips made in a second, assembled in a third, and then sold everywhere.

HEART: Nationalism, in this narrow economic sense, means a country pursuing its own economic interests independently of other countries, or in competition with them. That is the only sense the word carries here. And again it is a spectrum. Countries toward the globalized end trade more in both directions, let capital move more freely across borders, allow currencies to be exchanged freely, and are more open to cultural exchange.

LEWIS: Now put the two lenses together. Picture one axis running from cooperation to noncooperation, crossed by another running from globalization to nationalism. You get four quadrants, four archetypes, and this is the part of the reading the exam loves. Real countries rarely sit neatly in one box, but the boxes give you the vocabulary.

HEART: Picture four people in a small town. The first grows her own food, makes her own clothes and keeps to herself. That is autarky, noncooperative and nationalist. The goal is self reliance, producing most or all of what the country needs at home. Autarky tends to come with a state dominated society, where the government controls industry and the media. History offers a near example. For roughly two centuries, until the eighteen fifties, Japan sharply limited its contact and trade with the outside world.

LEWIS: The second person owns the biggest store in town. Everyone deals with him and he deals with everyone, but on his terms. That is hegemony, noncooperative but globalized. A hegemon takes full part in the world economy, and it is big enough to sway other countries without having to cooperate with them.

HEART: The third person makes handshake deals one neighbor at a time. She may have a deal with half the street, but each one is negotiated separately, and she stays out of the neighborhood association. That is bilateralism, cooperative but nationalist. Cooperation happens between two countries at a time. A country can have many of these pairings while avoiding arrangements that bring many countries together at once.

LEWIS: And the fourth person joins the farmers' cooperative, the neighborhood association and the town council, all with shared rules. That is multilateralism, cooperative and globalized. These countries trade heavily and cooperate with many other countries at once. One variation is regionalism, where a country cooperates multilaterally with its neighbors but much less with the wider world.

HEART: Here is the trap. The word hegemony sounds closed and controlling, so people want to put it on the nationalist side. It belongs on the globalized side. What makes a hegemon is that it does not need to cooperate on equal terms, not that it stays out of the world economy.

LEWIS: A way to keep them apart. Both cooperative archetypes have lateral in their names, and lateral refers to sides. Bilateral is two sides at a time, and it leans nationalist. Multilateral is many sides at once, and it is the globalized one.

HEART: One more wrinkle. A country's companies and investors may be far more globalized than its government. Businesses look abroad to raise profits, cut costs and reach new customers. Investors look abroad for higher returns and for diversification.

LEWIS: And their money crosses borders in two forms. Buying the shares or bonds of foreign companies is a portfolio investment flow. Owning actual production capacity in another country, such as building a factory there, is foreign direct investment. A portfolio investor can usually sell within a day. A factory owner is staying for years.

HEART: Next, the international organizations that support trade and finance. Three matter here, and exam questions like to swap their jobs. The International Monetary Fund and the World Bank were both designed at the Bretton Woods conference in nineteen forty four, near the end of the Second World War.

LEWIS: The International Monetary Fund, the I M F, deals with money between countries. Its purposes are to promote cooperation on monetary matters, to help international trade grow in a balanced way, to promote stable exchange rates, to help build a system through which countries can make payments to one another, and to make funds available, with safeguards, to members in balance of payments trouble.

HEART: Balance of payments trouble means a country cannot pay for what it buys abroad or meet its foreign debts, often with its currency under heavy pressure. So think of the I M F as the emergency lender to countries, the one that steps in when foreign money runs short, usually in return for commitments to change policy.

LEWIS: The World Bank is about long term development and fighting poverty. It provides money and technical help to developing countries through two arms. The International Bank for Reconstruction and Development lends to middle income countries and to poorer countries that are creditworthy. The International Development Association focuses on the very poorest countries. Between them, they offer low interest loans, interest free credits and grants for things like schools, health care, roads and power, farming, the environment, government administration, and stronger financial and private sectors. They also share expertise and help governments and private businesses work together.

HEART: The World Trade Organization, the W T O, is the rulebook and the referee for trade. It is the one global body devoted to the rules of trade between countries, and its goal is trade that moves freely and works smoothly, with as few surprises as possible.

LEWIS: Most trading nations have negotiated, signed and ratified its agreements, which act as the binding rules of commerce among members. Each member gains rights in the other members' markets and accepts limits on its own trade policies. And when members clash, the dispute settlement process works out what the agreements require, so a trade quarrel is more likely to be settled by the rules than to escalate into a political or military confrontation.

HEART: So here is the sort. A currency or payments crisis that needs emergency financing points to the I M F. Roads, schools and clinics in a developing country point to the World Bank. Trade rules and trade disputes point to the W T O.

LEWIS: Now the tools of geopolitics, the levers that countries, mostly through their governments, pull to advance their interests. There are three categories: national security tools, economic tools and financial tools.

HEART: National security tools include armed conflict, espionage, and agreements between two countries or among many that are meant to reinforce or prevent armed conflict, such as a mutual defense pact. A tool is active if a country is using it now, and threatened if it is not using it yet but looks likely to. Armed conflict hurts economies directly. It destroys factories, roads and other productive capital, and it drives people to migrate away from the fighting.

LEWIS: Economic tools come in cooperative and noncooperative versions. The cooperative ones are the trading arrangements coming up in the next module: free trade areas, common markets, and economic and monetary unions. The noncooperative ones include domestic content requirements, voluntary export restraints, and nationalization, which is the state taking control of a company or a whole industry. Picture a government taking over the foreign owned mines in its territory. The foreign investors lose control of what they paid for, and other investors think twice before coming in.

HEART: Financial tools center on foreign investment and the exchange of currencies. A country that welcomes foreign investment and lets its currency be exchanged freely is wielding them cooperatively. One that restricts either is wielding them noncooperatively. And sanctions are restrictions aimed at the financial interests of a specific actor, such as a particular country, company or person. They often go hand in hand with national security tools.

LEWIS: That brings us to geopolitical risk, the possibility of events that interrupt peaceful relations between countries. It comes in three types.

HEART: Event risk is about events whose timing you know but whose outcome you do not. An election, a referendum, a treaty deadline. Picture a close national election. The date has been on the calendar for months, but nobody knows the winner, and the currency can swing hard on the night the results come in.

LEWIS: Exogenous risk is the unanticipated kind, an outbreak of war or a rebellion that nobody scheduled. When Kuwait was invaded in the summer of nineteen ninety, oil prices roughly doubled within a few months.

HEART: Thematic risk covers known factors whose effects play out over long periods. Think of neighbors competing for water from a shared river over decades, or a changing climate slowly moving where crops can grow. Everyone knows these themes exist. What nobody can name is a single date when they hit.

LEWIS: And here is the trap. Event risk and thematic risk are both known. The difference is the calendar. Event risk has a date. Thematic risk builds slowly with no single date. Exogenous risk is the one nobody saw coming.

HEART: How does any of this reach prices? Through the risk premium. When geopolitical risk rises, investors demand a higher return to hold assets in that country or region, and a higher required return means lower prices today. When the risk fades, the premium shrinks and prices can recover.

LEWIS: To judge a particular risk, ask three questions. How likely is it? That is likelihood. How much would it hurt investment outcomes? That is impact. And how quickly would prices reflect it? That is velocity.

HEART: The two lenses from earlier help with likelihood. A cooperative, globalized country tends to be less likely to end up in armed conflict, partly because it has so much to lose by fighting its trading partners. But it is more exposed to other risks, such as supply chain disruptions. After the earthquake and tsunami in Japan in twenty eleven, carmakers on other continents ran short of parts, because so many of them depended on the same few suppliers.

LEWIS: Velocity is sorted into high, medium and low. High velocity means the effect shows up in prices in the short term, and exogenous risks often move this fast. A black swan is a low likelihood exogenous event with large short term effects. Here the investment horizon matters. A long horizon investor can usually ride out a black swan, while a short horizon investor, who needs the money soon, may have to act.

HEART: Medium velocity risks tend to damage particular companies or industries, by raising their costs or disrupting how they produce. Picture a shipping route that becomes dangerous, so freight and insurance costs climb for every importer that depends on it. Low velocity risks play out over the long term and tend to show up as environmental, social and governance issues. Those matter most to investors with long horizons.

LEWIS: Then impact. Studying every geopolitical risk in depth would cost more than it is worth, so investors save their effort for the high impact ones, the risks that could do real damage. For those, the next question is reach. A discrete impact lands on one company or one industry. A broad impact lands on a whole country, a region, or the world.

HEART: And the business cycle changes the answer. The same geopolitical shock tends to hit investment values harder in a recession than in an expansion. A healthy person shrugs off a cold that would flatten someone who is already run down.

LEWIS: Because these risks rarely unfold in a straight line, investors run scenario analysis. It can be qualitative, a story about how events might play out, or quantitative, with numbers attached for growth, interest rates and earnings. Then they pick signposts, pieces of data that signal when a scenario is becoming more or less likely. The cost of insuring a government's bonds against default, the price of shipping insurance through a contested strait, or a central bank steadily spending its foreign currency reserves can all serve as signposts.

HEART: Banks and credit unions do the same thing in stress testing. A geopolitical scenario gets translated into paths for interest rates, credit losses and deposit flows, and the signposts tell management when to move from watching to acting.

LEWIS: The takeaway. Cooperation crossed with globalization gives four archetypes: autarky, hegemony, bilateralism and multilateralism. Geopolitical risk comes as event, exogenous or thematic risk. You judge it by likelihood, impact and velocity, and it reaches prices through the risk premium.

## Module 17.1, International Trade

HEART: Why do countries trade at all? Start in a restaurant kitchen. The head chef chops onions faster than the prep cook. In fact she is faster at every job in that kitchen. But every hour she spends chopping is an hour she is not creating the dishes that fill the dining room. So she leaves the chopping to the prep cook, because her time is worth far more elsewhere.

LEWIS: That is comparative advantage. A country has a comparative advantage in a good when its relative cost of producing it is lower, meaning it gives up less of other goods to make it. So each country does more of what it makes relatively cheaply, sells that abroad, and buys the rest from countries that make it relatively cheaply. The same resources now yield more output in total, and both countries come out richer.

HEART: The trap is absolute advantage, being able to make something with fewer resources than anyone else. The chef had the absolute advantage in chopping. Trade still follows comparative advantage. A country that is more productive at everything still gains by specializing and trading, and so does the less productive country.

LEWIS: Where does comparative advantage come from? Two sources. Differences in technology, meaning how productive a country's workers and methods are. And differences in resource endowments, meaning what a country has in abundance. A country with vast sunny farmland and a country with a deep pool of trained engineers will naturally specialize in different things.

HEART: Newer models of trade add more sources of gain. Economies of scale come first. A factory that serves the whole world can be far bigger than one that serves a single country, which spreads its fixed costs thinner and lowers the cost of each unit. Then there is variety, more choice for consumers. Competition from abroad lowers costs and raises quality. And resources shift toward their most productive uses.

LEWIS: Trade also checks domestic monopolies. If one company is the only cement maker in the country, it can charge what it likes. Let in cement from abroad, and that pricing power shrinks.

HEART: And countries often trade the same kind of product in both directions. A country may sell its own cheeses abroad and buy cheeses from its neighbors. Each producer makes a differentiated version, which is monopolistic competition across borders, and consumers get both variety and the lower costs that come from specialization. Economists call this intra industry trade.

LEWIS: Now the costs, because trade has losers. The most cited are lost jobs in industries that compete with foreign goods, and greater income inequality. And the winners and losers differ in each country. In the importing country, buyers of the good gain, while the workers and firms that compete with those foreign goods lose. In the exporting country, the producers and their workers gain, but local buyers may pay more, because foreign demand bids the price up.

HEART: Picture a country that exports a lot of beef. Ranchers and meatpackers do well. But shoppers at home pay more for steak, because they are now competing with buyers overseas.

LEWIS: Labor costs add another layer. Suppose a high wage country starts buying goods that take a lot of hand labor to make, such as shoes or toys, from a low wage country. Jobs and wages in that kind of work at home fall, and income inequality can widen.

HEART: So why do economists still favor trade? Because the overall gains are judged to be larger than the losses, especially in the long run. The winners gain so much that, in principle, they could make the losers whole and still come out ahead, and both the importing and the exporting country end up with a net gain. The short run pain is real. Unemployment can rise while workers retrain. But once they move into expanding industries, the costs are softened, or even reversed.

LEWIS: Governments restrict trade anyway, and they give reasons. Only two have some support from economists. The first is the infant industry argument. Shield a brand new industry from foreign competition for a while, like training wheels on a bicycle, so it can get big enough, and learn to produce cheaply enough, to hold its own against established foreign rivals. The second is national security. A country may choose to keep its own makers of ammunition and warships in business, even when buying abroad would be cheaper, because foreign supplies could be cut off in a war.

HEART: Two popular reasons get little support. Protecting domestic jobs sounds compelling, but trade also creates jobs, in export industries and in growing service industries, and every shopper pays more while the barrier stays up. Protecting domestic industries usually turns out to be firms lobbying for shelter from foreign competition, and again consumers pay for it through higher prices.

LEWIS: Other stated reasons include retaliating against another country's restrictions, raising government revenue from tariffs, offsetting subsidies that foreign governments pay their producers, and stopping dumping, which means foreign firms selling exports below their cost of production.

HEART: Now the restrictions themselves. A tariff is a tax on imported goods, collected by the government. A quota is a limit on how much of a good can be imported over some period. An export subsidy is a payment from a government to firms that export. A minimum domestic content rule requires that a certain share of a product's content come from the home country. Think of a rule that a set share of the parts in a car must be made at home. And a voluntary export restraint is an exporting country agreeing to hold back its own shipments of a good, often to head off a tariff or quota that a trading partner might otherwise impose.

LEWIS: Start with the tariff. Picture a toll booth at the border for imported bicycles. Assume a small country, one too small to move the world price. The tariff lifts the domestic price above the world price by the amount of the tariff. Domestic bike makers can now charge more, so they make more. Buyers pay more, so they buy fewer. Purchases from abroad get squeezed from both sides.

HEART: Who wins and who loses? Domestic producers gain. The government gains the tariff revenue. Foreign exporters lose sales. And domestic consumers lose, because they pay more for every bike they still buy and they give up some bikes they would have bought.

LEWIS: Economists keep score with surplus. Consumer surplus is the gap between what buyers would be willing to pay and what they actually pay. Producer surplus is the gap between the price producers receive and the least they would accept. The tariff wipes out a slice of consumer surplus. Part of that slice moves to domestic producers as extra producer surplus. Another part moves to the government as revenue, which is the tariff on each unit still imported. What is left over goes to nobody. That is the deadweight loss.

HEART: The deadweight loss has two pieces. One is on the production side. Bikes that could have been bought abroad at the world price are now made at home at a higher cost, which wastes resources. The other is on the consumption side. Some buyers valued a bike above the world price, but not above the new higher price, so they go without. For a small country, the gains to producers and the government never make up for the loss to consumers. National welfare falls.

LEWIS: Now the quota. Instead of taxing foreign goods, the government caps the quantity that can come in. Define an equivalent quota as one that cuts imports by the same amount as the tariff. Then the domestic price rises by the same amount, domestic producers gain the same amount, consumers lose the same amount, and the deadweight loss is identical.

HEART: So what is different?

LEWIS: Who collects the gap between the domestic price and the world price on each unit that still comes in. Under a tariff, the government collects it. Under a quota, the government hands foreign exporters licenses that let them sell a set amount into the country, and it can either sell those licenses or give them away. Think of a town that issues only a handful of liquor licenses. Because they are scarce, each one is valuable.

HEART: If the government sells the licenses for their full value, it captures that gap, and the quota works just like the tariff. If it hands them out free, the foreign exporters holding them keep the gap, and that gain is called quota rent. Then the importing country is hit twice, by the deadweight loss and by the quota rent that leaves the country. Together, that is everything consumers lose minus what producers gain.

LEWIS: A voluntary export restraint is the same story told from the other side. The exporting country agrees to limit what it ships. For the importing country, the cost is the same as an equivalent quota whose licenses are handed out free, so the quota rent goes to the foreign exporters and the importing government collects nothing. In the early nineteen eighties, Japan agreed to limit how many cars its makers shipped to the United States. With supply capped, car prices rose, and the scarcity value went to the sellers rather than to the American government.

HEART: And the trap is the word voluntary. It sounds gentle, but it costs the importing country more than an equivalent tariff, because it carries the same deadweight loss plus the lost rent.

LEWIS: Export subsidies run the other direction. The government pays its exporters for each unit sold abroad, and exporters gain. But think about what that does at home. If a firm can earn the world price plus the subsidy by selling abroad, it will not sell at home for less. So in a small country, the domestic price rises by the amount of the subsidy, to the world price plus the subsidy. Consumers at home pay more and lose surplus, taxpayers fund the subsidy, and national welfare falls.

HEART: For a large exporter, there is a second effect. Pushing so much extra product onto the world market drives the world price down. Buyers in other countries now pay less, so part of the subsidy ends up with them, while producers in those countries face cheaper competition and lose. In effect, the exporting country's taxpayers end up helping foreign buyers. That is one reason offsetting foreign subsidies shows up on the list of reasons for restricting trade.

LEWIS: Now the pattern, because this is where questions cluster. For the importing country, a tariff, a quota and a voluntary export restraint all do the same five things. Imports fall. The domestic price rises. Consumer surplus falls. Domestic production rises. Producer surplus rises.

HEART: And with one exception, they all reduce national welfare. The exception is a tariff or a quota imposed by a large country, one that buys so much of a good that cutting back pushes the world price down. Under the right conditions, paying less for the goods it still buys from abroad can outweigh the deadweight loss. But the gain comes at the expense of its trading partners, and they may well retaliate.

LEWIS: Restrictions apply to money as well as goods. Capital restrictions limit the flow of financial capital across borders. A country can ban foreigners from investing at home, ban or tax the income its own citizens earn on foreign investments, bar foreign investment in certain industries, or restrict foreign companies from sending their local earnings back home, which is called repatriation.

HEART: Economists generally see capital restrictions as a drag on economic welfare. Even so, some developing countries have leaned on them for short term protection. Think of a dam on a river. In a boom, it holds back a flood of foreign money pouring in. In a panic, it stops the reservoir from draining all at once. The catch is that a country that keeps the gates shut too long can find itself cut off from international capital markets, and that long run cost may outweigh the short run protection.

LEWIS: Last, trading blocs, also called regional trading agreements. Each one is a club whose members cut the barriers between themselves. Members gain as each country leans further into what it makes at the lowest relative cost, which is comparative advantage again, and as firms across the club compete harder. Some firms and workers lose out, and some of those workers have to retrain for new jobs. Taken together, economic welfare rises.

HEART: With one warning. Suppose a country buys tractors from the cheapest maker in the world, which sits outside the bloc. After the country joins, a member's tractors come in tariff free, while the outsider's still face the tariff. Buyers switch to the member's tractors, even though they cost more to make. The country is now spending more real resources per tractor than before, and its government has lost the tariff revenue.

LEWIS: That switch is called trade diversion. The good case, where costly home production is replaced by cheaper goods from a partner, is called trade creation. A bloc improves welfare when trade creation outweighs trade diversion, and the more a bloc raises barriers against outsiders, the bigger the risk that it does not.

HEART: Now the ladder. There are five levels of integration, and each one keeps everything from the level below and adds one feature.

LEWIS: A free trade area removes all barriers to trade in goods and services among its members. Each member still sets its own trade policy toward the rest of the world.

HEART: A customs union adds a common set of trade restrictions toward nonmembers, such as one common external tariff. A historical case is the customs union the German states formed in the eighteen thirties, decades before Germany became a single country.

LEWIS: A common market adds free movement of the factors of production, labor and capital, among members. Workers can take jobs anywhere in the bloc, and investment can flow anywhere in it.

HEART: An economic union adds common institutions and a shared economic policy for the whole bloc.

LEWIS: And a monetary union adds a single currency. That removes the cost of exchanging money and the risk of exchange rate swings on trade across borders. The price is that each member gives up its own monetary policy. It cannot set its own interest rate, or let its currency weaken when its economy struggles.

HEART: For a picture of the top rung, think of the fifty American states. Goods cross state lines with no tariffs, there is one tariff schedule at the national border, people and capital move freely, there are shared institutions and a shared economic policy, and there is one currency. That is the whole ladder inside a single country.

LEWIS: To place a bloc on the ladder, listen for the highest feature mentioned. A common external tariff means at least a customs union. Free movement of workers and capital means at least a common market. Shared institutions and policy mean at least an economic union. A shared currency means a monetary union.

HEART: And notice the link back to the first module. Free trade areas, common markets, and economic and monetary unions are exactly the cooperative economic tools of geopolitics. Countries bound together by trade have more to lose from conflict.

LEWIS: The takeaway. Trade pays because of comparative advantage, and the gains outweigh the losses overall even though specific groups lose. Tariffs, quotas and voluntary export restraints raise prices, help producers, hurt consumers by more, and reduce welfare. The one exception is a tariff or quota set by a large importer that pushes the world price down. Who keeps the price gap decides how big the loss is. And trading blocs climb from a free trade area to a monetary union, one feature at a time.

## Recall check

LEWIS Q: A large country trades heavily with the whole world, but it relies on its size to set terms rather than entering reciprocal agreements as an equal. Which archetype fits?

HEART: Answer. Hegemony. Here is why. On the two axes, this country is globalized, because it is deeply involved in the world economy. It is noncooperative, because its scale lets it influence others without needing to cooperate. The tempting wrong answer is autarky, but an autarkic country turns inward and tries to produce what it needs at home.

HEART Q: A risk is widely known, has been building for years and has no particular date attached. Is it event risk, exogenous risk or thematic risk?

LEWIS: Answer. Thematic risk. Here is why. Thematic risk covers known factors whose effects play out over long periods. Event risk is also known, but it is pinned to a date, like an election. Exogenous risk is the surprise nobody anticipated, so known but with no date means thematic.

LEWIS Q: A black swan event hits markets. How fast does its effect show up, and does a long horizon investor usually need to react?

HEART: Answer. It is high velocity, and a long horizon investor usually does not need to react. Here is why. A black swan is a low likelihood exogenous event with large short term effects, so prices move quickly. For an investor holding for many years, a short term shock is a small part of the overall result. An investor with a short horizon may have to act, because the short term is the whole horizon.

HEART Q: A country cannot pay for what it buys abroad, and its currency is under heavy pressure. Which international organization is designed to help, and which one is the tempting wrong answer?

LEWIS: Answer. The I M F, and the tempting wrong answer is the World Bank. Here is why. The I M F's purposes include stable exchange rates and making funds available, with safeguards, to members in balance of payments trouble. The World Bank finances long term development and poverty reduction, such as schools, health care and infrastructure. The W T O handles trade rules and disputes, not financing.

LEWIS Q: Tension rises between two countries, but nothing has changed yet in any company's earnings. Why can stock prices in the region still fall?

HEART: Answer. Because the risk premium rises. Here is why. Investors demand a higher return to hold assets exposed to greater geopolitical risk. A higher required return means future cash flows are worth less today, so prices fall even before earnings change. When the tension eases, the premium shrinks and prices can recover.

HEART Q: A country is more productive than its neighbor at making both computers and furniture. Should it make both and buy neither from abroad?

LEWIS: Answer. No, it should specialize where its relative cost is lowest and import the other good. Here is why. Being more productive at everything is absolute advantage, and trade does not follow it. What matters is opportunity cost, how much of one good you give up to make the other. Each country gains by making the good where it gives up less, and total output rises for both.

LEWIS Q: Of all the reasons governments give for restricting trade, which two have some support from economists?

HEART: Answer. The infant industry argument and national security. Here is why. A new industry may need temporary protection to reach a competitive scale and learn to produce efficiently. And a country may want goods it would need in a conflict made at home, even if foreign ones are cheaper. Protecting jobs and protecting industries get little support, because other jobs are created and consumers pay higher prices.

HEART Q: Why does a voluntary export restraint cost the importing country more than a tariff that cuts imports by the same amount?

LEWIS: Answer. Because the quota rent goes to foreign exporters instead of the importing government. Here is why. Both raise the domestic price by the same amount and create the same deadweight loss. Under the tariff, the government collects the gap between the domestic and world price on each imported unit. Under the restraint, the foreign exporters keep that gap, so the importing country loses it on top of the deadweight loss.

LEWIS Q: A small country starts paying its exporters a subsidy on each unit sold abroad. What happens to the price at home, and does the country gain?

HEART: Answer. The domestic price rises to the world price plus the subsidy, and the country as a whole loses. Here is why. Exporters can earn the world price plus the subsidy abroad, so they will not sell at home for less. Producers gain, but consumers pay more and taxpayers fund the subsidy, and together those losses outweigh the producers' gain.

HEART Q: Under what condition can a tariff raise a country's national welfare?

LEWIS: Answer. When the country is a large importer whose tariff pushes down the world price. Here is why. A small country cannot move the world price, so its consumers bear the full cost and the deadweight loss is a pure loss. A large importer that buys less can drive the world price down, so it pays less for the goods it still buys from abroad. If that gain outweighs the deadweight loss, national welfare rises, at the expense of its trading partners.

LEWIS Q: After joining a trading bloc, a country stops buying from the cheapest supplier in the world, which is outside the bloc, and buys from a pricier member instead. What is this called, and why does it hurt?

HEART: Answer. Trade diversion, and it hurts because the country now spends more real resources to get the same good. Here is why. The member wins the business only because it no longer faces the tariff that the outsider still pays. Buyers see a lower price, but the good costs more to produce, and the government gives up the tariff revenue it collected before. A bloc helps only when trade creation outweighs trade diversion.

HEART Q: Countries remove trade barriers among themselves, adopt a common external tariff, and let workers and capital move freely, but each keeps its own currency and its own economic policy. Which level of integration is this?

LEWIS: Answer. A common market. Here is why. Removing internal barriers makes it a free trade area, and the common external tariff makes it at least a customs union. Free movement of labor and capital takes it to a common market. It stops short of an economic union, which needs shared institutions and policy, and of a monetary union, which needs a single currency.

## Key takeaways

HEART: First, a few memory hooks.

LEWIS: For the archetypes: autarky stays home and goes it alone. Hegemony is open to the world but plays by its own rules. Both cooperative boxes have lateral in the name. Bilateral is two at a time and nationalist, multilateral is many at once and globalized.

HEART: For geopolitical risk: event risk has a date, exogenous risk has no warning, and thematic risk is known but slow. Judge each one by how likely, how big and how fast, which is likelihood, impact and velocity. It reaches prices through the risk premium.

LEWIS: For the tools: security, goods and money. For the organizations: the I M F handles money between countries and lends in a payments crisis, the World Bank builds for the long term to fight poverty, and the W T O writes and referees the rules of trade.

HEART: For trade: follow the lower opportunity cost, not the bigger muscle.

LEWIS: For barriers on goods coming in, five moves in the importing country: imports down, price up, consumer surplus down, home production up, producer surplus up. Then follow the price gap on each imported unit. A tariff or a sold license hands it to the government. A free license or a voluntary export restraint hands it to foreign exporters, the costliest case. The one way a barrier can raise welfare is a large importer pushing down the world price.

HEART: For the export subsidy: in a small country, the home price climbs to the world price plus the subsidy. A large exporter also drags the world price down, handing foreign buyers part of the subsidy.

LEWIS: For the bloc ladder: Friends Can Cooperate Even More. Free trade area, customs union, common market, economic union, monetary union. Each rung adds one thing: no internal barriers, a common outside tariff, free movement of labor and capital, shared institutions and policy, and finally one currency.

HEART: Now, how you would say the big ideas to a senior leader.

LEWIS: On geopolitics: countries range from going it alone to working with everyone, and from closed to open. Where a country sits, and which way it is drifting, tells us which risks to watch. The jargon is cooperation versus competition, globalization versus nationalism, and the four archetypes.

HEART: On risk: for each geopolitical threat, we ask how likely it is, how much damage it could do and how fast it would hit prices, and we watch a few early warning signals. When tension rises, investors want a higher return to hold that region's assets, so prices fall. The jargon is likelihood, impact, velocity, signposts and the risk premium.

LEWIS: On trade: trade makes a country richer overall by letting it specialize, but the losses land on specific workers and communities. The jargon is comparative advantage and the gains from trade.

HEART: On tariffs: protection helps the industry it shields and may raise some revenue, but buyers lose more than that, so the country ends up poorer, unless it is big enough to push world prices down. The jargon is consumer and producer surplus, deadweight loss, quota rent and the large country exception.

LEWIS: And on trade deals: a deal pays off when it replaces costly home production with cheaper goods from partners, and it costs us when it merely steers purchases from a cheap outsider to a pricier partner. The jargon is trade creation and trade diversion.
