LEWIS: Reading 20 covers the legal forms a business can take, what sets a corporation apart, and how public and private companies differ, including the routes on and off a stock exchange. Reading 21 and Reading 22 cover what lenders, shareholders and other stakeholders each want, the environmental, social and governance factors investors weigh, and the governance tools that keep conflicts between these groups in check.

## Module 20.1, Features of Corporate Issuers

HEART: Picture a woman who builds custom bicycles in her garage and wants to grow. Every way she could set up the business answers the same five questions. Is the business a legal person apart from her? Who runs it? If it fails owing money, can creditors come after her own savings? How are the profits taxed? And how easily can it raise more money to grow and spread the risk?

LEWIS: Those are the five features used to compare organizational forms: legal identity, the relationship between owners and the people running the business, owner liability, tax treatment, and access to capital.

HEART: The simplest form is the sole proprietorship. One person owns the business and runs it. Legally the business is just an extension of her, so she has unlimited liability. If a customer wins a lawsuit or the bank calls in a loan, her house and savings are on the line. She keeps all the profit, bears all the losses, and the profit is taxed as her personal income. And the business can only borrow as much as a lender will lend to her personally, which is why sole proprietorships tend to stay small.

LEWIS: Next, she teams up with a skilled welder, and they form a general partnership. A partnership agreement sets out each partner's duties and each one's share of the profits or losses. Each general partner has unlimited liability for the business's debts, and the profit allocated to each partner is taxed as that partner's personal income.

HEART: The agreement does not even have to be written. It can be spoken, or simply implied by the way the partners behave. Two people who split the work and the profits can be partners in the eyes of the law without ever signing anything.

LEWIS: Now the business needs outside money, from people who want to invest but not to work in the shop or put their homes at risk. That is the job of the limited partnership, which has two levels of partner. One or more general partners run the business and keep unlimited liability. Alongside them sit limited partners, who can lose only what they invested.

HEART: Limited partners are passengers, not drivers. Their share of the profits is sized to what they invested, and they normally get no vote on who the general partners are. Because the general partners run the business, the partnership agreement usually gives them a larger cut of the profits for the money they put in. Profits are still taxed once, as each partner's own income. Many investment funds are set up this way, with the fund manager as general partner and the investors as limited partners.

LEWIS: One variation. Some places also allow a limited liability partnership, which needs no general partner, so every partner's liability is limited. In the United States you mostly see this form at professional firms, such as law and accounting practices, and the rules on who may form one vary from state to state.

HEART: That brings us to the form that dominates large business, the corporation, also called a limited company. Its defining feature is that a corporation is a legal entity in its own right, distinct from its owners and its managers. It is created when the founders file articles of incorporation with the government body that registers companies. From then on the law treats it much like a person. It can hire staff, sign a lease, take out a loan and own a factory, all in its own name.

LEWIS: And because its debts are its own, every shareholder has limited liability. If the company goes bankrupt and the shares become worthless, an owner can lose the entire investment, but nothing beyond it. If owning a few shares in an airline made you liable for its debts, nobody would buy a share of a company they could not watch every day. Limited liability is what lets millions of strangers become owners.

HEART: Which brings in the next feature, the split between owners and managers. Shareholders do not direct daily operations. They vote to elect a board of directors, and the board hires the senior managers who run the company. In principle, the directors and the executives they appoint all work for the shareholders. That chain, owners to board to managers, is where every governance question in Module 22.1 begins.

LEWIS: Put those features together and you get the corporation's great advantage, access to capital. It can sell shares to many investors and borrow on its own credit, so no other form can raise as much ownership money, called equity, or borrowed money, called debt. That is why most big businesses are corporations. And once its shares trade on an exchange, owners can sell easily.

HEART: A corporation may pay out its profits to shareholders, but it does not have to. The board decides whether to declare a dividend and how much, and the rest can be reinvested in the business.

LEWIS: Now the drawback, and it depends on the country. In many places a corporation faces double taxation. The government taxes the company on its earnings, and then taxes the dividends again as personal income when shareholders receive them.

HEART: The way to measure the damage is the effective tax rate, the total tax paid at both levels divided by the company's pretax earnings. And the key direction is this. The second layer only bites on the part of the profit that is paid out. So a company that pays out a smaller share of its profits and reinvests the rest has a lower effective tax rate than one that pays out everything.

LEWIS: So the trap is assuming double taxation hits every corporate dollar equally. The other three forms avoid the problem, because their profits pass through to the owners and are taxed once, as personal income. And of the four forms, only the corporation is a legal entity apart from its owners, and only the corporation has virtually unlimited access to capital.

HEART: Within corporations there is one more split. A private limited company has a limited number of shareholders and restrictions on transferring the shares. A public limited company sells shares to the public, and most public limited companies are listed, meaning their shares trade on a stock exchange.

LEWIS: An exchange runs on published rules, and the price and size of every trade is public, which is called price and volume transparency. The owners can be almost anyone, from governments and charities to other companies and ordinary savers.

HEART: Now a term worth knowing, free float. Shares held by insiders such as founders and executives, by strategic investors such as a business partner, or by sponsors who backed the company, tend to stay put. The shares that actually trade make up the free float, usually stated as a percentage of all the shares outstanding. A listed company whose founding family holds most of the stock may have only a modest free float.

LEWIS: Listing comes with homework. A public company must follow its regulator's compliance and reporting rules, filing financial reports every quarter or every year, depending on the market, and announcing any material change in its business or ownership.

HEART: Private companies sit on the other side of all that. With no exchange quoting their shares, nobody can look up what a stake is worth, and finding a buyer is hard. Most investors cash out only when the company lists or is sold. In return, private companies face lighter regulation, reveal less, and with a small circle of owners, can plan for the long haul.

LEWIS: Private companies raise equity through private placements, selling securities directly to selected buyers, usually limited to accredited investors such as corporations, institutions and wealthy individuals.

HEART: There are three ways for a private company to go public. The best known is the initial public offering, the IPO. The company has to meet the exchange's listing standards, and it usually hires an investment bank to underwrite the issue, meaning the bank typically buys the new shares at an agreed price and resells them, carrying the risk of finding buyers. Because the shares are newly created, an IPO raises fresh capital. After that, investors trade with each other on the exchange, and the company is not a party to those trades.

LEWIS: The second way is a direct listing. The exchange simply admits the shares that already exist. No new shares are created, so the company raises no new capital. What it gains is speed, and it skips the underwriter.

HEART: The third way is to be acquired by a company that is already public, and the special case to know is the special purpose acquisition company, the SPAC. A SPAC is a shell company with no business of its own. It raises cash in its own IPO, parks it in a trust, and has a deadline to buy a private company, or the cash goes back to its investors.

LEWIS: The investors in that IPO do not know what the SPAC will buy, because the target does not have to be identified in advance. They are handing over a blank check, which is why SPACs are called blank check companies. When the SPAC buys the private company, that company becomes public.

HEART: Here is the trap. If a question says the company raised no new money and used no underwriter, that is a direct listing, not an IPO.

LEWIS: The trip can also run the other way. Sometimes a public company is underperforming, and a buyer believes it could be restructured more easily away from public markets. In a take private deal, the buyer, often a private equity firm, buys every outstanding share, and the stock is delisted, meaning removed from the exchange. Lighter regulation and lower costs are a big part of the appeal. When the purchase is financed mostly with borrowed money, it is called a leveraged buyout, an LBO.

HEART: The takeaway. From sole proprietor to partnership to corporation, owners move from unlimited toward limited liability, and access to capital opens up with the corporation. The corporation pays for that with the split between owners and managers and, in many countries, double taxation of the profits it pays out.

## Module 21.1, Stakeholders and ESG Factors

LEWIS: Think about a house bought with a mortgage. The bank is owed a set amount on set dates, no more and no less. The homeowner owns whatever is left after the mortgage is paid. If the house soars in value, all of the gain goes to the owner. If it collapses, the owner's stake is wiped out first.

HEART: A company works the same way. Its lenders, or debtholders, are owed set interest and principal under an enforceable contract, which is called a contractual claim. Its owners, the equity holders, get whatever net assets remain after everyone else is paid, a residual claim. Lenders stand at the front of the line. Being first and promised a fixed amount makes debt safer, so lenders accept a lower return, and debt becomes the cheaper source of money for the company.

LEWIS: Now the payoff shapes, which explain almost every conflict in this episode. In the worst case, the two groups look alike. If the company fails, either one can lose everything it put in, but never more than that. The sharpest difference is the best case. A lender's best outcome is simply being paid what was promised, while the owners' upside has no theoretical ceiling.

HEART: Here is a clean way to hold it. The value of the company equals the value of its debt plus the value of its equity. When the company is worth more than its debt, every extra dollar of company value goes to the equity, and the debt's value holds steady. But when the company is worth less than its debt, the equity is worth zero, and the debt is worth less than promised and moves up and down with the value of the company. So debt has downside exposure, but no upside.

LEWIS: That asymmetry shows up in returns. Picture two identical coffee chains. One is funded entirely by its owners, the other mostly by borrowing. In a strong year, the borrower's owners earn a higher return on equity, or ROE. The shops paid for with borrowed money earn more than the interest they cost, and that surplus goes to a smaller pool of owners' money. That works only while the return on the assets beats the interest rate on the debt.

HEART: Now picture a weak year. Sales fall, but the interest still comes due in full. The borrowing chain's return on equity falls much faster, and if sales fall far enough, the lender may not get its full interest either. Leverage magnifies the owners' results in both directions, while the lender never gets more than the promised interest.

LEWIS: So the two groups want different things. A lender mainly wants to be repaid and cares far less about growth. Owners will often take more risk for faster growth, and the classic move is borrowing more. Extra debt can lift the return on equity, and unlike selling new shares, it avoids dilution, so existing owners keep the same percentage of the company.

HEART: But existing lenders may oppose it. More debt raises the chance of default, and a lender's expected return does not rise to match, because its upside was capped from the start. So lenders protect themselves with covenants, provisions in the debt agreement that limit what the borrower can do. Common ones set a maximum level of leverage or a minimum interest coverage ratio, which measures how comfortably earnings cover interest payments. Covenants come back in detail in Fixed Income. And here is the trap. A tempting answer says shareholders face unlimited losses. They do not. Limited liability caps their loss at what they invested, just like a lender's.

LEWIS: Now widen the lens. Shareholder theory says governance serves the owners, and the target is the highest possible market value for the common stock. So the conflict that matters most is between owners and the managers they hired.

HEART: Stakeholder theory casts a wider net. Lenders, employees, suppliers, customers and governments all have something riding on the company, so governance must weigh conflicts among all of them and balance their interests.

LEWIS: A stakeholder is any group with a vested interest in how the company performs. Take them one at a time, starting with shareholders. Besides the residual claim, they have the vote. They elect the board and decide other major questions, which is how owners control a company they do not run. They want profits and growth that push up their share value.

HEART: Lenders come in two kinds. Public debtholders are mainly bondholders. Private debtholders, such as banks, lend directly through loans, credit lines and leases. A private lender can often get monthly figures and forecasts straight from management, which narrows information asymmetry, the gap between what insiders and outsiders know. That is why small and medium sized businesses, often too small to sell bonds, lean so heavily on private lenders.

LEWIS: A commercial lending team at a bank or credit union works this way every day, asking borrowers for regular financial updates and tailoring the terms of each loan. A bondholder gets no such access. It sees only what the company publishes and has almost no say in running it. A private lender that also owns shares in the borrower thinks partly like an owner and may be more flexible about loosening covenants. Covenants protect both kinds of lenders, to different degrees.

HEART: Next, the board of directors, the shareholders' guardians. It picks, pays and can remove the senior managers, sets strategic direction, and watches financial results and the business more broadly. Boards mix two kinds of directors. Inside directors include senior executives and founders. Independent directors have no material relationship with the company. Insiders know the business best, but their interests can clash with the owners', so independent directors may protect shareholders better.

LEWIS: Board design varies by country. American and British companies use a one tier board, with executives and independent directors side by side. Major exchanges usually require independent directors to fill most seats, and some ask for a range of backgrounds and skills. Many continental European companies, Germany being the classic case, use two tiers. A supervisory board with no executives on it oversees a separate management board of executives.

HEART: Directors serve fixed terms, commonly a year. A staggered board, also called a classified board, splits the seats into groups, often three, and only one group is elected each year. So unhappy shareholders cannot replace the whole board at one meeting. Companies that keep staggered boards say they bring stability and keep a long term strategy on course.

LEWIS: Senior managers' pay, also called remuneration, usually has three parts: a salary, a bonus tied to company performance, and perks, such as a car and driver or club memberships. They want to keep their jobs and maximize their total pay, and the bonus ties their fortunes to the company's success.

HEART: The rest of the staff are the company's human capital, the labor and skills it runs on. A nurse or a delivery driver cares about pay, promotion, training, working conditions and the employer's survival and success. Some employees own shares through employee stock plans, meant to align them with shareholders, and in some industries workers bargain through unions.

LEWIS: Suppliers want a lasting relationship, profitable trade with the company, and a company that grows and stays stable. There is a second angle. A supplier that ships goods today and gets paid in a month is really a short term lender, so suppliers care about the company's solvency and financial strength.

HEART: Customers want a product that works at a fair price, and often help after they buy. Losing their goodwill can sink a company, and more customers now weigh how it treats the environment and society. Finally, governments count on companies for taxes, jobs, growth and social welfare, and regulators want them to obey the law.

LEWIS: That brings us to ESG, short for environmental, social and governance factors. Some lenders and shareholders, especially those with a stakeholder view, analyze them, for three main reasons.

HEART: First, many governments pursue climate and social goals through regulation, though the pace and direction vary by country. Second, these factors can hit results materially, through lost customer goodwill, fines and court judgments, and in the case of poor governance, managers serving themselves at shareholders' expense. Third, many younger investors manage their wealth with ESG in mind.

LEWIS: A key idea underneath all of this is the negative externality, a cost a company's actions impose on others, which the company and its investors do not fully bear. Picture a factory upstream that discharges waste into a river. The town downstream pays to clean its water, and the factory's own accounts show nothing.

HEART: Regulation and pressure from stakeholders can push those costs back onto the companies that cause them. Sometimes the cost shows up explicitly in the financial statements, as a fine or a cleanup liability. Sometimes it shows up implicitly, in lost customers or a damaged reputation.

LEWIS: Now the three groups of factors. Environmental factors include a company's contribution to climate change, air and water pollution, deforestation, energy efficiency, waste management and water scarcity. When these factors are material, they can substantially affect a company's operations or even its business model. Industries that use a lot of natural resources have a direct impact on the environment, while others have an indirect one.

HEART: Climate risk comes in two flavors. Physical risk is direct harm to assets or operations, for example a warehouse in a flood zone as severe storms become more frequent. Transition risk comes from the shift to a lower carbon economy, when new regulations or changing consumer choices force a switch from high carbon to low carbon activities.

LEWIS: Transition risk can produce stranded assets, assets that become unviable because of such changes. Picture a fleet of older diesel delivery vans, perfectly legal today, that a city's planned emissions rules would bar from its streets in a few years. The vans still run, but their economic life has been cut short.

HEART: Weak safety policies or weak governance also raise the chance of a sudden disaster, such as a chemical spill, with costs from penalties, cleanup, litigation and lost reputation.

LEWIS: Social factors cover how a company treats people. They include protecting customer privacy and information security, customer satisfaction, employee engagement, diversity and inclusion, labor relations and community relations.

HEART: And reducing social risk can cut costs, through more productive employees, lower turnover, more loyal customers and fewer lawsuits. A retailer that leaks its customers' card data, by contrast, faces legal costs, lost customers and a damaged reputation all at once.

LEWIS: Governance factors cover how the company itself is run. They include the composition of the board, the internal audit function and the committee that oversees it, executive pay, bribery and corruption, political contributions and lobbying. A good governance system has enough checks to keep managers acting ethically, lawfully and in shareholders' interests.

HEART: So an analyst identifies the ESG risks a company is exposed to, measures them, and judges how they could affect its future cash flows. And the exposure differs by security. Shareholders take the hardest hit from a bad ESG event, because their residual claim is the first to absorb a loss in value. Lenders feel much less, unless the damage is big enough to put repayment in doubt.

LEWIS: Even among lenders, exposure differs. Some ESG risks arrive slowly, years down the road. A bond that matures in two years will most likely be repaid before a slow moving risk arrives. A bond that matures in fifteen years may still be outstanding when it does. So longer maturity debt can carry more ESG exposure than short term debt.

HEART: The takeaway. Lenders hold a fixed, senior claim with no upside, and shareholders hold the residual claim with unlimited upside, though both can lose only what they invested. And equity is the most exposed to ESG risks, and long term debt more than short term debt.

## Module 22.1, Corporate Governance

LEWIS: You hire a contractor to remodel your kitchen and agree to pay by the hour. You want a good kitchen, quickly and at a sensible cost. The contractor wants a good kitchen too, but every extra hour on the job is extra income. Your interests overlap, but not completely.

HEART: That is a principal agent relationship. You are the principal, and the contractor is the agent, hired to do a job on your behalf. The agent has goals of their own, and wherever those goals drift from yours, you have a principal agent conflict.

LEWIS: The costs of that conflict are called agency costs. Some are direct, such as paying an inspector to check the contractor's work, the cost of monitoring the agent. Others are indirect, such as the opportunity cost of business lost because of the conflict.

HEART: In a corporation, shareholders are the principals, the owners. The managers and the directors on the board are their agents. And the most basic conflict is about risk. A manager's income, and often a whole career, is tied to one company. Shareholders can diversify cheaply across many companies, so a single failure hurts them less. As a result, managers and directors may choose a lower level of business risk than shareholders would prefer.

LEWIS: Conflicts also arise when inside directors favor management over shareholders, or when directors favor one group of shareholders over another. And much of this is made worse by information asymmetry. Managers know more about how the company works and where it is headed than shareholders do, which makes it harder for shareholders and nonexecutive directors to judge whether managers are serving them. The gap is widest in three cases: a sprawling group spanning many businesses and countries, a company whose products outsiders struggle to understand, and a company with low institutional ownership and a small free float, so few professional investors are watching and little stock trades.

HEART: Now five common ways the conflict between shareholders and managers plays out. The first is insufficient effort. A manager who coasts sizes up investments and their risks carelessly, and the company's total costs rise.

LEWIS: The second is a distorted appetite for risk, and it depends on how managers are paid. A stock option gives a manager the gain when the share price rises above a set level, but if the price ends below that level, the option simply expires worthless. The manager shares in the upside without sharing the losses, so options can tempt managers to push risk higher.

HEART: The opposite problem comes with pay that is mostly cash. A manager with little stake in the share price has little reason to take risks, even good ones, and may be too cautious.

LEWIS: The third is empire building. When pay is tied to the size of the company, managers have a reason to make it bigger, even through acquisitions that add size but no value.

HEART: The fourth is entrenchment, a manager acting mainly to keep the job: avoiding bold bets, copying rivals so nobody can fault them for being different, or backing money losing ventures that only they know how to run, so they are hard to replace. Directors entrench too, by nodding along with management instead of challenging it.

LEWIS: The fifth is self dealing, exploiting company resources for personal benefit, such as perks far beyond what the job needs, or steering company business toward the manager's own interests.

HEART: Next, conflicts between groups of shareholders. A shareholder or group holding most of the votes is called a controlling shareholder, and it can push the company in ways that hurt the minority shareholders.

LEWIS: Suppose the family controlling a regional grocery chain has nearly all its wealth in it. To feel safer, the family pushes the chain to buy hotels. Outside shareholders already own dozens of companies, so this spreads no risk for them. They just see grocery profits poured into an unfamiliar business to calm the family's nerves.

HEART: Control can also come from the share structure itself. Some companies have a dual class structure, with classes of common stock carrying different voting power. A class held by the founders might carry several votes per share, while the class sold to the public carries one. That can give the founders effective control even when their claim on the company's earnings and assets is less than half.

LEWIS: Supporters argue it lets founders pursue a long term plan without pressure from short term investors. Critics favor one vote per share, because unequal votes let one group of shareholders advance its interests at the expense of the others. CFA Institute, which sets this exam, takes that view and opposes dual class structures.

HEART: The third conflict, creditors against shareholders, picks up where Module 21.1 left off. Because a lender's upside is capped, owners can gain by shifting risk onto lenders. New debt raises the default risk for existing lenders. A bigger dividend sends cash to owners, leaving fewer assets to back the debt. Long term lenders are the most exposed.

LEWIS: Now the response to all these conflicts, corporate governance. Corporate governance is the system of internal controls and procedures by which a company is managed. It sets out the rights, roles and responsibilities of the different groups in and around the company, and its objective is to manage and minimize conflicts of interest among stakeholders.

HEART: Related to it is stakeholder management, which rests on understanding what each group wants and communicating with it effectively. For shareholders, corporate law sets many standard practices, broadly similar from country to country.

LEWIS: The foundation is reporting. Stakeholders learn about a company from its annual reports, proxy statements and public notices, which cover financial performance and standing, related party transactions, executive pay and the governance structure. A related party transaction is a deal between the company and someone connected to it, such as a director, an executive or a major shareholder.

HEART: Private companies have lighter reporting rules and usually share information privately with their own investors. Either way, more disclosure means less information asymmetry, so stakeholders can judge whether the company serves their interests.

LEWIS: Now the shareholder mechanisms. After the fiscal year ends, a corporation usually holds an annual general meeting, the owners' yearly check in. Management hands over the audited financial statements, reviews the year and its big decisions, and answers questions. Corporate law fixes the timing and notice, and generally any shareholder may attend, speak and vote.

HEART: But most shareholders never go. They vote by proxy, handing their vote to someone who will attend, typically a director, a manager or their own investment adviser. Like a signed permission slip, it can say exactly how to vote on each item or leave the choice to the holder. Proxy voting is the primary shareholder mechanism.

LEWIS: Ordinary resolutions, such as approving the auditor and electing directors, need a simple majority of the votes cast. Other matters go to an extraordinary general meeting, which can be called whenever a matter requires a shareholder vote. Examples include amending the company's bylaws, approving a merger or takeover, a special board election proposed by shareholders, or liquidating the company.

HEART: Next, shareholder activism. An activist builds a sizable stake and leans on the company for changes it thinks will lift the value, such as selling a weak division. Hedge funds do this more and more. Tactics include seeking board seats, putting resolutions to a vote, suing, and campaigning to the other shareholders or the public.

LEWIS: Two tactics aim at control. In a proxy contest, a group asks other shareholders for their proxies, so it can vote those shares for its own proposals or its own candidates for the board. In a tender offer, a buyer offers to purchase enough shares directly from shareholders to gain control. The simplest way to hold the difference: a proxy contest asks for your vote, a tender offer asks for your shares.

HEART: Shareholders can vote out the directors, and through the board, the senior managers, if they think performance is poor. And the threat of a hostile takeover, one that management does not support, gives managers and boards a reason to stay aligned with shareholders.

LEWIS: But the threat can also push management to protect itself with takeover defenses. A staggered board means a bidder cannot replace the whole board in a single election. A poison pill lets existing shareholders other than the bidder buy additional shares at a low price once the bidder builds a large stake, which dilutes the bidder and makes the takeover far more expensive.

HEART: Now the creditor mechanisms. When a company issues a bond, the bondholders' rights and the company's obligations are set out in a legal document called the bond indenture. It typically includes covenants, some requiring the company to take certain actions, such as delivering financial statements, and some restricting it, such as limits on extra borrowing or on dividends.

LEWIS: A bond can also be backed by collateral, a specific asset pledged to the bondholders, which they can claim if the company defaults. And a financial institution may act as trustee, monitoring whether the company is complying with its covenants.

HEART: When an issuer hits financial distress, bondholders may band together in creditor committees, and some countries require one once a company files for bankruptcy. Earlier, while the company is struggling to pay, a few bond investors may form an ad hoc committee. It speaks only for its members, but their interests usually match the other bondholders'.

LEWIS: Next, the board itself, elected by shareholders to act for them. Its duties fall into three groups. People: choosing, paying and judging senior managers, and planning the chief executive's succession. Direction: setting strategy, approving capital structure changes, big acquisitions and large investments, and correcting course when results slip. Control: overseeing internal controls and risk management, and ensuring the quality of financial reporting and internal audit.

HEART: Boards do much of that work through committees of members with the right expertise. The committees report to the full board, which keeps overall responsibility. Three come up again and again.

LEWIS: The audit committee watches the numbers and the people who check them. It oversees financial reporting and how accounting policies are applied. It monitors the effectiveness of internal controls and the internal audit function. It recommends the independent external auditor and that auditor's pay. And it proposes remedies based on what the internal and external audits find.

HEART: The nominating committee, often called the governance committee, looks after the board itself and the rules. It oversees the company's corporate governance code, including board elections, and sets the policies for nominating board candidates. It carries out the code of ethics and the policies on conflicts of interest. And it monitors changes in laws and regulations and makes sure the company complies with them and with its own governance policies.

LEWIS: The compensation committee, also called the remuneration committee, recommends to the board how much directors and senior managers are paid, and in what form. It may also oversee employee benefit plans and evaluate senior managers. Because managers should never set or judge their own pay, this committee should be made up only of independent directors, and in many countries that is required.

HEART: Here is the trap with committees. The code of ethics sounds like an audit job, but it belongs to the nominating or governance committee.

LEWIS: Other committees are specific to an industry. In financial services, a risk committee advises the board on risk policy and risk tolerance and oversees risk management across the whole enterprise. At a bank, that is the committee that signs off on how much interest rate risk and liquidity risk the institution is willing to carry. In insurance, an investment committee reviews investment and capital management policies.

HEART: The number and size of committees follow the company's size, complexity and line of business. Many regulators insist on an audit committee, and the audit, compensation and governance committees are often staffed entirely by nonexecutive or independent directors.

LEWIS: Now the other stakeholders. Employees rely on labor laws, employment contracts and the right to unionize. Some countries, Germany for one, also give workers seats on large companies' boards, and employee stock ownership plans can help line up their interests with the company's.

HEART: Customers and suppliers manage their relationships mainly through contracts, and increasingly, customers and other stakeholders turn to social media to pressure companies.

LEWIS: Governments write and enforce the rules, often through agencies that regulate whole sectors, such as financial markets, or watch specific issues, such as workplace safety or environmental protection. In some countries, regulators publish corporate governance codes that companies must either adopt or explain publicly why they have not, an approach called comply or explain. In others, governance rules are part of a stock exchange's listing requirements.

HEART: Finally, what is at stake. When governance is weak, audits and board oversight tend to be weak as well, so some stakeholders can gain at the expense of others. Accounting fraud, or simply sloppy bookkeeping, damages performance and value. Unmonitored managers may choose a level of risk that is wrong for the owners, collect incentive pay that serves themselves, or steer related party transactions toward friends or family.

LEWIS: Then come the legal and reputational risks. Sloppy compliance brings regulators and courts down on the company, trampling stakeholders' rights invites lawsuits, and public rule breaking tarnishes its name. Mishandle lenders' rights, and default and bankruptcy can follow. So the risks of poor governance boil down to five: weak control systems, poor decision making, legal risk, reputational risk and default risk.

HEART: The benefits of good governance mirror those risks. Aligned incentives make the company run more efficiently. Strong controls and compliance head off many legal and regulatory problems. Written rules on conflicts of interest and related party deals help results. Treating lenders well lowers default risk, so they charge less to lend. And managers who think like owners tend to deliver better performance and a more valuable company.

LEWIS: The takeaway. An agency conflict arises whenever an agent's interests differ from the principal's, and information asymmetry makes it harder to police. Governance answers with transparency, shareholder votes, creditor contracts, an independent board and its committees, and outside rules.

## Recall check

LEWIS Q: Of the four main business forms, which one is a legal entity apart from its owners, and which owners carry unlimited liability?

HEART: Answer. Only the corporation is a legal entity apart from its owners, and sole proprietors and general partners carry unlimited liability. Here is why. A sole proprietorship and both kinds of partnership are legally extensions of their owners, so the people running them answer personally for the business's debts. Limited partners and shareholders can lose only what they invested.

HEART Q: A corporation raises the share of its profit that it pays out as dividends. What happens to the effective tax rate on that profit?

LEWIS: Answer. It rises. Here is why. The company's earnings are taxed once at the corporate level no matter what. The second layer, the personal tax on dividends, applies only to the part paid out. Pay out more, and more of the profit gets taxed twice, so total tax divided by pretax earnings goes up.

LEWIS Q: A private company has its existing shares listed on an exchange, issues no new shares and hires no underwriter. Which route did it take, and did it raise new capital?

HEART: Answer. A direct listing, and it raised no new capital. Here is why. In a direct listing the exchange simply lists the shares that already exist, so no money flows to the company. An IPO issues new shares, usually through an underwriter, which is how it raises capital.

HEART Q: Why is a SPAC called a blank check company?

LEWIS: Answer. Because its investors hand over money before they know what it will buy. Here is why. A SPAC raises cash in its own IPO and holds it in a trust that must be used for an acquisition within a set time. The target does not have to be identified when the money is raised.

LEWIS Q: A company ends up worth less than the amount it owes its lenders. What is the equity worth, and what happens to the value of the debt?

HEART: Answer. The equity is worth zero, and the debt is worth less than promised and moves with the company's value. Here is why. The value of the company equals its debt plus its equity, and lenders are paid first. Once the company cannot cover the debt in full, nothing is left for the owners, and the lenders are left with whatever value remains.

HEART Q: When does borrowing raise a company's return on equity, and why might existing lenders oppose more borrowing?

LEWIS: Answer. Borrowing helps when the return on assets beats the interest rate on the debt, and lenders oppose it because their risk rises while their return does not. Here is why. Each borrowed dollar earns the return on assets but costs only the interest rate, and the surplus goes to the owners. But a lender's upside is capped at the promised payments, so it bears the extra default risk without any extra expected return.

LEWIS Q: Why is a bank lending privately to a company often better informed than a bondholder?

HEART: Answer. Because the bank can get nonpublic information and negotiate directly with management. Here is why. A private lender can ask for figures and plans that are not public, which reduces information asymmetry. Bondholders rely only on public information and have little or no influence over how the company is run.

HEART Q: A rule taking effect in about ten years will ban the main product a company makes, with no effect on today's cash flows. Which is most exposed: its shares, its bond due in two years, or its bond due in fifteen years?

LEWIS: Answer. The shares, and among the bonds, the fifteen year bond. Here is why. Equity holds the residual claim, so it absorbs any loss in the company's value first and most. The two year bond will most likely be repaid before the ban takes effect, while the fifteen year bond will still be outstanding, so its holders carry more of that delayed risk.

LEWIS Q: One chief executive is paid mostly in stock options, another mostly in cash. Which way might each tilt on risk?

HEART: Answer. The option holder may take too much risk, and the executive paid in cash may take too little. Here is why. An option pays off when the share price rises above a set level and simply expires worthless if it does not, so its holder shares the gains but not the losses. A manager paid mainly in cash gets little from a rising share price but could lose a job if a risky bet fails, so caution wins.

HEART Q: A controlling family with most of its wealth in one company pushes it to buy an unrelated business to spread risk. Why might minority shareholders oppose the purchase?

LEWIS: Answer. Because they can diversify on their own, more cheaply. Here is why. Minority shareholders typically already hold diversified portfolios, so the company spreading its risk does little for them. They see resources spent on a less attractive business to serve the controlling family's own risk concerns.

LEWIS Q: Which board committee recommends the external auditor, and which one oversees the code of ethics?

HEART: Answer. The audit committee recommends the external auditor, and the nominating or governance committee oversees the code of ethics. Here is why. The audit committee owns the numbers: financial reporting, accounting policies, internal controls, and internal and external audit. The nominating or governance committee owns the board and the rules: board elections, the governance code, ethics and conflict of interest policies, and compliance.

HEART Q: What is the difference between a proxy contest and a tender offer, and how does a staggered board slow either one down?

LEWIS: Answer. A proxy contest seeks other shareholders' votes, a tender offer seeks to buy their shares, and a staggered board limits how many seats can change at once. Here is why. Either way, real control means control of the board. With only a fraction of the board up for election each year, even a challenger with the votes needs more than one annual election to win a majority of seats.

## Key takeaways

HEART: First, a few memory hooks.

LEWIS: For business forms, ask five questions. Is it its own legal person? Who runs it? Who pays if it fails? How is the profit taxed? How easily can it raise money? Walk from sole proprietor to partnership to corporation, and liability moves from unlimited toward limited, while access to capital opens up with the corporation.

HEART: For double taxation: pay out more, pay more tax. The effective rate is all the tax at both levels, divided by pretax earnings.

LEWIS: For going public: an IPO sells new shares through an underwriter and raises money. A direct listing just opens the door to trading, with no new money. A SPAC is a pile of cash looking for a private company to buy.

HEART: For lenders and owners: debt plus equity equals the value of the company. Lenders get a fixed, senior claim with no upside. Owners get what is left, with no ceiling. Both can lose only what they put in.

LEWIS: For ESG: E is the planet, S is people, G is how the company is run. Physical risk is the weather, transition risk is new rules and changing tastes. And for exposure, think equity most, long dated debt next, short dated debt least.

HEART: For the five ways managers can let shareholders down, picture managers asleep at the wheel, and spell sleep. Self dealing, low effort, empire building, entrenchment, and pay driven risk, where options push risk up and cash pushes it down.

LEWIS: For board committees: audit has the numbers and the auditors, nominating or governance has the board seats and the rules, and compensation has the pay, staffed only by independent directors.

HEART: For control fights: a proxy contest asks for your vote, a tender offer asks for your shares. A poison pill dilutes the bidder, and a staggered board slows it down.

LEWIS: Now, how you would say the big ideas to a senior leader.

HEART: On legal form: incorporating protects the owners' personal assets and opens the door to large amounts of capital, but in many countries the profits we pay out get taxed twice. The jargon is limited liability, a separate legal entity and double taxation.

LEWIS: On lenders versus owners: our lenders want their money back on time, while our owners want growth, so borrowing more or paying out more shifts risk onto our lenders. The jargon is contractual versus residual claims, financial leverage and covenants.

HEART: On environmental and social risks: some costs we cause today are not on our books yet, but regulation and public pressure can push them there, and our shareholders would feel it first. The jargon is negative externalities, transition risk and stranded assets.

LEWIS: On governance: whenever we hire people to act for us, their interests can drift from ours and they usually know more than we do, so we need clear reporting, real shareholder votes and an independent board to keep everyone pulling the same way. The jargon is the principal agent problem, information asymmetry and corporate governance.

HEART: And on why it pays: companies with strong oversight tend to have fewer nasty surprises, borrow more cheaply and be worth more. The jargon is agency costs, default risk and the cost of debt.
