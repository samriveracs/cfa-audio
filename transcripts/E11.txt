LEWIS: Reading 23 covers the cash conversion cycle, what liquidity means and where it comes from, the three liquidity ratios, and conservative versus aggressive ways to run working capital. Reading 24 covers the four kinds of capital investment, the four step allocation process, net present value, internal rate of return and return on invested capital, and then the cash flow principles, the common pitfalls and real options.

## Module 23.1, Liquidity Measures and Management

HEART: Picture a furniture maker. It buys lumber, spends weeks turning it into tables, ships the tables to stores, and then waits for the stores to pay. Call that whole loop the trip. The cash conversion cycle counts only the stretch of the trip the firm funds with its own cash, from the day it pays the lumber yard to the day the stores pay it.

LEWIS: It has three parts, each measured in days. Days of inventory on hand is how long goods sit before they are sold. Days sales outstanding is how long customers take to pay after a sale. Days payables outstanding is how long the firm itself takes to pay its suppliers. You will also hear them shortened to D O H, D S O and D P O.

HEART: The cycle adds the first two and subtracts the third. Inventory days plus collection days, minus payable days. Why subtract the payables? Because every day the furniture maker waits to pay the lumber yard, the lumber yard is funding part of the trip. The firm only has to finance the gap between paying its suppliers and collecting from its customers.

LEWIS: And that subtraction is the trap. More days of payables sounds like a longer cycle, but it makes the cycle shorter. Pay your suppliers later, and your own cash is tied up for fewer days.

HEART: Shorter is generally better. Every day trimmed from the cycle is a day of operating cash the firm no longer has to finance, so more of its capital is free for other uses. A cycle that is long, or stretching out, can squeeze cash flow and crowd out spending on growth.

LEWIS: But the right length depends on the business. A fine jeweler may hold a necklace for months before it sells, so its cycle is naturally long. A burger chain sells its food within days of buying the beef, gets paid at the counter, and pays its suppliers weeks later, so its cycle can even turn negative, with suppliers funding the business. So compare a firm with its own industry, or with its own past.

HEART: The levers are obvious: carry less inventory, collect from customers faster, or pay suppliers later. Each one has a price. Run the raw materials bin low, and one late shipment halts the production line. Run the finished goods low, and a sudden rush of orders empties the shelves. Tighten credit terms for customers, and some of them buy from a rival instead. Stretch your payables, and you lean harder on your suppliers.

LEWIS: That last lever deserves a closer look, because what you owe a supplier really is a loan. Nobody signs a loan agreement. The credit simply comes bundled with the purchase, which is why it is called implicit credit. A bank loan, with its own paperwork and rate, is explicit credit. Suppliers often sweeten the bundle with terms like a small discount if you pay within a short window, or the full amount by a later due date.

HEART: So skipping the discount is really borrowing from the supplier. You keep the money for the days between the discount deadline and the final due date, and the interest you pay is the discount you gave up.

LEWIS: And that interest is usually dear. The discount looks tiny, but it buys only a few weeks of credit, and a year holds many of those short stretches. Stretch that cost over a full year with compounding, and even a one percent discount for paying twenty days early works out to roughly twenty percent a year. A bigger discount, or a shorter gap between the two dates, makes it dearer still.

HEART: Which gives you the rule. If the firm can borrow from its bank for less than that implied annual rate, it should borrow and take the discount. Two traps when you think it through. The borrowing period is only the gap between the two dates, not the whole time until the bill is due. And the cost is measured against the smaller amount you would have paid early, not the full invoice.

LEWIS: Beyond the cycle, analysts look at the overall level of working capital. Total working capital is current assets minus current liabilities. Many analysts prefer net working capital, which keeps only the operating pieces. It leaves out cash and marketable securities on the asset side, and short term borrowing and debt falling due within the year on the liability side. What remains is mostly inventory and receivables against payables and other operating bills, the same items that drive the cash conversion cycle.

HEART: To compare firms of different sizes, scale working capital to sales. And as with the cycle, compare within an industry, because a grocer and a shipbuilder need very different amounts.

LEWIS: Now liquidity itself, which is really about distance. For an asset, it is the distance to cash. For a liability, it is the distance to being paid off. Marketable securities sit right behind cash, because you can sell them in a day. Receivables come next, and inventory is the least liquid current asset.

HEART: Why does inventory trail receivables? Because it is two steps from cash. A bicycle shop's bikes may still need assembly, and once a cycling club buys a dozen on an invoice, they become a receivable that still has to be collected. A receivable has already done the selling.

LEWIS: For a company, liquidity means having enough cash and liquid assets to meet its short term obligations. Keep it apart from solvency, the long run question of whether the business earns enough cash, year after year, to cover everything it owes. A firm can be solvent and still run short of cash next Friday. To judge how well a firm manages its liquidity, analysts go first to the statement of cash flows.

HEART: So where does liquidity come from? Primary sources are the everyday ones: cash and marketable securities on hand, borrowing from banks, and the cash the business itself generates. Think of a household. The paycheck, the savings account and the credit line on a card.

LEWIS: Secondary sources are what a company reaches for when the primary ones fall short. It can suspend its dividend, delay or cut capital spending, sell assets, issue new shares, or restructure its debt to push the maturities further out. As a last resort it can file for bankruptcy protection, which pauses the payments on its liabilities. In the household, that is selling the car, cancelling the kitchen remodel and calling the lender to stretch out the mortgage.

HEART: Reaching for these has two downsides. They tend to be pricier than the everyday sources, and the market notices. Investors see a suspended dividend or a rushed asset sale and conclude that something is wrong. Here is the trap. Issuing new shares sounds like an ordinary financing move, but when a firm sells shares to plug a cash shortfall, it is tapping a secondary source.

LEWIS: The cycle also moves around. A snowblower maker's cycle stretches every mild winter, when unsold machines sit in the warehouse and days of inventory climb. Seasons and business conditions move every firm's cycle, and a cushion of cash and marketable securities is what absorbs the swings.

HEART: Sizing that cushion is a balancing act. Idle cash is not free, because money parked in an account is money not building the business. But run the cushion too thin, and the firm is pushed onto the expensive secondary sources just when it can least afford them.

LEWIS: One of those expenses has a name, the cost of liquidity. It is how far below fair value you have to price an asset to turn it into cash right now, rather than selling it at a normal pace. Picture selling your house this week instead of over the usual few months. For a company, a Treasury bill can be sold within the hour at almost its full value. A specialized printing press that must be sold by Friday may fetch far less than it is worth. The less liquid the asset, the bigger the discount for speed.

HEART: Two words describe what squeezes liquidity. A drag on liquidity is cash coming in late. Picture a toy maker stuck with last season's unsold toys while a big retail customer stretches out its payments. Days of inventory on hand climb, and so do days sales outstanding. A pull on liquidity is cash going out early. The toy maker's main supplier grows nervous, cuts the credit it offers and asks to be paid sooner, so days payables outstanding fall.

LEWIS: Picture the firm's cash as water in a bathtub. A drag narrows the tap. A pull widens the drain. Either way the level falls, the cash conversion cycle gets longer, and a longer cycle means less liquidity.

HEART: Now the ratios. Each one compares a set of liquid assets with current liabilities, and each is stricter than the one before.

LEWIS: The current ratio is current assets divided by current liabilities. Above one means the firm's current assets are enough to cover its current liabilities.

HEART: The quick ratio throws out inventory, the slowest of the current assets to become cash. It counts only cash, marketable securities and receivables against current liabilities, so it is a stricter test.

LEWIS: The cash ratio drops the receivables as well and keeps only cash and marketable securities. It is the strictest of the three.

HEART: Because each one counts fewer assets against the same current liabilities, the current ratio is always at least as large as the quick ratio, and the quick ratio is always at least as large as the cash ratio. For all three, higher means more liquid.

LEWIS: Here is the trap. A healthy current ratio can hide a problem. Picture an electronics retailer whose stockroom is full of last year's televisions. Its current ratio looks comfortable, because all that inventory counts. Its quick ratio tells the truer story. If the current ratio holds steady while the quick ratio falls, inventory is propping up the current ratio.

HEART: Finally, how a firm runs its working capital. The aim is a balance: earn as much as possible from the business without ever being caught unable to pay a bill. Two tensions sit inside that aim. Holding more short term assets is safer, but those assets earn low returns. And short term borrowing usually costs less than long term debt or equity, but it comes due again and again, and each time the firm needs lenders willing to renew it on decent terms. That danger is rollover risk.

LEWIS: To compare firms of different sizes here, analysts again scale by sales, looking at short term assets and long term assets each as a proportion of sales.

HEART: A conservative firm keeps a thick layer of short term assets compared with its long term assets, and pays for its working capital with long term debt and equity. Think of a homeowner with a thirty year fixed mortgage and a fat emergency fund. Nobody can call the loan in, a market panic does not touch them, and the bills always get paid. The price is that long term money costs more and idle cash earns little, so profits are lower. And long term lenders often attach strings, such as a promise to keep interest coverage above a set floor.

LEWIS: An aggressive approach runs lean on short term assets and funds its working capital with short term debt. That is cheaper, so returns are higher. The danger is the day the money will not roll. If lenders pull back or funding markets seize up, the firm can find itself unable to pay what it owes.

HEART: The moderate approach splits the difference by asking how long each need lasts. The base level of current assets a firm always carries is permanent, so it gets long term capital. The part that swells and shrinks with the seasons gets short term borrowing. Picture a garden center. Its year round stock gets long term money. The spring surge of plants and seed gets a short term loan, paid back once the summer sales come in.

LEWIS: Banks and credit unions face this tradeoff in a sharper form. Their loans run for years, while much of their funding can leave quickly. Leaning on cheap short term money boosts earnings right up until it cannot be rolled over, so they hold a cushion of liquid securities and lock in some longer term funding, even though both cost them some return.

HEART: Last, short term funding sources. Treat them like spare tires. Have more than one, know what each one costs, and sort them out before the blowout. Five factors shape which sources a firm can get. Size, since a corner hardware store cannot borrow the way a national chain can. Creditworthiness, which drives the rate it pays and the strings attached. The legal system, because lenders offer more ways to borrow where the courts protect them well. Regulation, because banks and utilities face rules on where they raise money and how much. And the underlying assets the firm can pledge as collateral.

LEWIS: The takeaway. The cash conversion cycle is inventory days plus collection days minus payable days, and shorter is generally better within an industry. The liquidity ratios get stricter from current to quick to cash. A conservative approach buys safety at the price of returns, and an aggressive approach buys returns at the price of rollover risk.

## Module 24.1, Capital Investments and Project Measures

HEART: A capital investment is spending today on something that pays off over years: a factory, a fleet of trucks, a software system, even a whole company.

LEWIS: They come in four types. First, going concern projects, which keep the existing business running or cut its costs. Replacing a leaking roof is maintenance, and it barely needs analysis. Once the firm decides to keep that operation going, and to keep running it the same way, the roof gets fixed. A cost saving project asks something subtler, such as whether a commercial bakery should swap ovens that still work for newer ones that burn far less gas.

HEART: Two notes on going concern projects. Firms like to pay for long lasting equipment with long term money, the way you would not buy a house on a credit card. That habit is called match funding, and it lowers financing risk because the firm is not forced to refinance halfway through the asset's life. And there is a handy rule of thumb. The yearly depreciation charge is a rough guide to how much a company must reinvest just to stand still, since it reflects how fast the existing assets are wearing out.

LEWIS: Second, regulatory or compliance projects. These are projects the firm is told to do, by a regulator or by its insurer, usually over safety or the environment. Think of a hotel whose insurer insists on a new sprinkler system. These projects bring in little or no revenue, so the real question is which way of complying makes the most sense.

HEART: Third, expansion projects, which grow the business, either by taking what it sells somewhere new or by selling something new to the customers it already has. A regional coffee chain moving into a neighboring state is an example. These need demand forecasts and detailed forecasts of revenue and expenses.

LEWIS: Fourth, other projects, the catch all for bets outside the firm's current lines of business. Think of a furniture maker launching a home design app, which is really a startup tucked inside an established company. Buying a company in an unfamiliar industry belongs here too, with the obvious risk of overpaying. Uncertainty is high, so the analysis has to be detailed.

HEART: Capital allocation is how a firm picks the projects it will fund when their cash flows play out over more than a year. Why does it matter so much? Get a big, long lasting bet wrong and the firm carries the mistake for a decade. The same discipline also shows up in other decisions, such as managing working capital and doing mergers. And above all, this is where management either builds shareholder value or burns it.

LEWIS: The process has four steps. Step one is idea generation, and it is the most important step, because no amount of analysis can rescue a pool of weak ideas. A good idea can come from the boardroom, a plant floor, a sales rep or a customer. Step two is analyzing the proposals, which means forecasting each project's cash flows to judge whether it is worth doing.

HEART: Step three is the firm wide capital budget, where the winners compete with each other. Money and people are limited, cash arrives at different times, and some good projects simply do not fit where the firm is heading. Step four is monitoring the decisions and running a post audit, a report card on last year's forecasts. Where results missed, someone has to explain why. Over time that exposes patterns, like a division whose forecasts are always too rosy, so future forecasts get more honest and the business itself runs better.

LEWIS: Now the measures. Net present value, NPV, is the workhorse. Picture someone offering you a deal. Pay a sum today, and receive a series of payments over the next several years. To judge it, translate each future payment into today's money at the return you require, add those values up, and subtract what you pay today. What is left over is the NPV.

HEART: Two inputs matter. The cash flows are the expected incremental cash flows after tax, meaning only the cash that changes because the firm takes the project. And the discount rate starts from the firm's cost of capital, then moves up for a riskier project or down for a safer one. That rate is the project's required rate of return.

LEWIS: The decision rule for an independent project is simple. Accept a positive NPV and reject a negative one. Positive means shareholders are expected to come out richer, negative means poorer, and zero means no expected change either way.

HEART: Why does that work? The discount rate is what investors could earn elsewhere at the same risk. So a project with a positive NPV beats that alternative, and the NPV is the surplus, in today's dollars, left after investors have been paid their full required return. If the market trusts the forecast, the company's total value should rise by about that surplus, spread across its shares.

LEWIS: The trap is the zero. An NPV of zero does not mean the project earns nothing. It means the project earns exactly its required return. Investors are paid in full, but no extra value is created.

HEART: The internal rate of return, IRR, runs the same idea in reverse. Instead of picking a rate and finding the value, you ask which discount rate makes the present value of the inflows equal the present value of the outflows. Put another way, it is the rate at which NPV is exactly zero. Think of it as the project's own expected rate of return.

LEWIS: The IRR rule is to accept if the IRR is above the required rate of return and reject if it is below. That required return, set for the project's risk, works as a bar to clear, which is why the minimum acceptable IRR is called the hurdle rate.

HEART: For one independent project with ordinary cash flows, NPV and IRR always agree on accept or reject. Here is why. Raise the discount rate, and future cash is worth less today, so NPV falls. IRR is the rate where NPV reaches zero. If your hurdle rate sits below the IRR, the NPV at that hurdle must still be above zero. If the hurdle sits above the IRR, the NPV is below zero.

LEWIS: Ordinary has a proper name. In a conventional cash flow pattern, the signs flip just once. Money goes out, perhaps over several periods, and then money comes back. In an unconventional pattern, the signs flip more than once. Picture a ferry that costs money up front, earns for some years, needs an expensive overhaul halfway through its working life, and then earns again. Outflow, inflows, outflow, inflows.

HEART: That is where IRR stumbles. With more than one sign change, a project can have more than one IRR, or none at all, and the results are hard to interpret. NPV at the required return still gives a single answer. Cash flows that arrive at uneven intervals are often lumped in with the unconventional cases too, and a spreadsheet is the practical tool for both.

LEWIS: Each measure has its strengths. NPV answers the question shareholders care about most, how much richer this makes them, in dollars. IRR speaks in rates, the return on each dollar put in, which is how most people compare investments. And IRR shows you a cushion that a dollar figure hides, called the margin of safety. Think of it as how far above the bar the project sails. If the forecast return is well above the hurdle, the actual return can fall a long way short of the forecast before the NPV turns negative.

HEART: So a project that barely clears its hurdle has almost no room for error.

LEWIS: IRR has two weaknesses. One is the multiple IRR problem we just covered. The other is the reinvestment assumption. IRR implicitly assumes the project's interim cash flows are reinvested at the IRR itself. NPV assumes they are reinvested at the required rate of return. For a project with a very high IRR, earning that same rate again on every dollar it throws off is unrealistic. The required return is the more believable assumption.

HEART: That difference matters most when the two measures disagree, which can happen with mutually exclusive projects, where taking one rules out the other. Think of one vacant lot that can hold a car wash or an apartment building.

LEWIS: Two things cause the conflict. First, scale. Would you rather earn a hundred percent on ten dollars, or twenty percent on ten thousand dollars? The first has the higher rate of return. The second makes you far richer. A small project can post a dazzling IRR and still add fewer dollars of value than a big project with a solid one.

HEART: The second is timing. A project whose cash comes back early tends to show a higher IRR than one whose cash comes back late, even when the late project returns more dollars in total. But the late project's NPV is more sensitive to the discount rate, the way a long bond's price is more sensitive to yields. So at a low required return the late project can win on NPV, while at a high one the early project wins. The IRR ranking never looks at your actual required return.

LEWIS: When they conflict, go with NPV. Shareholders spend dollars, not percentages, and NPV's reinvestment assumption is the realistic one.

HEART: The third measure steps back from single projects to the whole company. Return on invested capital, ROIC, sometimes just called return on capital, is after tax operating profit divided by the average book value of total capital, meaning debt plus equity.

LEWIS: Why operating profit after tax, rather than net income? Because the denominator includes the lenders' money, so the numerator has to be the profit available to lenders and shareholders together. Net income has already paid the lenders their interest. So you add that interest back, after tax, and you get after tax operating profit. Analysts shorten it to NOPAT, net operating profit after tax.

HEART: The yardstick is the return the firm's investors require, a blended rate that covers both the lenders and the shareholders. Beat that blend over time and the firm is adding value. Fall short, and its investors would have done better with the money somewhere else at similar risk.

LEWIS: ROIC splits neatly into two levers. It equals the after tax operating margin, profit per dollar of sales, times capital turnover, also called asset turnover, which is sales per dollar of capital. A luxury watchmaker earns a fat margin but turns its capital over slowly. A discount grocer earns pennies on each sale but turns its capital over again and again. Both can reach the same ROIC by different routes, and either can improve it by widening the margin or speeding up the turnover.

HEART: For an outside investor, ROIC has two things going for it. You can work it out from the published financial statements, without seeing a single internal forecast. And it measures the thing you can actually buy. You cannot buy a slice of one new plant, only a share of the whole company.

LEWIS: Its blind spots are just as clear, and there are three. Two firms can account for the same business differently, so their ROIC figures may not line up. It describes the past, and it can lurch from one year to the next. And it is an average across the whole firm, so a star division can hide a money losing one, and a few good calls can cover for some bad ones.

HEART: In short, NPV and IRR look forward at a single project, and they are the insiders' decision tools. ROIC looks back at the whole company, and it is the outsiders' scorecard.

LEWIS: The takeaway. Accept positive NPV projects, and accept when the IRR beats the hurdle rate. The two agree on a single conventional project, and when they rank mutually exclusive projects differently, NPV wins. ROIC above the blended required return means the firm has been creating value.

## Module 24.2, Capital Allocation Principles and Real Options

HEART: NPV is only as good as the cash flows fed into it, and a few principles keep those cash flows honest.

LEWIS: First, work with after tax cash flows, not accounting income. Profit on the income statement is built on accruals, which assign revenue and costs to the period they belong to, so it can say little about when cash actually moves. And only cash after tax counts, because the tax collector's share was never the shareholders' to begin with. That cuts both ways. Depreciation and amortization are not cash, but they shrink the tax bill, and that tax saving is real cash that belongs in the analysis.

HEART: Second, count only incremental cash flows, the ones that change because the firm takes the project. Ask what the firm's cash looks like with the project and without it. Only the difference counts.

LEWIS: That rules out sunk costs, money that is gone whatever the firm decides. Picture a hotel chain that already paid an architect for early drawings of a new tower. Those fees stay spent whether the tower is built or not, so they stay out of the decision. It is the same reason you should not sit through a dull movie just because you paid for the ticket.

HEART: Incremental thinking also reaches into the rest of the business. When a new product takes sales from an existing one, that is cannibalization, a negative effect that counts against the project. A coffee chain that opens a new store three blocks from an old one will pull some customers away from the old store, and that lost cash flow is a cost of the new store.

LEWIS: Spillovers can be good news too. A positive externality is a project that lifts sales of the firm's other products. A printer maker that launches a cheaper printer may sell a lot more ink, and that extra ink cash flow belongs to the printer project.

HEART: Two more points follow from the same logic. Opportunity costs count. If the project takes over a warehouse the firm already owns but could rent out, the rent it gives up is a cost of the project, even though no cash changes hands. And financing costs, such as interest, stay out of the cash flows. The cost of financing is already built into the discount rate, so subtracting the interest as well would count it twice.

LEWIS: Third, timing matters. Money received sooner is worth more than money received later, which is why NPV discounts each cash flow according to when it arrives.

HEART: Now the ways firms go wrong. The mistakes fall into two groups: cognitive errors, which are mistakes in the numbers, and behavioral biases, which are lapses of judgment.

LEWIS: There are three cognitive errors. The first is poor forecasting. Two common forms are getting overhead wrong, for example charging a project with a slice of head office costs that would be the same either way, and ignoring how competitors will respond. Launch a cheaper phone plan, and a rival may match it the next week.

HEART: The second is ignoring the cost of internal funds. Cash a firm has kept from past earnings is not free money. It could have been paid out to shareholders as dividends, so its cost is the cost of equity. A company that would rather sit on cash than pay it out is the most exposed, because money burning a hole in its pocket feels free, and weak projects get funded.

LEWIS: The third is mishandling inflation. The rule is consistency. If the cash flow forecasts leave inflation out, which is called real terms, discount them at a real rate. If the forecasts include inflation, which is nominal terms, match them with a nominal rate. Mix them and the answer is biased. Real cash flows at a nominal rate understate the NPV, and nominal cash flows at a real rate overstate it.

HEART: Then the behavioral biases, and there are four. Pet projects come first. If a powerful executive loves an idea, nobody wants to be the one who trims its sales forecast, and the tough questions other proposals face go unasked.

LEWIS: Second, inertia. Picture a budget meeting where every department gets last year's number plus a little. The budget is anchored to the past instead of to the opportunities in front of the firm. A firm serious about shareholder value starts each year from the opportunities. A thin year means the spare cash goes back to the owners, and a year full of positive NPV projects means a bigger budget. For an analyst, the warning sign is a capital budget that never shrinks, even as the returns it earns keep sliding.

HEART: Third, basing investment decisions on earnings per share, EPS, or return on equity, ROE. Tie a manager's bonus to those numbers, and watch what happens to a project that hurts them early but pays off handsomely later. A new plant with heavy startup costs is the classic victim. It has a positive NPV over the long run, and it gets turned down because it dents EPS or ROE in the next year or two.

LEWIS: Fourth, failing to generate alternatives. The first workable plan wins by default. Before approving a new warehouse, did anyone ask about leasing one, adding on to the old one, or doing nothing at all?

HEART: Now real options. Investing today often buys the firm choices it can make later, and those choices are real options. They behave like financial options: the firm may act, but it never has to. And a right you are free to ignore cannot hurt you. If acting later would destroy value, the firm simply does not act, so a real option is never worth less than zero.

LEWIS: And that is why leaving them out can make a good project look bad. A standard NPV assumes the firm follows one fixed plan whatever happens. Real managers adapt, and that flexibility is worth something.

HEART: There are five types. A timing option is the right to wait and learn before committing. A developer holding a plot of land can wait a year to see whether housing demand in the area firms up before it builds.

LEWIS: An abandonment option is the right to walk away. The test compares two present values: the incremental cash flows the firm expects from getting out, against those it expects from staying in. When getting out is worth more, it walks. A restaurant group that can shut a weak location and sell its kitchen equipment holds this option. It works like a put option, the right to sell the project's assets for what they would fetch and step away.

HEART: An expansion option, or growth option, is the right to scale up later if the first step proves out. A retailer that opens one pilot store in a new city holds the option to roll out twenty more if the pilot thrives. It works like a call option, the right to buy more of the project later by paying the cost of expanding.

LEWIS: Flexibility options are about running the project once it exists, and there are two main kinds. A price setting option lets the firm charge more when demand is strong, so it earns more from the same output. Think of a ski resort that raises lift ticket prices on the busiest powder weekends. A production flexibility option lets the firm change how or what it makes: run overtime shifts, swap one raw material for another, or shift the product mix. A factory that can switch between making gloves and making masks as orders change holds one.

HEART: Last, a fundamental option. Here the option is not a feature added to the project. The project is the option, because what it pays depends on the price of some underlying asset. Picture a backup power plant that sits idle most of the year and fires up only when electricity prices spike. Almost everything it earns depends on the price of power, so owning the plant is, in effect, owning an option on that price.

LEWIS: How do you bring options into the decision? One way is to put a number on each option, with an option pricing model or a decision tree, add it to the NPV, and take off whatever extra the firm pays to get the option, such as a higher price for a site with room to expand. The other way is simpler. Leave the options out of the number, and read the plain NPV as a floor, the least the project is worth, since the options can only add to it.

HEART: Here is the trap. The option itself is never worth less than zero, but paying for it can still be a mistake. Paying more for a site with room to grow makes sense only if the option to expand is worth more than the extra it costs.

LEWIS: The takeaway. Work with after tax, incremental cash flows, timed when they occur, leaving out sunk costs and financing costs and including spillovers and opportunity costs. Watch for cognitive errors in the numbers and behavioral biases in judgment. And real options add value, because a right you never have to use cannot be worth less than nothing.

## Recall check

LEWIS Q: A company starts paying its suppliers ten days later than before, with nothing else changing. Does its cash conversion cycle get longer or shorter?

HEART: Answer. Shorter, by those ten days. Here is why. The cycle is inventory days plus collection days minus payable days, so more payable days means more is subtracted. Suppliers are now funding more of the trip from buying inventory to collecting cash, so the firm's own cash is tied up for less time.

HEART Q: Suppliers start demanding faster payment. Is that a drag on liquidity or a pull, and what happens to the cycle?

LEWIS: Answer. A pull, and the cycle gets longer. Here is why. A pull means cash going out sooner, while a drag means cash coming in later. Paying suppliers faster cuts days payables outstanding, and because payables are subtracted, the cycle grows. A longer cycle means less liquidity.

LEWIS Q: A retailer's current ratio has held steady for three years while its quick ratio has fallen. What is the likely story?

HEART: Answer. Inventory is building up and propping up the current ratio. Here is why. The two ratios share the same current liabilities, and the main difference is that the quick ratio leaves out inventory. If the current ratio holds while the quick ratio falls, the more liquid assets are shrinking relative to what the firm owes, and inventory is filling the gap. Inventory is the least liquid current asset, so liquidity is weaker than the current ratio suggests.

HEART Q: To meet its obligations, a firm suspends its dividend and restructures its debt to push the maturities out. Are those primary or secondary sources of liquidity, and how will the market read them?

LEWIS: Answer. Secondary sources, and the market will read them as a negative signal. Here is why. Primary sources are the normal ones: cash and securities on hand, bank borrowing and the cash the business generates. Reaching past them to cut the dividend or rework the debt tells investors the normal sources were not enough. Secondary sources also tend to cost more.

LEWIS Q: Compared with a conservative approach, does an aggressive working capital approach have higher or lower returns, and what risk does it add?

HEART: Answer. Higher returns, with more rollover risk and less liquidity. Here is why. The aggressive firm keeps its short term assets lean, so less money sits in low yielding assets, and it funds itself with short term debt, which usually costs less than long term debt or equity. But that debt has to be renewed again and again, and if lenders pull back or rates jump, the firm may not be able to meet its obligations.

HEART Q: A project's NPV is exactly zero. Does that mean it earns no return?

LEWIS: Answer. No, it earns exactly its required rate of return. Here is why. NPV discounts every cash flow at the required return, so a zero means the cash flows are just enough to pay investors that return in full. Nothing is left over, so no extra value is created, but nothing is lost either. Its IRR equals the required return.

LEWIS Q: Two projects are mutually exclusive. One has the higher IRR, and the other has the higher NPV at the firm's required return. Which do you pick?

HEART: Answer. The one with the higher NPV. Here is why. NPV measures the dollars of value a project adds, and shareholders are made richer by dollars, not percentages. IRR can favor a small project with a dazzling rate, or one whose cash comes back early. NPV also assumes interim cash flows are reinvested at the required return, which is more realistic than reinvesting at the IRR itself.

HEART Q: A project's cash flows change sign more than once, for example because of a big overhaul midway through its life. What goes wrong with IRR?

LEWIS: Answer. There can be more than one IRR, or none at all. Here is why. IRR is the rate at which NPV equals zero. With a single sign change, NPV falls as the rate rises and crosses zero at most once. With several sign changes, NPV can rise and fall as the rate changes, so it can cross zero more than once or never. NPV at the required return still gives one clear answer.

LEWIS Q: Why do outside investors lean on ROIC, and what can it hide?

HEART: Answer. It is built from published accounting data and covers the whole firm, but it can hide weak projects behind strong ones. Here is why. Outsiders cannot see or buy into individual projects, so a firm wide return, compared with the blended required return, is the scorecard actually open to them. Because it averages everything, a star division can mask a money losing one.

HEART Q: A firm weighing a new product paid for engineering studies last year, expects the product to take some sales from an older product, and will get a tax saving from depreciating the new equipment. Which of these belong in the project's cash flows?

LEWIS: Answer. The lost sales and the depreciation tax saving belong, and the engineering studies do not. Here is why. Only incremental cash flows count, the ones that change because of the decision. The study money is sunk, gone whatever the firm decides. The cannibalized sales are cash the firm loses only if it launches, and the tax saving is real cash it gains only if it buys the equipment.

LEWIS Q: A manager treats retained earnings as free money when judging projects. Which kind of pitfall is that, and what should the cost of those funds be?

HEART: Answer. A cognitive error, and the cost should be the cost of equity. Here is why. Cash kept inside the firm could have been paid to shareholders as dividends, and they could have invested it elsewhere at a return that fits the risk. Treating it as free lowers the bar, so weak projects get funded. Firms that avoid paying dividends are especially prone to this.

HEART Q: Can a real option ever reduce a project's value, and how do you bring one into the decision?

LEWIS: Answer. No, the option itself cannot be worth less than zero, though acquiring it can cost money. Here is why. An option is a right, not an obligation, so the firm acts on it only when that adds value. To include it, add the option's value to the NPV and subtract whatever extra it costs to obtain, or treat the NPV without options as a floor. If the plain NPV is already positive, the project clears the bar before any options are counted.

## Key takeaways

HEART: First, a few memory hooks.

LEWIS: For the cash conversion cycle: shelf time plus collection time, minus the time your suppliers wait. Paying later shortens it.

HEART: For what squeezes liquidity: a drag holds back the cash coming in, and a pull yanks the cash going out.

LEWIS: For the ratios, picture peeling layers. Current keeps every current asset, quick peels off the inventory, and cash peels off the receivables too. Each peel makes the test stricter, so current is never below quick, and quick is never below cash.

HEART: For sources of liquidity, think of a household. The paycheck, the savings and the credit line are primary. Selling the car is secondary, and the neighbors notice.

LEWIS: For working capital strategy: conservative sleeps well, aggressive eats well. Moderate funds permanent needs with permanent money and seasonal needs with seasonal money.

HEART: For the four types of capital investment: keep it running, keep it legal, grow it, or try something new. For the four steps: think, test, choose, check.

LEWIS: For NPV: zero means a fair return, not no return. For IRR: the rate where NPV hits zero, and its cushion above the hurdle is the margin of safety. When they disagree on mutually exclusive projects, NPV wins, because you bank dollars, not percentages.

HEART: For ROIC: margin times turnover, compared with the blended required return. It is the outsider's rearview mirror.

LEWIS: For cash flows: after tax, incremental and on time. Sunk is sunk, and interest belongs in the discount rate.

HEART: For the behavioral biases, four P's: pet projects, past budgets, pay plans tied to EPS or ROE, and the missing plan B. The cognitive errors are slips in the numbers: bad forecasts, treating internal funds as free, and mixing real with nominal.

LEWIS: For real options: wait, walk away, grow, flex, or own a project that is itself the option. A right you never have to use is never worth less than zero.

HEART: Now, how you would say the big ideas to a senior leader.

LEWIS: On working capital: every day our cash sits in inventory or waits on a customer, we are financing the business ourselves, and every day a supplier waits on us, they are financing it. The jargon is the cash conversion cycle.

HEART: On liquidity and funding: short term money is cheaper, but it has to be renewed, sometimes just when the market is shut, and selling assets in a hurry means taking a discount. The jargon is rollover risk, secondary sources of liquidity and the cost of liquidity.

LEWIS: On investing: fund a project only if its future cash, valued today at the return investors demand for that risk, is worth more than it costs. The jargon is net present value and the hurdle rate.

HEART: On competing projects: a high percentage return on a small project can add less value than a solid return on a large one, so rank by value added. The jargon is mutually exclusive projects and the conflict between NPV and IRR.

LEWIS: And on flexibility: the ability to wait, expand or walk away has a value of its own that a fixed plan leaves out. The jargon is real options.
