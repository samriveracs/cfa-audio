LEWIS: Reading 25 covers what a company's money costs on a blended basis, what lets a company carry more or less debt, and the theories of how much to borrow, from Modigliani and Miller to the tradeoff between tax savings and financial distress. Reading 26 covers what a business model has to explain, the main pricing strategies, and the common types of business model.

## Module 25.1, Weighted-Average Cost of Capital

HEART: Think about how a family pays for a house. Part comes from a mortgage and part from savings. The mortgage costs interest, and the savings cost whatever that money could have earned elsewhere. The true cost of the house money is a blend of the two, weighted by how much of each went in.

LEWIS: A company works the same way. It raises money from lenders and from owners, and each group expects a return for the risk it carries. Blend those required returns, weighting each one by its share of the company's funding, and you get the weighted average cost of capital, or WACC. It is the average rate the company has to earn to keep all of its investors satisfied.

HEART: Two ingredients go in. The cost of debt is the return lenders require today, and the cost of equity is the return shareholders require. Debt is the cheaper of the two, and the reason is priority. Lenders have the first claim. They are promised fixed payments, and they get paid before the owners see anything. Shareholders hold the residual claim, whatever is left over, so they carry more risk and demand more.

LEWIS: Picture a small bakery. Out of every month's sales come the flour, the wages, the rent and the interest on the oven loan. The owner keeps whatever remains, so in a bad month the lender still gets paid and the owner comes up short. That is why owners demand more than lenders.

HEART: Then comes the twist that makes debt cheaper still. In most countries a company can deduct its interest expense before it works out its income tax, so every dollar of interest shrinks the tax bill. The cost of debt that goes into the WACC is therefore the after tax cost, the pretax rate times one minus the tax rate. With a tax rate of one quarter, a dollar of interest really costs the company about seventy five cents.

LEWIS: Dividends get no such break. They are paid out of income that has already been taxed. So the cost of equity goes into the blend exactly as it is, with no tax adjustment.

HEART: Put it into one relationship in words. The WACC is the weight of debt times the after tax cost of debt, plus the weight of equity times the cost of equity. If a company also has preferred stock, it simply gets its own weight and its own cost in the blend, again with no tax adjustment, because preferred dividends are not deductible either.

LEWIS: A few directions to hold onto. A higher tax rate makes the interest deduction worth more, so the after tax cost of debt falls and, other things equal, the WACC falls with it. And the WACC always lands somewhere between the after tax cost of debt and the cost of equity, nearer to whichever source carries the bigger weight. If an answer falls outside that range, something has gone wrong.

HEART: Here is the trap. The tax adjustment belongs to debt alone. Forget it, and the WACC comes out too high. Apply it to equity as well, and the WACC comes out too low. Both wrong answers tend to be waiting in the choices.

LEWIS: So what does the number mean? The WACC is the company's opportunity cost of capital, the return its investors could expect elsewhere for taking on the same kind of risk. That makes it the hurdle an investment of average risk for that company has to clear. Earn less than the WACC on new projects, and the company destroys value even while it reports a profit.

HEART: Now the weights. There are two ways to set them. Target weights are the mix of debt and equity the company aims to hold over time. Market value weights are the mix today, measured at market prices. Because market values move with current conditions, they suit the job of measuring today's opportunity cost. In practice targets are often stated in book value terms, and Module 25.2 comes back to who prefers which, and why.

LEWIS: The second half of this module asks a bigger question. Why do some companies borrow heavily while others in the same economy barely borrow at all?

HEART: Companies typically aim for the mix that keeps the WACC as low as possible. They also try to match the length of their funding to the life of their assets. A utility building a power plant that will run for decades funds it with long term bonds. A toy retailer stocking up for the holidays funds that inventory with short term borrowing that it repays once the season's sales come in.

LEWIS: Banks and credit unions know the cost of getting that match wrong. Fund long fixed rate loans with deposits that can reprice every month, and a jump in rates squeezes the margin. Matching the timing of funding to the timing of assets is a big part of managing that risk.

HEART: Underneath the choice sits one question. How much debt can this company reliably service? The factors that answer it come in two groups. Internal factors are about the company itself: the characteristics of its business and industry, its stage in the life cycle, how much debt it already carries, and its corporate tax rate. External factors are about the world around it: market and business cycle conditions, regulation, and industry norms.

LEWIS: Regulation is easy to picture in finance, where minimum capital requirements set part of a bank's funding mix by rule. Industry norms matter because lenders and rating agencies judge a company against its peers.

HEART: Now the company characteristics, and the logic behind all of them is the same. Lenders want to be confident the payments will keep coming. So look for five things. First, the growth and stability of revenue. Second, the growth and predictability of cash flow. Third, the amount of business risk, meaning operating risk and demand risk, which make earnings and cash flow swing. Fourth, how much the company owns and how liquid those assets are, because assets can be pledged as collateral. Fifth, the cost and availability of debt itself. When lenders are eager and rates are low, debt looks more attractive.

LEWIS: That gives you three comparisons the exam likes, each with other things equal. A company in a noncyclical industry can carry more debt than one in a cyclical industry. People keep buying toothpaste and paying water bills in a recession, but they put off buying a new car. A company with low operating leverage, meaning fixed costs are a small share of its total costs, can carry more debt than one with heavy fixed costs. A staffing agency's costs shrink when its billed hours shrink, but a chip factory's costs barely move when orders dry up.

HEART: And a company with subscription revenue can carry more debt than one paid per use. A home alarm company with monthly contracts knows next year's revenue far better than a bowling alley paid by the game. The common thread is simple. The more the cash arrives on a regular, repeating schedule that holds up in good years and bad, the bigger the share of debt a company can support.

LEWIS: The kind of assets matters too. Lenders would rather lend against things they can touch, so tangible assets beat intangible ones as collateral. Best of all is an asset that can be turned into cash fast at nearly full value, which is what liquid means here, or one that is interchangeable with many others like it, which is what fungible means. A fleet of delivery trucks makes good collateral. A brand name or a body of software code does not, because if the company fails, its value may vanish with it.

HEART: Ownership counts as well. A grocery chain that owns its stores and warehouses has collateral to offer. A grocery delivery service that relies on other companies' stores and on drivers in their own cars has far less to pledge. More collateral means easier access to debt and lower borrowing costs.

LEWIS: Then there is the starting point. A company that already carries a lot of debt, or whose earnings jump around, has less room to borrow. Analysts gauge that room with leverage ratios, like debt to equity or debt to operating profit, and with coverage ratios. The main one is interest coverage, operating profit, also called EBIT, divided by interest expense. In plain words, how many times over the profit could pay the interest bill. More coverage means more room.

HEART: All of this comes together in the company life cycle. Picture a coffee company, from its first shop to its thousandth.

LEWIS: The startup stage is the first shop. The register has barely started ringing, profits and cash flow are thin or negative, and business risk is high. There are few receivables and little equipment to pledge. Any lender would demand a very high rate, if it lent at all. So startups are financed almost entirely with equity, from founders, friends and venture investors.

HEART: Two exceptions are worth knowing. If the company is growing fast and its shares are soaring, it may find buyers for convertible debt, bonds that investors can later swap for shares. Because investors value that option, they accept a lower interest rate, and the company avoids diluting its existing owners right away. The other exception is leasing, which gives a young company the equipment it needs without buying it, a form of debt financing open even at this stage.

LEWIS: The growth stage is the tenth shop. Sales and cash flow are climbing, and the business is somewhat less risky than it was. Lenders become willing, often with loans secured by the equipment or by receivables, and the company borrows, but conservatively.

HEART: The mature stage is the thousandth shop. Growth has cooled, but cash comes in reliably and in size, and the business is far less risky. Now lenders line up to lend at fairly low rates, and many will lend unsecured, with nothing pledged but the company's proven ability to pay. Mature companies tend to carry significantly more debt.

LEWIS: The cost of capital is also pushed around by top down factors. These are the big economic forces that move the benchmark interest rate, such as the yield on Treasury bonds, and the credit spread each borrower pays above it. Think inflation, real GDP growth, monetary policy and exchange rates.

HEART: The business cycle shows up in those spreads. In a downturn, lenders worry more about default, so they demand wider spreads over the benchmark, and companies in cyclical industries feel it most. But the environment does not hit every industry the same way. A weaker home currency, for instance, can lift an exporter's profits and help its credit standing at the same time it squeezes an importer.

LEWIS: The trap in this module. A question describes a young, fast growing technology company and asks how it most likely finances itself. Debt is cheaper, so debt tempts you. But cheap debt only helps when cash flow is steady enough to make the payments. The young company leans on equity.

HEART: The takeaway. The WACC blends the after tax cost of debt with the cost of equity, and only debt gets the tax adjustment. A company can carry more debt when its revenue and cash flow are stable and its assets make good collateral, which is why debt rises from startup to maturity.

## Module 25.2, Capital Structure Theories

LEWIS: The mix of debt and equity a company funds itself with is its capital structure. This module asks whether that mix can change what the company is worth. The starting point is a paper published in nineteen fifty eight by Franco Modigliani and Merton Miller, both of whom later won the Nobel prize in economics. Their results are known as the M and M propositions.

HEART: They began in an imaginary perfect world, and every later theory in this module comes from relaxing one of its five assumptions.

LEWIS: First, capital markets are perfectly competitive, with no transaction costs, no taxes and no bankruptcy costs. Second, investors have homogeneous expectations, meaning they all expect the same cash flows from the company. Third, there is riskless borrowing and lending, so investors can borrow and lend at the risk free rate. Fourth, there are no agency costs, so managers and shareholders never have conflicting interests. Fifth, investment decisions are unaffected by financing decisions, so the company's operating income is the same however it is financed.

HEART: In that world, M and M Proposition one says capital structure is irrelevant. The value of the company does not depend on how much debt it carries.

LEWIS: Picture a twenty dollar bill. Break it into two tens, or four fives, and you are no richer. How the value is divided does not change how much value there is. A company's operating earnings, its EBIT, are like that bill. Debt and equity are just two ways of dividing up the claims on it.

HEART: Here is the logic in finance terms. However the claims are split, lenders and owners together collect every dollar of operating earnings, no more and no less. The owners of an identical company with no debt collect exactly the same dollars, with exactly the same risk. Same earnings, same risk, so the two companies must be worth the same in total. The value comes from the operating earnings and how risky they are, not from the labels on the claims.

LEWIS: And the borrowing assumption explains why the two values cannot drift apart. If a company with debt sold for more than an identical company without it, an investor could borrow personally at the same rate, buy shares in the debt free company, and build the same payoffs more cheaply. That is called homemade leverage, and it pulls the two values back together.

HEART: Proposition two looks at the same world through the cost of capital instead of value. It says the cost of equity rises in a straight line as the debt to equity ratio rises.

LEWIS: Why should it rise at all? Go back to priority. Lenders are promised fixed payments and get paid first, so their cash flows are more certain, and the cost of debt is lower than the cost of equity. Shareholders get the remainder. The more debt there is, the bigger the fixed bite that comes out first, and the more the remainder swings. A riskier remainder means a higher required return.

HEART: Picture two friends buying a rental house. One lends part of the price at a fixed rate, and the other owns the house with the rest. If the lender puts in a little, a bad year with an empty unit barely dents the owner. If the lender puts in most of the price, the same bad year could wipe out the owner's whole return. Same house, same rents, but the owner's share gets far bumpier as the loan grows.

LEWIS: In words, the cost of equity equals the cost of equity the company would have with no debt, plus a premium. That premium is the gap between the no debt cost of equity and the cost of debt, multiplied by the debt to equity ratio. Double the debt to equity ratio and you double the premium.

HEART: Now the punch line. As the company swaps equity for cheaper debt, the cost of equity climbs by exactly enough to cancel the savings. The cost of debt stays where it is, the WACC stays where it is, and so does the value. Propositions one and two are the same idea told two ways.

LEWIS: The trap is the intuition most people walk in with. Debt is cheaper, so surely more debt lowers the WACC. In the M and M world without taxes, it does not. Cheaper debt is offset point for point by more expensive equity.

HEART: Now relax one assumption and let the government in. In most tax systems, interest is deductible and dividends are not. That difference changes the answer.

LEWIS: Think of the government as a third party with a claim on the operating earnings, alongside lenders and owners. Every dollar paid out as interest escapes corporate tax, so the more debt a company carries, the smaller the government's share and the more is left for lenders and owners together. That saving is called the debt tax shield.

HEART: M and M Proposition one with taxes says the value of a company with debt equals the value of the same company with no debt, plus the value of the tax shield. For debt kept in place permanently, the value of that shield is the tax rate times the amount of debt. More debt or a higher tax rate means a bigger shield and a higher value.

LEWIS: Proposition two with taxes says the cost of equity still rises with leverage, but more slowly, because the premium is scaled down by one minus the tax rate. The tax saving cushions the owners. So now the cheaper debt is no longer fully offset, and the WACC falls steadily as debt rises.

HEART: Follow that to the end. If every extra dollar of debt adds value and lowers the WACC, the best capital structure is one hundred percent debt. That is the conclusion of M and M with taxes. Value is at its highest, and the WACC at its lowest, when the company is funded entirely by debt.

LEWIS: And real companies do not come anywhere near that. M and M themselves suggested one reason, that investors pay different tax rates on interest income and on dividends, which can offset part of the corporate advantage. But the explanation most theory leans on today is a cost the model left out by assumption, the cost of getting into financial trouble.

HEART: These are the costs of financial distress. Distress means operating earnings have sunk so low that paying the fixed financing costs, mainly the interest on the debt, becomes a struggle, and the costs are everything extra that struggle brings. What matters for value is the expected cost, and that has two parts: how large the costs would be, and how likely distress is.

LEWIS: The costs come in two kinds. Direct costs are the cash expenses of bankruptcy itself, the legal fees and the administrative fees. Indirect costs are subtler and often bigger. Picture an appliance maker rumored to be near bankruptcy. Shoppers wonder whether anyone will honor the warranty, suppliers want cash up front, the best engineers take other offers, and good investments get passed up because no one will fund them. The company loses the trust of customers, creditors, suppliers and employees.

HEART: There is a third piece. In distress, the interests of managers, who act for the shareholders, can split sharply from those of the lenders. Shareholders with little left to lose may favor a long shot gamble with what is really the lenders' money, and lenders protect themselves with tighter terms and higher rates. Those costs are called the agency costs of debt.

LEWIS: Then the probability. Distress becomes more likely as operating leverage and financial leverage rise, because more fixed costs and more fixed payments mean a smaller drop in sales can tip the company over. Weak management and poor corporate governance raise the probability as well.

HEART: So higher expected costs of financial distress discourage heavy borrowing, other things equal, and those costs climb as debt piles up.

LEWIS: Put the two forces together and you have the static tradeoff theory. The value of a company with debt equals its value with no debt, plus the tax rate times the debt, minus the present value of the expected costs of financial distress.

HEART: At low levels of debt, distress is a remote possibility, so each extra dollar of debt adds more in tax savings than it costs in expected distress, and value rises. Keep borrowing, and at some point the extra expected distress cost from one more dollar of debt outgrows the extra tax saving it brings, and value starts to fall. The point where those two extra amounts just balance is the optimal capital structure.

LEWIS: Think of a marathon pace. A little faster than comfortable gets you a better time. Push much harder, and the risk of blowing up in the last few miles costs more than the speed gained. The best pace sits in between.

HEART: And here is the link that ties the reading together. The optimal capital structure is where the company's value is highest, and at that very same point, the WACC is lowest. Picture the WACC against the amount of debt. It falls at first as cheap after tax debt comes in, bottoms out at the optimum, then rises as distress risk drives up the cost of both debt and equity. Value is the mirror image, rising to a peak and then falling.

LEWIS: Each company's optimum is its own. It depends on business risk, meaning operating risk and sales risk, the tax rate, corporate governance, industry influences and other factors. A steady utility's optimum sits at far more debt than a young software company's.

HEART: So you have three worlds and three answers. With no taxes and no distress costs, capital structure is irrelevant. With taxes but no distress costs, all debt is best. With taxes and distress costs, there is an optimum somewhere in between.

LEWIS: Next, the target capital structure. That is the long run mix management steers toward because it believes that mix gives the company its highest value, with the internal and external factors from Module 25.1 shaping the choice. It is the aim over time, not where the structure sits on any given day.

HEART: When an analyst builds a WACC, the weights belong to that long run target, valued at market prices, with any preferred stock sitting alongside the debt and the equity. Some managements announce their target, but most keep it to themselves.

LEWIS: Without one, the analyst has to infer it, and there are three common routes. Take today's mix at market values as a proxy for the target. Tilt that figure in the direction the mix has been moving, if there is a clear trend. Or borrow the typical mix for the company's industry.

HEART: Managers, by contrast, often think in book values, and there are three reasons that can make sense for internal decisions. A share price that jumps in a hot month, or a bond price that dips, has not changed how much debt the business can safely carry. Management's job is to put capital to work in projects, which is a different view from an investor pricing the shares. And credit rating agencies measure credit quality with book values, so managers keep those measures in mind.

LEWIS: In practice, the actual structure wanders around the target, the way a room's temperature drifts around the thermostat setting. Three things push it off. Market values move, especially the value of the equity, so a rally in the stock raises the equity weight without anyone lifting a finger. Management may seize an opportunity in one market, such as locking in a large long term bond while borrowing is unusually cheap, which leaves more debt than the target. And outside capital is raised in lots of a minimum size, so a company cannot raise exactly the slice it needs every time.

HEART: Now three ideas that explain real financing behavior. The first is asymmetric information. Managers know more about the company's prospects than its shareholders or lenders do. The harder a company is to understand from outside, because of complex products or murky accounts, the wider that gap, and investors charge for it with higher required returns on both debt and equity.

LEWIS: Because investors know the gap exists, they watch what management does for clues. Picture a restaurant owner who knows the books better than anyone. If she asks to borrow from you at a fixed rate, she is signaling confidence that the restaurant can make the payments. If she instead offers to sell you half the restaurant, you have to wonder why she wants out of half of it now.

HEART: Markets judge financing choices the same way. Taking on debt commits the company to fixed payments, which signals that management expects to make them. New shares get the opposite reception. Investors assume managers sell stock when they think it is overvalued, so an equity issue is usually taken as a negative signal. That is why the cost of asymmetric information grows the more a company leans on equity.

LEWIS: The second idea is agency costs of equity. They come from conflicts of interest between managers and owners. A manager who owns little of the company pays only a sliver of the cost of an inflated salary, or of steering the company too boldly or too timidly. Shareholders know this, so they spend money to limit it. The total bill, what they spend plus what still slips through, is called the net agency cost of equity. It has three parts.

HEART: Think of hiring a house sitter. You install a doorbell camera and call to check in. Those are monitoring costs, and for a company they include reporting to shareholders and paying the board of directors. Strong corporate governance keeps them down.

LEWIS: The sitter carries insurance that pays you if something goes wrong. Those are bonding costs, what managers take on to assure owners they will act in the owners' interest, like insurance premiums that guarantee performance or the hidden cost of agreeing not to work for a competitor. And even so, you come home to find the snacks gone. That is the residual loss, what still slips through because monitoring and bonding never give a perfect guarantee.

HEART: Debt has a role to play here, through the free cash flow hypothesis. Required interest and principal payments soak up cash, so managers have less free cash flow to spend on themselves or on pet projects. Debt forces discipline, so more financial leverage tends to reduce the agency costs of equity.

LEWIS: The third idea is the pecking order theory, which grows out of asymmetric information. Managers reach first for the money least likely to send a bad signal, which is the money outsiders can see least, so the ranking runs from the least visible choice to the most visible. Internally generated funds, meaning retained earnings, come first, because they send no signal at all. Debt comes second. New outside equity comes last.

HEART: That has a striking implication. Under the pecking order, there is no target at all. A company's capital structure is simply a byproduct of a long string of individual financing decisions. Contrast that with the static tradeoff theory, where management aims for a target that balances tax savings against distress.

LEWIS: The takeaway. Without taxes the mix does not matter, because the cost of equity rises just enough to keep the WACC flat. Taxes favor debt, distress costs limit it, and the static tradeoff optimum is where value peaks and the WACC bottoms out.

## Module 26.1, Business Model Features and Types

HEART: Every company that lasts clears four hurdles. It has something to sell, people who want it, a way to get it to them, and money left over at the end. A business model is the account of how a company clears those hurdles today, or how it means to. Strip it down and it answers one question: how does this company turn what it does into profit?

LEWIS: A practical way to build one is to answer five questions: who, what, how, where and how much.

HEART: Who is the customer. That covers who the potential customers are, how the company will win them, what it costs to acquire each one, and how it will track and keep up their satisfaction. Dividing customers into groups is called segmenting. A running shoe company might treat marathoners, casual walkers and nurses on long shifts as three different segments, each wanting something different.

LEWIS: Companies that sell to other businesses are called B to B, short for business to business. Companies that sell to consumers are B to C, business to consumer.

HEART: What is the product or service, the need it meets, and what sets it apart from the competition, whether that is a low price, premium quality or innovative features.

LEWIS: How covers the key assets and the key suppliers. For a coffee chain, the key assets might be its brand, its store locations and its trained staff. Its key suppliers are the coffee growers and roasters, and the dairy that delivers the milk every morning.

HEART: Where is the channel strategy. Where will the product be sold: online, in physical stores, by direct mail, at trade shows or through sales representatives? Will the company sell straight to buyers, which is direct sales, or go through intermediaries like wholesalers, retailers, agents or franchisees? And how will it deliver? A strategy that combines digital and physical channels is called omnichannel. Think of browsing a clothing store's app, trying the jacket on in the shop, and sending it back by mail.

LEWIS: And how much is the pricing strategy: what the company will charge, and why buyers will pay that, given the competition.

HEART: Pricing starts with power. A company selling an undifferentiated product, a commodity producer, is a price taker. A wheat farmer gets the market price and nothing more. Pricing power comes from the opposite situation, few rivals or a product so differentiated that buyers cannot easily swap it for another. Think of the only toll bridge across a wide river. And a company can sometimes charge a premium by including services or features customers value, like free installation or a longer warranty.

LEWIS: Then come the named pricing models, and the exam skill is matching each name to its mechanism. Start with price discrimination, which means charging different prices to different customers or groups of customers.

HEART: Tiered pricing sets the price by volume, so a coffee roaster might charge a cafe less per pound the more it buys. Dynamic pricing changes the price with timing, the time of day or the day of the week. Peak and off peak pricing is the classic form, like a cheaper movie matinee, or hotel rates that soar on a big game weekend.

LEWIS: Value based pricing ties the price to the value the buyer gets, so a buyer who gains more pays more. Fuel saving software for truck fleets might charge a share of the fuel it saves. And auction pricing lets bidders set the price, the way the government sells Treasury bills at auction.

HEART: Some pricing models span more than one product. Bundling sells complementary products together as a package, like a burger, fries and a drink as a combo meal. Razors and blades pricing sells the equipment cheaply, at a thin margin, and earns the profit on the consumables. A coffee pod machine sold near cost, with the pods priced generously, fits the pattern. Add on pricing offers options at high margins after the customer has already decided to buy, like the extended warranty pitched once you have chosen the washing machine.

LEWIS: Then three models that look generous at first. Penetration pricing deliberately prices thin, even below cost, for a while, to grab market share fast and grow to a larger scale. A new mobile carrier pricing well below the big incumbents to fill its network is an example. Freemium gives the basic product away and charges to unlock more. A cloud storage service with a small free allowance and paid plans for more space works this way.

HEART: Hidden revenue means the product is free to the user and the money comes from somewhere else, usually advertisers or the sale of user data. A free radio station that earns everything from its ads is the simplest example.

LEWIS: Here is the trap. Freemium and hidden revenue both look free. The difference is who pays. Under freemium, some of the users themselves pay for the upgrade. Under hidden revenue, no user pays, and a third party foots the bill.

HEART: The last group offers alternatives to buying outright. A subscription model charges a recurring fee for access, like a monthly car wash membership. Licensing lets another company put your intellectual property to work for a fee. An audio technology firm, for instance, might license its noise canceling method to several headphone makers and collect a royalty on every pair they sell, without ever running a factory of its own.

LEWIS: Franchising rents out a whole way of doing business. The franchisee gets the brand, usually with a territory of its own, and hands the franchisor a set percentage of its sales. In return the franchisor helps with the product and the marketing. A hotel brand often works this way. A local owner builds and runs the hotel, and pays fees for the name, the reservation system and the standards.

HEART: Two terms tie the business model together. The value proposition is the case for buying: what customers get from the product, weighed against rival products and their prices. It is the promise, the reason to choose us. The value chain is the machinery that keeps that promise: the company's assets and the way its organization adds value and presses its competitive advantage. It takes in how well the product is made and works, the service that comes with it, the way it is sold, and its price against rivals.

LEWIS: Michael Porter's value chain analysis splits that machinery into the activities a company has to do well. Inbound logistics, bringing inputs in. Operations, turning them into the product. Outbound logistics, getting the product out to customers. Then marketing, sales and service. Picture a bakery: flour deliveries in, baking overnight, vans out at dawn, the chalkboard specials, the counter, and the friendly fix when an order comes out wrong.

HEART: The trap is mixing the two. The value proposition is what the customer is promised. The value chain is how the company delivers on it.

LEWIS: Now the types of business models. Conventional business models tend to be specific to an industry, the familiar ways that miners, manufacturers, retailers, banks or software companies make money.

HEART: Three other business models are worth knowing by name. Private label or contract manufacturers make products that other companies sell under their own brand names, like a drug maker turning out a supermarket's store brand pain reliever. Licensing agreements let one company put another's brand on its products for a fee, like a sports team's logo on a cap made by a clothing company. And value added resellers distribute complex equipment and add installation, service, support or customization, like a firm that sells restaurants their point of sale systems, sets them up and trains the staff.

LEWIS: Fresh business models tend to come from outsiders, newcomers sometimes armed with new technology, who take on the incumbents. Online only banks without branches, and meal kit companies delivering recipes and ingredients to the door, both took on established businesses this way.

HEART: Network effects are the next idea. A network effect means a network becomes more valuable to each user as the number of users grows. The very first telephone was useless. Every phone added after it made all the others more useful. A payment app that lets friends send each other money works the same way, since it is only handy if the people you owe are on it too.

LEWIS: That is why network effects support penetration pricing at the start. Price low, build the user base fast, and the network itself becomes the reason new users come. The advantage builds on two fronts, size, meaning the number of users, and scope, meaning the range of things the network offers them. And some networks are two sided or multisided. A food delivery app connects diners, restaurants and couriers, and each group values the app more as the others grow.

HEART: Finally, crowdsourcing. Here the users themselves supply much of the product. Diners write the restaurant reviews that make a review site worth visiting. Home cooks post and rate the recipes on a cooking site. The more people contribute, the more useful the product becomes for everyone.

LEWIS: The takeaway. A business model answers who, what, how, where and how much. Know the pricing models by their mechanism, keep freemium and hidden revenue apart by who pays, and remember that the value proposition is the promise while the value chain is the delivery.

## Recall check

LEWIS Q: Why does only the cost of debt get multiplied by one minus the tax rate in the WACC?

HEART: Answer. Because interest is tax deductible and dividends are not. Here is why. Every dollar of interest a company pays reduces its taxable income, so the tax saving covers part of the interest bill. Dividends on common or preferred stock come out of income that has already been taxed, so there is no saving to subtract.

HEART Q: Other things equal, the corporate tax rate rises. What happens to the after tax cost of debt and to the WACC?

LEWIS: Answer. Both fall. Here is why. A higher tax rate means each dollar of interest saves more tax, so the after tax cost of debt drops. Debt is one of the ingredients in the blend and nothing else changed, so the WACC drops with it. That is also why a higher tax rate nudges a company toward more debt.

LEWIS Q: Which company can most likely carry more debt, a noncyclical subscription business with low fixed costs, or a cyclical business paid per use with high fixed costs?

HEART: Answer. The noncyclical subscription business with low fixed costs. Here is why. Lenders care most about whether the payments will keep coming. Subscription revenue in a noncyclical industry is stable and predictable, and low operating leverage means profits do not collapse when sales dip. The other company's revenue swings with the economy, and its fixed costs magnify every swing, which leaves less reliable cash for interest.

HEART Q: Why are startups financed almost entirely with equity, and which two kinds of debt financing may still be open to them?

LEWIS: Answer. Their cash flows are low or negative, their risk is high and they have little collateral, though convertible debt and leasing may still be possible. Here is why. A lender wants dependable payments and assets to fall back on, and a startup offers neither, so ordinary debt would be very expensive if it were available at all. A fast growing company with a rising stock price can issue convertible debt at a lower rate, because investors value the chance to swap into shares. Leasing gets it equipment without buying it.

LEWIS Q: In the M and M world without taxes, a company replaces some equity with debt. What happens to its cost of equity, its WACC and its value?

HEART: Answer. The cost of equity rises, while the WACC and the value stay the same. Here is why. Lenders are paid first, so more debt makes the owners' leftover cash flow riskier, and they demand a higher return. That higher cost of equity exactly offsets the saving from the cheaper debt. The operating earnings and their risk have not changed, so neither has the value of the whole company.

HEART Q: Add corporate taxes to the M and M world, but no costs of financial distress. What capital structure maximizes value, and why?

LEWIS: Answer. One hundred percent debt. Here is why. Interest is deductible, so every dollar of permanent debt adds a tax shield worth the tax rate times that dollar. The cost of equity still rises with leverage, but more slowly, so the WACC keeps falling as debt grows. With nothing on the other side of the scale, value peaks at all debt, which is why the model needs distress costs to describe real companies.

LEWIS Q: Under the static tradeoff theory, what is true of the WACC at the optimal capital structure?

HEART: Answer. It is at its lowest, at the same point where the company's value is at its highest. Here is why. Up to the optimum, each extra dollar of debt adds more in tax savings than in expected distress costs, so value rises and the WACC falls. Past it, expected distress costs grow faster than the tax benefit, so value falls and the WACC climbs. The peak in value and the bottom of the WACC are the same point seen two ways.

HEART Q: What two things set the expected cost of financial distress, and what makes the second one larger?

LEWIS: Answer. The size of the costs if distress happens and the probability that it happens, and the probability rises with operating and financial leverage and with weak management and governance. Here is why. An expected cost weighs a possible cost by its likelihood. More fixed costs and more fixed debt payments mean a smaller drop in sales can leave the company unable to pay. Poor management or governance makes it more likely the company stumbles into that position.

LEWIS Q: Under the pecking order theory, how do managers rank retained earnings, debt and new shares, and why do new shares come last?

HEART: Answer. Retained earnings first, then debt, then new shares last, because issuing equity sends the worst signal. Here is why. Managers know more about the company's prospects than outside investors do, so investors watch financing choices for clues. Internal funds send no signal at all, and debt signals confidence that the fixed payments can be met. A new share issue hints that managers think the stock is overvalued.

HEART Q: According to the free cash flow hypothesis, how does more debt affect the agency costs of equity?

LEWIS: Answer. It tends to reduce them. Here is why. Agency costs of equity arise because managers may spend shareholders' money on themselves or on pet projects. Required interest and principal payments soak up cash, leaving less free cash flow for managers to waste. Debt enforces a discipline that shareholders would otherwise have to pay to monitor.

LEWIS Q: A video app is free and earns its money from advertisers, while a photo editing app is free but charges for its advanced tools. Which is freemium and which is hidden revenue?

HEART: Answer. The photo editor is freemium, and the video app is hidden revenue. Here is why. Under freemium, users get the basic version free and some of them pay to unlock more. Under hidden revenue, no user pays anything, and a third party such as an advertiser provides the money. The question to ask is always who pays.

HEART Q: Why do network effects make penetration pricing attractive at the start?

LEWIS: Answer. Because the product becomes more valuable to every user as more users join, so a big early user base is worth paying for. Here is why. Low prices, even losses, attract users quickly. Each new user makes the network more useful to everyone else, which pulls in still more users. Once the network is large, its size becomes the advantage that keeps customers coming.

## Key takeaways

HEART: First, a few memory hooks.

LEWIS: For the WACC: debt gets the tax break, equity pays full price, and the blend always lands between the after tax cost of debt and the cost of equity.

HEART: For debt capacity, think steady, light and solid. Steady revenue and cash flow, light fixed costs, and solid assets to pledge, ideally tangible, liquid and fungible.

LEWIS: For the life cycle: startups sell ownership, growth companies borrow against their equipment and receivables, and mature companies can borrow on their good name alone. Equity, then secured debt, then unsecured debt as well.

HEART: For capital structure, three worlds, three answers. No taxes, the mix does not matter. Taxes, all debt. Taxes plus distress costs, an optimum in between.

LEWIS: Breaking a twenty does not make you richer. And debt is cheap, but it makes equity dearer, so the cost of equity rises in a straight line with the debt to equity ratio.

HEART: For the static tradeoff: value peaks where the WACC bottoms out. Same point, two views.

LEWIS: For the pecking order: save, borrow, then sell shares, because a share sale hints the stock is overvalued.

HEART: For agency costs, the house sitter. The camera is monitoring, the insurance is bonding, and the missing snacks are the residual loss. Debt helps by soaking up the free cash flow.

LEWIS: For business models: who, what, how, where and how much. For free products, ask who pays. Freemium, some users. Hidden revenue, someone else. The value proposition is the promise, and the value chain is the delivery.

HEART: Now, how you would say the big ideas to a senior leader.

LEWIS: On the cost of money: our funding costs a blend of what lenders and owners require, and because interest is tax deductible, borrowing is cheaper than its headline rate. An investment of ordinary risk has to beat that blend to add value. The jargon is the weighted average cost of capital and the after tax cost of debt.

HEART: On whether the mix matters: in a frictionless world, how we split the claims between lenders and owners would not change what the company is worth, so any value from the mix has to come from taxes and real world frictions. The jargon is the Modigliani and Miller propositions on capital structure irrelevance.

LEWIS: On how much to borrow: debt saves tax, but past some point the rising risk of financial trouble costs more than the tax savings, so there is a sweet spot, and it differs for every company. The jargon is the static tradeoff theory, the debt tax shield, the costs of financial distress and the optimal capital structure.

HEART: On how financing looks from outside: borrowing tells the market we are confident we can pay, while issuing new shares can suggest we think our stock is richly priced, so companies tend to fund from their own cash first. The jargon is asymmetric information, signaling and the pecking order theory.

LEWIS: And on any business: before the numbers, ask who the customers are, what they buy, where they buy it, at what price, and how we deliver it at a profit. The jargon is the business model, the value proposition, the channel strategy and the value chain.
