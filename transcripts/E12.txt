## The big picture

This episode is about the right hand side of the balance sheet, and then about the business that sits behind it. Every company funds its assets with some mix of debt and equity, and that mix is called its capital structure. Two questions follow. What does that money cost, on average? And can a company make itself more valuable just by changing the mix?

Module 25.1 answers the first question with the weighted average cost of capital, or WACC. It is the blended return a company must earn to satisfy all of its lenders and owners, and it is the discount rate for projects of average risk. The module also covers the internal and external factors that push a company toward more or less debt, including where it sits in its life cycle.

Module 25.2 takes on the second question. It starts with the famous result from Modigliani and Miller that, in a perfect world, the mix does not matter. Then it adds taxes, the costs of financial distress and information problems, and it arrives at an optimal capital structure and the target structure managers actually use. Module 26.1 then steps back to the business model, the logic of how a company makes money. On the exam, expect a WACC calculation, direction of effect questions on Modigliani and Miller, and matching questions on pricing strategies and business model types.

## Module 25.1, Weighted-Average Cost of Capital

Start with first principles. A company raises money from lenders and from owners, and each group expects a return for the risk it takes. Lenders take less risk, because they are paid first and their payments are fixed, so debt is the cheapest source. Common shareholders are paid last and absorb the losses, so equity is the most expensive. The WACC blends these costs into one number, weighted by how much of each source the company uses.

Here is the formula, slowly. WACC equals the weight of debt times the before tax cost of debt times one minus the tax rate, plus the weight of preferred stock times the cost of preferred stock, plus the weight of common equity times the cost of common equity. The weights are each source's share of total capital, and they add up to one. The before tax cost of debt is the yield the market demands today on the company's debt. The tax rate is the marginal tax rate. The cost of preferred stock is the annual preferred dividend divided by the preferred share price.

Why does only debt get the tax adjustment? Interest is tax deductible, so every dollar of interest saves the company the tax rate times that dollar. If the company pays 6 percent and its tax rate is 25 percent, its true after tax cost is 6 times 0.75, or 4.5 percent. Preferred dividends and common dividends are paid out of after tax income, so they get no adjustment. Here is the trap. Applying one minus the tax rate to equity or to preferred, or forgetting to apply it to debt, are the two most common WACC mistakes.

Now a worked example with round numbers. A company targets 40 percent debt and 60 percent equity, with no preferred stock. Its before tax cost of debt is 6 percent, its tax rate is 25 percent, and its cost of equity is 12 percent. The after tax cost of debt is 4.5 percent, and 40 percent of that is 1.8 percent. Sixty percent of 12 percent is 7.2 percent. Add them, and the WACC is 9 percent.

Notice the wrong answer. Forget the tax adjustment and you get 40 percent of 6, which is 2.4, plus 7.2, for 9.6 percent, and that figure will be among the choices. Also, the WACC must land between the after tax cost of debt and the cost of equity, closer to whichever source has the larger weight. If your answer falls outside that range, something went wrong.

How do you interpret the result? The WACC is the minimum return the company must earn on a project of average risk to satisfy every provider of capital, so it is the discount rate for net present value analysis of projects that look like the existing business. Two cautions apply. First, use marginal costs, meaning what new capital would cost today, not the coupon on bonds issued years ago. Second, a project that is riskier or safer than the company as a whole needs a discount rate adjusted for its own risk.

Weights deserve a moment. In theory they should reflect the target capital structure at market values, because that is how the company will raise money going forward. The exam often hands you a debt to equity ratio instead, so convert it. The weight of debt equals the debt to equity ratio divided by one plus the debt to equity ratio. A debt to equity ratio of 0.5 means 1 dollar of debt for every 2 dollars of equity, so debt is 1 dollar out of 3, or one third of capital, not 50 percent.

The second part of this module explains what drives the costs that go into WACC, and what pushes a company toward more or less debt. Top down factors hit every issuer, such as interest rates, inflation, and the state of the economy and the credit markets. Company specific factors include how volatile sales are, how high operating leverage is, how much debt is already outstanding, how well earnings cover interest, and how good the assets are as collateral. The cost of debt and the cost of equity tend to move in the same direction, because both are claims on the same stream of cash flows.

The capital structure choice itself depends on internal and external factors, starting with the internal ones. First, the business model. Predictable revenue and cash flow support more debt, and so do tangible assets, such as real estate or vehicles, that can back a loan. A capital light business whose value lives in software, brands or people has less to pledge, so it usually borrows less. Second, existing leverage, because a company that already carries a lot of debt pays more for the next dollar it borrows.

Third, the corporate tax rate. A higher marginal tax rate makes the interest tax shield more valuable, which favors debt, while a company with losses and no taxable income gets little benefit from deducting interest. Fourth, the company's own capital structure policies, such as a maximum debt to capital ratio or a minimum interest coverage ratio. Closely related are third party debt ratings, because a lower rating raises the cost of borrowing, so many companies manage their debt to protect a target rating.

The external factors are three. Market conditions and the business cycle matter, because credit spreads widen and lenders grow cautious in downturns. Regulation matters in some industries, since banks and insurers face minimum capital requirements and utility regulators often influence how much debt a utility carries. Industry norms matter too, because lenders, rating agencies and analysts benchmark a company against its peers. For a bank or credit union, regulatory capital minimums play exactly this role, so part of the capital structure decision is set by the rules rather than chosen.

Finally, the company life cycle ties these factors together. A start up has little or no revenue, negative cash flow and high business risk, so it relies on equity from founders and venture capital, and debt, if available at all, is scarce and expensive. A growth company has rising revenue and improving cash flow, and its business risk is falling, so it can begin to use debt, though equity is still the main source. A mature company has slower but steady growth and positive, predictable cash flow, so it can borrow cheaply on an unsecured basis and typically uses the most debt. Mature companies also tend to return cash to shareholders through dividends and share buybacks.

Here is the trap. Asked how a fast growing technology company most likely finances itself, many candidates reach for debt because it is cheaper. But cheap debt only helps if cash flows are stable enough to service it, so the growth company most likely leans on equity. Hold on to the core formula. WACC equals the weight of debt times the before tax cost of debt times one minus the tax rate, plus the weight of preferred times its cost, plus the weight of equity times its cost.

## Module 25.2, Capital Structure Theories

The starting point is the work of Modigliani and Miller, whose propositions set the baseline for whether the mix of debt and equity can create value. From here on I will call them M and M. They began with a perfect world, and the assumptions matter because every later theory comes from relaxing one of them.

The assumptions are these. There are no taxes, no transaction costs and no bankruptcy costs. Investors all have the same information and expectations, and they can borrow and lend at the risk free rate. There are no agency costs, and the company's investment decisions are not affected by how it finances them.

M and M Proposition 1, without taxes, says capital structure is irrelevant. The value of a levered company equals the value of an otherwise identical unlevered company. Think of a pizza. Cutting it into more slices does not make more pizza, and splitting operating cash flows between lenders and owners does not create more cash flow. If a levered company sold for more, investors could create the same payoffs more cheaply by buying the unlevered company's shares with borrowed money, so the price gap could not last. This idea is called homemade leverage.

M and M Proposition 2, without taxes, explains what happens to the cost of equity. Debt is cheaper than equity, but adding debt makes the remaining equity riskier, so shareholders demand more. Here is the formula. The cost of equity equals the unlevered cost of capital, plus the difference between the unlevered cost of capital and the cost of debt, times the debt to equity ratio. The unlevered cost of capital is what the company's capital would cost if it were financed entirely with equity, so it reflects business risk only. The cost of equity rises in a straight line as the debt to equity ratio rises.

Try it with round numbers. Say the unlevered cost of capital is 10 percent, the cost of debt is 6 percent, and the company moves to a debt to equity ratio of 1, so half debt and half equity. The cost of equity becomes 10 plus 4 times 1, or 14 percent. Now compute the WACC with no taxes. Half of 6 is 3, half of 14 is 7, and 3 plus 7 is 10 percent, exactly the unlevered cost. Cheaper debt was offset, point for point, by more expensive equity, so the WACC and the company's value did not change.

Now add corporate taxes. Interest is deductible, so debt shields income from tax, and less of the company's cash flow goes to the government. M and M Proposition 1 with taxes says the value of a levered company equals the value of the unlevered company plus the tax rate times the value of debt. That second term is the present value of the interest tax shield, assuming the debt is permanent. For example, an unlevered company is worth 100 million dollars, it adds 40 million dollars of permanent debt, and the tax rate is 25 percent. The levered value is 100 million plus 10 million, or 110 million dollars.

With taxes, the cost of equity still rises as debt is added, but more slowly, because the risk premium term is multiplied by one minus the tax rate. In the earlier example with a 25 percent tax rate, the cost of equity rises only to 13 percent instead of 14, and the WACC falls from 10 percent to 8.75 percent. Keep adding debt and the WACC keeps falling, so taken literally, M and M with taxes says the best capital structure is 100 percent debt. Here is the trap. That conclusion is not a recommendation. It shows what is missing from the model, which is the cost of getting into financial trouble.

Costs of financial distress come in two kinds. Direct costs are the legal, administrative and advisory fees of restructuring or bankruptcy. Indirect costs are often larger, because customers walk away, suppliers tighten terms, key employees leave, managers are distracted, and good investment opportunities are passed up. What matters for value is the expected cost, which is the probability of distress times the cost if it happens. The probability rises with leverage. The cost is lower for companies with tangible, easily sold assets, and higher for companies whose value depends on intangibles, reputation and people.

Put the tax shield and distress costs together and you get the static trade off theory. The value of a levered company equals the value of the unlevered company, plus the tax rate times debt, minus the present value of the expected costs of financial distress. At low debt levels, each extra dollar of debt adds more in tax savings than in expected distress costs, so value rises. Beyond some point the distress costs grow faster, and value falls. The optimal capital structure is the debt level where the two effects balance at the margin.

Here is the key link back to WACC. The optimal capital structure maximizes the value of the company, and the same structure minimizes the WACC. Value and WACC are two views of the same thing, so value peaks exactly where WACC bottoms out. Plot the WACC against leverage and you get a U shaped curve, falling at first because of cheap after tax debt, then rising as both the cost of debt and the cost of equity climb with distress risk. The trap answer says more debt always lowers the WACC, which is only true in the M and M world with taxes and no distress costs.

Next, the target capital structure. This is the mix of debt and equity that management aims for over time when it raises new money, and ideally it sits at management's best estimate of the optimum. The actual structure drifts around the target for three reasons. First, share prices move, which changes the market value weights every day. Second, issuing securities has economies of scale, so companies raise money in large, lumpy blocks rather than a little of each source every time. Third, managers exploit market conditions, for example by issuing debt when rates are unusually low or equity when the share price looks high.

For WACC, analysts use the target weights. If the company does not disclose a target, you can use its current market value weights, the trend in its capital structure, or the averages of comparable companies. Some companies state their targets in book value terms, but market values are the theoretically preferred basis for WACC weights. A quick example. If debt has a market value of 300 million dollars and equity a market value of 700 million dollars, the weights are 30 percent and 70 percent.

Two more ideas explain real financing behavior. The pecking order theory starts from asymmetric information, the fact that managers know more about the company's prospects than outside investors do. Because of that gap, financing choices send signals, and managers prefer internal funds first, meaning retained earnings, then debt, with new equity last. Issuing new shares sends the worst signal, because investors suspect managers sell stock when they think it is overvalued, so the share price tends to fall on the announcement. Taking on debt, by contrast, suggests management is confident the company can make the fixed payments.

The second idea is agency costs. Managers and shareholders do not always want the same things, and managers with plenty of spare cash may spend it on pet projects or empire building. Debt can discipline them, because required interest and principal payments leave less free cash flow to waste. That is the free cash flow argument, and it suggests some leverage can reduce the agency costs of equity. Debt also creates its own conflict. Shareholders keep the upside but have limited liability on the downside, so a highly levered company may be tempted toward risky bets, which is why lenders demand covenants and higher rates as leverage climbs.

Hold on to the anchors for this module. M and M without taxes says value and WACC do not change with leverage, while the cost of equity rises. With taxes, the value of a levered company equals the unlevered value plus the tax rate times debt. The static trade off theory subtracts the present value of expected financial distress costs, and the optimal structure is where value is highest and WACC is lowest.

## Module 26.1, Business Model Features and Types

A business model is a plain description of how a company makes money. It is not a detailed business plan or a forecast. Analysts care because the business model drives revenue growth, margins, capital needs and risk, and as you just heard, it also shapes how much debt a company can carry.

Think of the model in two halves. The first half is the value proposition, the reason customers choose this company over the alternatives. It answers four questions. Who is the customer, consumers or other businesses, and in which markets? What is offered, and how does it differ from competitors?

The last two questions are where and how it is sold, which is the channel strategy, and how much it costs, which is the pricing strategy. The second half is the value chain, which describes how the company organizes its activities and assets to deliver that proposition at a profit.

Channels first. In a traditional channel, a manufacturer sells to a wholesaler, which sells to a retailer, which sells to the customer. In direct sales, the company sells straight to the customer, which is common when selling to businesses and for complex products. Omnichannel combines physical and digital channels, such as ordering online and picking up in a store. The channel choice decides who owns the customer relationship and how much margin goes to intermediaries.

Pricing has two layers. The first is where to price relative to competitors, at a premium, at parity, or at a discount, and that depends on pricing power. A company selling a commodity is a price taker, while a company with a differentiated product can charge more. The second layer is the method. Value based pricing sets the price by what the product is worth to the customer, while cost based pricing adds a markup to the cost of making it.

Next come the named pricing models, and the skill is matching each name to its mechanism. Price discrimination charges different customers different prices based on their willingness to pay. Its forms include tiered pricing, with prices that vary by volume or by feature level, dynamic pricing, with prices that change with time and demand, such as peak and off peak rates, and auctions or reverse auctions, where bidding sets the price. Bundling sells related products together as a package. Razors and blades sells the base product cheaply and earns the profit on high margin consumables, like a printer sold near cost with expensive ink. Add on pricing sells optional features or upgrades on top of the base product, often at high margins.

Some models are built to win customers quickly. Penetration pricing sets low prices, even accepting losses, to build market share. Freemium gives a basic version away free and charges for a premium version, so some users pay. A hidden revenue model charges users nothing at all and earns its money from someone else, usually advertisers. Here is the trap. Freemium and hidden revenue both look free to the casual user, but under freemium some users pay for the upgrade, while under hidden revenue a third party pays.

Other models offer alternatives to outright ownership. Subscriptions create recurring revenue, which investors value for its predictability. Leasing lets customers use an asset without buying it. Licensing lets another company use intellectual property, such as a patent or a brand, in return for royalties. Franchising grants the right to operate under the franchisor's brand and system in return for fees and royalties, while the franchisee puts up most of the capital. Fractional ownership sells a share of an asset, such as part of a vacation property or an aircraft.

Now the value chain. It is the set of activities inside the company that create value for customers, such as bringing in inputs, operations, shipping to customers, marketing and sales, and after sale service, supported by procurement, technology, human resources and general management. Do not confuse it with the supply chain. The supply chain is the full sequence of steps needed to make and deliver a product, from raw materials to the end customer, and many of those steps are done by other companies. A key value chain choice is what to do in house and what to outsource, which shapes the company's assets, costs and risks.

The test of whether a business model works is profitability, and analysts check it with unit economics, which means revenue and cost per unit, such as per customer, per order or per subscriber. Here is a worked example. A subscription service charges 10 dollars a month, and the variable cost of serving one subscriber is 4 dollars, so each subscriber contributes 6 dollars a month. If fixed costs are 600,000 dollars a month, the service breaks even at 600,000 divided by 6, or 100,000 subscribers. If it spends 60 dollars in marketing to win each new subscriber, that subscriber needs 10 months of contribution just to pay back the cost of signing up.

Now the types of business models. Conventional models follow goods from raw material producers to manufacturers, then to wholesalers and distributors, then to retailers, and they also include service businesses such as banks, insurers and asset managers. Several variations are worth knowing. A private label or contract manufacturer makes products that another company sells under its own brand. A value added reseller distributes complex products and adds installation, customization or support. Licensing and franchising, which you just met as pricing approaches, are also variations on the conventional model.

Technology has enabled newer models. In online commerce, an affiliate marketer earns commissions by sending customers to other sellers. A marketplace connects buyers and sellers, takes a fee on each transaction, and usually holds no inventory. An aggregator looks similar, but it sells the service under its own brand and sets the terms, so customers think of the aggregator rather than the individual provider, as with ride hailing apps.

Platform businesses rely on network effects, which means the product becomes more valuable to each user as more users join. In a one sided network, all users are the same type, as in a messaging app where each new user makes it more useful for everyone else. A multi sided platform connects two or more distinct groups, such as buyers and sellers, or cardholders and merchants, and each group values the platform more as the other group grows. Network effects can create winner take most markets, because the biggest network attracts the most new users. Crowdsourcing is related, since users themselves contribute the content or product, as with an online encyclopedia, open source software or customer reviews. A hybrid model mixes types, such as a retailer that sells its own inventory and also runs a marketplace for outside sellers.

Hold on to the core idea for this module. A business model answers who, what, where, how much and how, with the value proposition covering the first four and the value chain covering how the company delivers at a profit. On pricing questions, match the name to the mechanism, and watch for the freemium versus hidden revenue trap and the value chain versus supply chain trap.

## Recall check

Q: A company has 50 percent debt and 50 percent equity. Its before tax cost of debt is 8 percent, its tax rate is 25 percent, and its cost of equity is 12 percent. What is its WACC?

Answer: 9 percent. The after tax cost of debt is 8 times 0.75, or 6 percent, so half of 6 is 3, half of 12 is 6, and the total is 9 percent. The tempting wrong answer is 10 percent, which forgets the tax deduction on interest.

Q: A company's target debt to equity ratio is 0.25. What weight of debt should you use in its WACC?

Answer: 20 percent. The weight of debt equals the debt to equity ratio divided by one plus that ratio, so 0.25 divided by 1.25 is 0.2. Using 25 percent treats a ratio of debt to equity as if it were a ratio of debt to total capital.

Q: In the M and M world with no taxes, a company replaces some equity with cheaper debt. What happens to its cost of equity, its WACC and its value?

Answer: The cost of equity rises, while the WACC and the company's value stay the same. The extra financial risk raises the return shareholders require by exactly enough to offset the cheaper debt. Capital structure is irrelevant in that world.

Q: Which company most likely has the highest proportion of debt: a start up with no revenue, a fast growing software company, or a mature company with stable cash flows and real estate it can pledge?

Answer: The mature company. Predictable cash flow can service fixed payments and tangible assets can back loans, so lenders offer it cheap debt. The start up and the growth company rely mostly on equity because their cash flows are too uncertain.

Q: Under the pecking order theory, what is the most likely market reaction when a company announces a large issue of new common shares, and why?

Answer: The share price most likely falls. Managers know more than outside investors and prefer internal funds, then debt, with new equity last, so choosing equity suggests they believe the shares are overvalued. Investors read the announcement as a negative signal.

Q: A video service is free to all viewers and earns its revenue from advertisers. Is this a freemium model or a hidden revenue model, and what makes the difference?

Answer: A hidden revenue model. The users pay nothing for any version, and a third party, the advertisers, provides the revenue. Under freemium, users get a basic version free, but some of them pay for a premium version.

## Recap

The WACC equals the weight of debt times the after tax cost of debt, plus the weight of preferred times its cost, plus the weight of equity times its cost, and only debt gets the one minus the tax rate adjustment because only interest is tax deductible. Use target weights at market values, and turn a debt to equity ratio into a weight by dividing it by one plus the ratio. Predictable cash flows, tangible assets, a higher tax rate and maturity all support more debt, while start ups and growth companies lean on equity.

M and M without taxes says capital structure is irrelevant, because the cost of equity rises just enough to keep the WACC constant. With taxes, a levered company is worth the unlevered value plus the tax rate times debt. The static trade off theory subtracts the present value of financial distress costs, and the optimal structure both maximizes value and minimizes the WACC. The pecking order runs from internal funds to debt to new equity, and an equity issue tends to be read as a bad signal. A business model explains who the customers are, what they buy, how they are reached and charged, and how the company delivers, and you should be able to match every pricing model to its description.
