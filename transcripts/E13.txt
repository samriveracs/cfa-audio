LEWIS: Reading 27 covers why analysts study financial statements, the six steps of an analysis, who writes and enforces the accounting rules, and what the filings, notes, management commentary and audit report each tell you. Reading 28 covers when a company may count revenue, through the five step model, and when it counts its costs, including the big choice between capitalizing a cost and expensing it.

## Module 27.1, Financial Statement Roles

HEART: Start with two jobs that sound alike but belong to different people. Picture the owner of a busy coffee shop who wants a bank loan to open a second location. The shop's statements are financial reporting. That is the company's job, showing its performance and financial position to lenders, investors and anyone else with a stake in it.

LEWIS: And the loan officer studying those statements is doing financial statement analysis. That is the user's job, taking the statements, plus any other useful information, and making an economic decision with them. Whether to buy a company's shares or recommend them. Whether to extend credit, either a bank loan or trade credit from a supplier. What credit rating the company's debt deserves.

HEART: In every case the analyst studies past performance and the current position, but the real question points forward. Can this business earn profits and generate cash in the future, and what risks stand in the way? And this is analysis by outsiders. Managers analyze their own company every day, but from internal reports outsiders never see.

LEWIS: The work follows a six step framework. Step one, state the objective and the context: what question, for whom, in what form, and with how much time and how many resources. Step two, gather the data: the statements, industry and economic information, and conversations with management, suppliers and customers, perhaps even a site visit.

HEART: Step three, process the data. Clean up the statements so they compare fairly, compute ratios, run the statistics, and turn the results into charts and common size statements. Step four, analyze and interpret. Now the numbers go to work on the question from step one, and the analyst settles on the conclusions the evidence can carry.

LEWIS: Step five, report the conclusions or recommendations to the intended audience, in a way that complies with the Code and Standards. Step six, update the analysis. Run through the steps again as new results come in, and revise the conclusion when the facts move.

HEART: Here is the trap. Calculating ratios feels like analysis, but it is step three, processing. Deciding what those ratios mean is step four. You cannot interpret a number you have not calculated, and you should not calculate anything before you know the question.

LEWIS: Next, who writes the rules and who enforces them. Think of a sport, with a rules committee and referees. The rule writers are standard setting bodies, private sector boards that decide how transactions should be reported. The referees are regulatory authorities, government agencies that can force companies to follow those rules and penalize them when they do not.

HEART: The two main rule writers are the Financial Accounting Standards Board, F A S B, which sets US GAAP, the generally accepted accounting principles of the United States, and the International Accounting Standards Board, I A S B, which sets IFRS, the International Financial Reporting Standards used across most of the world. Some of the international board's older standards still carry the earlier name, International Accounting Standards.

LEWIS: The referees are national regulators, such as the Securities and Exchange Commission, the S E C, in the United States, and the Financial Conduct Authority in the United Kingdom. Most of them belong to IOSCO, the International Organization of Securities Commissions. IOSCO itself is not a regulator. It is an association of regulators, working to make regulation and enforcement more consistent across borders.

HEART: Its three objectives are to protect investors, keep markets fair, efficient and transparent, and reduce systemic risk. To get there, it expects companies to give full, accurate and timely disclosure, and it expects the accounting standards behind their statements to be high quality and internationally accepted.

LEWIS: The S E C also enforces the Sarbanes Oxley Act of two thousand two, passed after a wave of accounting scandals. Three pieces matter. It bars a company's outside auditor from selling it certain other paid services, because an auditor earning large consulting fees from a client has a reason to go easy on that client. It makes top management certify that the statements are presented fairly and report on how effective the internal controls over financial reporting are. And it requires the outside auditor to confirm whether those controls are effective.

HEART: Now the S E C filings. Tie each one to its trigger. Form S one is the registration statement, filed before a company sells new securities to the public, with audited statements, the risks, the underwriters, and how much it expects to raise and what for.

LEWIS: Form ten K is the annual filing, with the business, its risks, its management, legal matters, and the full audited statements and disclosures. The glossy annual report sent to shareholders is similar, but it does not replace the ten K. Foreign issuers file Form forty F if they are Canadian and Form twenty F otherwise.

HEART: Form ten Q is the quarterly filing. Its interim statements, unlike the annual ones, do not have to be audited. Foreign issuers commonly send in their half year results on Form six K instead. Form eight K is the event filing, for anything material, such as buying or selling a significant business, a change in management or governance, or a problem involving the auditors or the statements. When you hear eight K, think breaking news.

LEWIS: The proxy statement goes to shareholders before a vote and is filed as Form D E F fourteen A. It covers board candidates and their qualifications, executive pay and stock options. Form one forty four is a notice about a planned sale of securities that were never registered, such as shares first placed privately with qualified buyers. And Forms three, four and five track holdings and trades by officers and directors, so analysts can watch what insiders do with their own money.

HEART: In the European Union, each member state has its own securities regulator, listed companies report under IFRS, and the European Securities and Markets Authority helps coordinate regulation across the union.

LEWIS: Now the notes to the financial statements, often called footnotes. The statements are summaries, and the notes carry the detail you need to judge the size, timing and uncertainty of the reported figures. They give the basis of presentation: the period covered, whether IFRS or US GAAP was followed, and which entities are consolidated. They explain the accounting methods, assumptions and estimates management chose. And they add detail on acquisitions and disposals, lawsuits, employee benefit plans, contingencies and commitments, significant customers, related party dealings, segment results, and significant events after the balance sheet date.

HEART: Picture a coffee roaster whose note on significant customers reveals that a single grocery chain buys a third of its output. Nothing on the face of the income statement would tell you that, and it changes how risky those earnings look. And the fact to lock in: the notes are audited along with the primary statements.

LEWIS: One note deserves its own picture, segment reporting, which both IFRS and US GAAP require. Imagine a company that runs coffee shops and also sells packaged beans through supermarkets. The totals blend two very different businesses, and segment data pull them apart.

HEART: An operating segment, or business segment, is a part of the company that makes up more than ten percent of its revenue, assets or income, and whose risk and return can be told apart from the rest. The segments reported must cover at least seventy five percent of the company's sales to outside customers. Geographic segments follow the same size test when a region's business environment is different.

LEWIS: For each segment the company discloses items such as revenue, a measure of profit, assets and liabilities, capital spending, depreciation and income tax. That lets you work out segment margins, asset turnover and return on assets, and forecast each business at its own growth rate. The trap is assuming you get everything. Segment disclosures are only a subset of what the company reports as a whole.

HEART: Next, management commentary, also called the management report, the operating and financial review, or management's discussion and analysis, M D and A. It is management explaining the numbers in its own words, and often one of the most useful parts of the annual report.

LEWIS: IFRS guidance recommends that it cover the nature of the business, management's objectives, past performance, the performance measures management relies on, and the key relationships, resources and risks. In the United States, the S E C requires it to discuss trends, significant events and uncertainties affecting liquidity, capital resources and results of operations. It must also cover material effects of inflation, off balance sheet and contractual obligations such as purchase commitments, accounting policies that require significant judgment, and planned capital spending and divestitures.

HEART: And the trap. Parts of management commentary may be unaudited, while the notes are audited. Treat the commentary as an informed but interested point of view.

LEWIS: Now the audit. Picture a home inspection before you buy a house. The inspector does not guarantee the house, but checks enough to tell you whether anything serious is wrong.

HEART: An audit is an independent review of a company's financial statements by an outside accounting firm, and the point of it is an opinion on whether those statements can be relied on as fair. The auditor, engaged by the board of directors, tests the company's accounting and control systems, checks that the assets and liabilities really exist, and hunts for material errors.

LEWIS: The standard opinion makes three points. First, the statements are prepared by management and are management's responsibility, and the auditor has reviewed them independently. Second, the audit followed generally accepted auditing standards, which give reasonable assurance that the statements contain no material errors. Third, the statements follow the accepted accounting principles, and the principles chosen and the estimates made are reasonable. If methods were not applied consistently from one period to the next, the report must explain that too.

HEART: Notice the word reasonable. Auditors test samples, and many numbers rest on judgment. So a clean opinion is not a guarantee against fraud, and it says nothing about whether the business is a good one.

LEWIS: There are four opinions. An unqualified opinion, also called unmodified or clean, says the auditor found no material errors or omissions. A qualified opinion says the statements are fair except for specific departures from the accounting principles, which the report explains. A limit on the audit's scope that touches only one area can also result in a qualified opinion. An adverse opinion says the statements as a whole are not presented fairly, because the departures from the standards are material and widespread. And a disclaimer of opinion means the auditor cannot express an opinion at all, for example because a scope limitation kept it from the evidence it needed. Any opinion other than unqualified is called a modified opinion.

HEART: Back to the home inspection. Clean means the house is sound. Qualified means sound, except for the roof. Adverse means the house is not sound. And a disclaimer means the inspector could not get inside, so there is no verdict either way.

LEWIS: Here is the trap. Adverse and disclaimer both sound bad, but they mean different things. Adverse means the auditor looked and found the statements materially wrong. Disclaimer means the auditor could not finish looking.

HEART: The report also carries an explanatory paragraph when a significant loss looks likely but nobody can yet put a reliable number on it. That can concern the going concern assumption, the assumption that the company will keep operating for the foreseeable future, or whether assets can be valued or turned into cash at their reported amounts, or the outcome of a lawsuit. A paragraph like that can signal serious trouble.

LEWIS: Then internal controls, the processes a company relies on to make sure its statements are accurate. Maintaining them is management's responsibility. For larger public companies in the United States, the auditor must also give an opinion on internal controls, either separately or as a fourth element of the standard opinion.

HEART: Finally, for listed companies the audit report must describe the matters the auditor considered most significant. Internationally these are key audit matters. In the United States they are critical audit matters. They are usually areas built on significant management judgment and estimates, significant transactions, or choices the auditor found especially subjective, which are the places most likely to be misstated. For a bank or credit union, the allowance for credit losses on its loans is a common example, because it rests on judgments about defaults that have not happened yet. These matters are a map of where to look hardest.

LEWIS: Now the two reporting systems. The boards work together to align their standards, but real differences remain. US GAAP is often described as rules based and IFRS as principles based. Picture one rulebook that spells out exact thresholds, and another that states the goal and expects judgment in applying it.

HEART: A handful of differences show up right away. LIFO, the last in first out inventory method, is allowed under US GAAP and prohibited under IFRS. Product development costs are expensed under US GAAP but may be capitalized under IFRS. Interest paid is an operating cash flow under US GAAP, but can be operating or financing under IFRS. And if inventory is written down and its value later recovers, IFRS allows the write down to be reversed, while US GAAP prohibits it. So when you compare an IFRS reporter with a US GAAP reporter, ask whether a gap reflects the business or just the accounting.

LEWIS: Standards also keep changing. They exist to make different companies' statements comparable and to tighten the band of estimates that count as reasonable, and a new standard can move reported numbers with no change in the business. New kinds of transactions may not fit existing standards, and the reporting framework, the underlying principles, guides how they will likely be treated. Analysts keep current through professional journals, the two boards' websites, CFA Institute position papers, and each company's disclosures of its significant policies and estimates.

HEART: Last, sources beyond the annual and interim reports, in four groups. Issuer sources come from the company itself: earnings calls with question and answer sessions and often earnings guidance, other presentations, press releases, and conversations with management and investor relations. These are unlikely to have been audited.

LEWIS: Public third party sources include free industry reports, white papers, trade journals, government statistics, the media and social media. Proprietary third party sources are the paid ones: analyst reports, data platforms, and industry agencies and consultants.

HEART: And proprietary primary research is the analyst's own legwork: commissioned studies, hands on experience with the company's products, and advice from technical specialists on the team. For a coffee chain, that could mean standing in a few of its shops during the morning rush and counting the line. Around all of it sits the wider context of the economy, the industry and the competitors.

LEWIS: The takeaway. Analysis follows six steps, from stating the objective to updating the conclusion. Standard setters write the rules and regulators enforce them. The notes are audited, while management commentary and other company sources may not be. And the four audit opinions are clean, qualified, adverse and disclaimer.

## Module 28.1, Revenue Recognition

HEART: Start back at the coffee shop, in December. A customer buys a gift card as a holiday present. Cash comes in, but the shop has not earned anything yet. It still owes that customer coffee. So the shop books the cash along with a liability called unearned revenue, sometimes called deferred revenue. In January the card gets used, the liability shrinks, and revenue finally appears.

LEWIS: Now flip it. In December the shop caters an office party on credit and gets paid in January. The revenue belongs to December, when the shop did the work, and the shop carries an asset, an account receivable, until the cash arrives.

HEART: That is the accrual principle. Revenue is recognized in the period it is earned, which is not necessarily the period the cash is collected. A plain cash sale with no right of return is the easy case, where revenue and cash arrive together. And revenue is reported net of returns and allowances, such as customer discounts.

LEWIS: Why have the rule at all? Because if revenue followed cash, profit would track payment habits instead of business activity. A company could look brilliant in any quarter when customers happened to pay in advance, and terrible when they paid late. Accrual accounting ties revenue to the work actually done.

HEART: IFRS and US GAAP now have converged revenue standards, nearly identical and built on principles rather than detailed rules. Its core idea is that revenue arrives when a promised good or service is handed over, meaning the customer gains control of it. That is the old idea of revenue when earned, made sharper, and the standard puts it to work in five steps.

LEWIS: Step one, identify the contract with the customer. Step two, identify the separate, distinct performance obligations in the contract. Step three, determine the transaction price. Step four, allocate that price to the performance obligations. Step five, recognize revenue when, or as, each performance obligation is satisfied.

HEART: Step one first. A contract is an agreement between two or more parties that creates rights and obligations each side can enforce. For a contract to exist, it must be probable that the seller will collect what it is owed. Here is a subtle difference worth knowing. Probable is defined differently under the two frameworks, with US GAAP setting the higher bar, so the same deal could pass the test under IFRS and fail it under US GAAP.

LEWIS: Step two, performance obligations. A performance obligation is a promise to deliver a distinct good or service. Something is distinct when two things are true. The customer can benefit from it on its own, or together with other resources that are readily available. And the promise to deliver it can be identified separately from the other promises in the contract.

HEART: Picture a new car sold with three years of scheduled maintenance included. The car is useful on its own, and the maintenance is a separate promise kept over time. Two performance obligations. The bolts holding the car together are not a separate promise, because they are just parts built into the car, not something promised on their own.

LEWIS: Step three, the transaction price, what the seller expects to be entitled to for the goods or services it promises. Often that is a set figure, but part of it can be variable, with bonuses, rebates, penalties or the customer's right to return goods.

HEART: And here is the guard rail. A company books revenue only to the extent it is highly probable that no significant part of it will later have to be reversed. Picture an online shoe store that offers free returns and knows from experience that a share of every month's orders comes back. It does not count all the shipped shoes as revenue. For the pairs likely to come back, it carries a liability for the money it expects to hand back, along with an asset for its right to recover those shoes.

HEART: Step four, allocation. When a contract has more than one performance obligation, the price has to be split among them. The usual basis is each item's standalone selling price, what the company would charge for it sold separately, so any bundle discount is spread across the items in proportion. For the car, part of the price goes to the maintenance package, and that part waits as a liability until the services are performed.

LEWIS: Step five, recognition. A performance obligation is satisfied when control of the good or service passes from seller to buyer. The signs that the customer has control include physical possession, the customer's acceptance, the customer bearing the risks and enjoying the rewards of ownership, the customer holding legal title, and the seller having a right to payment. Driving the car off the lot ticks most of those boxes at once.

HEART: Some obligations are satisfied at a single point in time, like that car. Others are satisfied over time, and the standard gives three tests. Meet any one of them and revenue is recognized over time.

LEWIS: Test one, the customer receives and benefits from the service as the company performs. A software subscription that customers reach over the internet works this way, and so does a cleaning or maintenance contract. Every month of service delivered is a month of benefit received.

HEART: Test two, the company's work creates or improves an asset that the customer controls while it is being built. Picture a contractor building a new gym on a school district's own land. The district controls the building as each wall goes up.

LEWIS: Test three, the asset has no alternative use to the supplier, and the supplier has an enforceable right to payment for the work completed so far. Picture a shipyard building a ferry to one city's exact design, under a contract that pays the shipyard for the work done so far even if the city cancels. The shipyard could not sell that ferry to anyone else without major changes, but it can enforce payment for its progress.

HEART: When revenue is recognized over time, the company has to measure its progress toward completion. Input methods measure effort, most often the costs incurred to date as a share of the total costs expected. Output methods measure results, such as engineering milestones reached or the share of total output delivered. If the gym's builder has spent about a third of the costs it expects in total, it recognizes about a third of the contract's revenue. Accountants used to call this the percentage of completion method. The current standard keeps the idea but drops the old label.

LEWIS: And the trap. Progress follows the work, not the billing schedule and not the cash. Billing dates are negotiated, and they say little about how much of the job is done.

HEART: That gap between work and billing shows up on the balance sheet. If the customer pays ahead of the work, as with the gift card, the company carries a contract liability. If the work runs ahead of what it can bill, it carries a contract asset, which becomes a receivable once the right to payment is unconditional.

LEWIS: The revenue standard also has one cost rule. When winning a long term contract costs money up front, such as the commission a salesperson earns for signing a business customer to a three year software subscription, that cost goes on the balance sheet as an asset and is expensed over the three years, alongside the revenue it helped win.

HEART: Now principal versus agent, which changes the size of reported revenue without changing profit. Picture a food delivery app. It connects diners with restaurants, but it does not cook the food, never owns it, and is not the party responsible for providing the meal. It bears no inventory risk. That makes it an agent, so it reports only its fee as revenue. That is net reporting.

LEWIS: Compare a grocery store that buys food, holds it on its shelves, sets the price and bears the risk of spoilage. That is a principal. It reports the full sale price as revenue and the cost of the food as an expense. That is gross reporting.

HEART: Now suppose a principal and an agent each make the same gross profit on a sale. The principal shows far more revenue and a thin gross margin. The agent shows a small revenue line and a gross margin that can be as high as one hundred percent. Same profit, very different picture.

LEWIS: The trap is comparing revenue growth or price to sales ratios across a principal and an agent as if the revenue meant the same thing. And watch for a company that switches its judgment from agent to principal. Revenue jumps overnight, and nothing about the business has changed.

HEART: A few applications round out the module. Franchising first. Picture a fitness chain that runs some gyms itself and licenses its brand to franchisees who run the rest. A franchisee pays an upfront fee for the right to operate for many years, so the chain treats that fee as deferred revenue and recognizes it gradually over the franchise period. Ongoing royalties, a share of each franchisee's sales, are recognized as they become payable under the contract.

LEWIS: The chain must also break its revenue into categories with similar nature, amount, timing and risk, such as membership dues at the gyms it runs itself, personal training packages, and the fees and royalties paid by franchisees. Those streams behave very differently in a downturn, which is exactly why they are disaggregated.

HEART: Next, software, where the question is license or service. Picture a company selling design software three ways. In the first, customers log in through the internet and never take possession of the software. That is a service, recognized over the life of the contract. In the second, customers install it themselves, but the company keeps changing the software for the whole license term, every change lands on the customer whether it helps or hurts, and those changes are not delivered as separate products. Under IFRS, that revenue is also recognized over the life of the contract.

LEWIS: In the third, the customer buys the right to use the software as is, frozen in its day one version, and any updates come under a separate support contract. Then the license revenue is recognized at the outset, and the support revenue as the support is provided. Same product, but revenue arrives up front or spread out depending on what was promised, which can make one software company look like it is growing much faster than another.

HEART: Last application, bill and hold. Picture a furniture maker that finishes custom booths for a restaurant whose new dining room is not ready, so the restaurant asks the maker to hold them. A customer paying before delivery normally creates deferred revenue. But revenue can be recognized before shipment if the seller has completed its obligations and the customer has control of the goods.

LEWIS: Under IFRS, the customer must have asked for the arrangement, the goods must be identified as that customer's, complete and ready to hand over, and impossible to redirect to another customer. This is also a classic place for revenue to be pulled forward, so a burst of bill and hold sales near a period end deserves a skeptical look.

HEART: Finally, disclosures. Companies must report their contracts with customers by category, contract assets and liabilities with their balances and changes, the performance obligations still outstanding and the price allocated to them, and the management judgments behind the amount and timing of revenue, including any changes in those judgments.

LEWIS: Those judgments are where the analysis happens: how progress toward completion is measured, how much variable consideration is included and how likely it is to be earned, how contract revisions are handled, and whether the company is a principal or an agent. Compare those choices with peers.

HEART: The takeaway. Revenue is recognized when control passes to the customer, through five steps: contract, performance obligations, transaction price, allocation and recognition. Cash in advance is a liability, not revenue. And principal or agent changes reported revenue without changing gross profit.

## Module 28.2, Expense Recognition

LEWIS: Revenue tells you when sales count. Expenses tell you when costs count, and together they set the profit in any period. Start with the definition. An expense is a decrease in economic benefits during the period, either because assets flow out or are used up, or because liabilities are taken on, which lowers equity. The one exclusion is a distribution to the owners.

HEART: So when the coffee shop pays its electricity bill, that is an expense. When it pays its owner a dividend, equity falls just the same, but that is a distribution, not an expense.

LEWIS: If companies kept their books on a cash basis, this would be easy, since every payment would be an expense the day it left the bank. Accrual accounting asks a harder question, which period actually used up the benefit of the spending, and puts the expense there. Three routes answer it: the matching principle, expensing as incurred, and capitalization.

HEART: Matching first. Costs that generate revenue are recognized in the same period as that revenue. Picture the coffee shop buying a large shipment of beans that will last three months. The beans do not hit the income statement the day they arrive. Each month, only the cost of the beans actually brewed and sold that month becomes cost of goods sold, sitting right next to that month's coffee sales. The rest waits on the balance sheet as inventory.

LEWIS: Why bother? Because otherwise the month of a big delivery would look dreadful and the next two months would look wonderful, and profit would reflect the purchasing schedule instead of the business.

HEART: The inventory arithmetic is simple in words. Beginning inventory plus purchases gives the goods available for sale. Whatever is not sold by the end of the period stays on the balance sheet as ending inventory, a current asset, and becomes the next period's beginning inventory. Cost of goods sold is the cost of the units actually sold.

LEWIS: Which cost, though, when prices change during the period? If each item can be tracked individually, like paintings in a gallery, each with its own purchase price, the company can use specific identification. When it cannot, it applies a cost flow method: first in first out, FIFO, last in first out, LIFO, or weighted average cost. The details come in the inventories reading. For now, remember that LIFO is not allowed under IFRS.

HEART: Warranties follow the same matching logic. Picture a shop selling electric bikes with a two year warranty. The repairs will happen later, but the cost of honoring the warranty is estimated and expensed in the period of the sale, when the revenue is recognized, not when a customer brings a bike back.

LEWIS: Bad debts work the same way. When the coffee shop caters on credit, some customers will never pay. The expected loss is estimated and expensed in the period of the sale, rather than waiting for a customer to default. A lender's allowance for credit losses rests on the same logic, on a much larger scale.

HEART: The second route is expensing as incurred. Some costs cannot be tied to particular sales. The manager's salary, the bookkeeping fees, the insurance on the shop. These are period costs, and they are expensed in the period they are incurred. The bookkeeper's work in December belongs on December's income statement, even if the invoice is not paid until January.

LEWIS: The third route is capitalization, which is really matching stretched over many years. A cost is first put on the balance sheet as an asset, then expensed gradually as its benefits are used up. Property, plant and equipment work this way, and so do intangible assets with finite lives.

HEART: Before the details, one piece of vocabulary. A policy that pushes expenses into the future is called aggressive, because it flatters current profit. A policy that pulls them forward is called conservative. Since expensing takes the whole cost at once, it is the more conservative choice.

LEWIS: So when should a cost be capitalized? Spending is capitalized when it should keep paying off over several accounting periods. When that future payoff is unlikely or too uncertain to count on, the cost is expensed when incurred.

HEART: Picture the coffee shop buying a new espresso machine. The asset goes on the balance sheet at its cost, which is typically its fair value when acquired plus whatever it takes to get it ready for use. So the purchase price, the delivery charge, the sales tax and the plumber who connects it all go into the asset.

LEWIS: What about training the staff to use it?

HEART: Expensed. Training gets the people ready, not the machine. That distinction is a favorite trap. Then, over the machine's life, routine cleaning and repairs that merely keep it working are expensed as they happen. But if, a few years in, the shop rebuilds the boiler and adds years to the machine's useful life, that spending creates more future benefit, so it is capitalized.

LEWIS: Once capitalized, the cost is spread over the asset's life, and the name depends on the asset. Depreciation for tangible assets like the machine. Depletion for natural resources, like a quarry or an oil field. Amortization for intangible assets with finite lives, like a patent. Each charge lowers the asset's carrying value, also called net book value, and lowers net income. Two exceptions are never spread out at all: land, and intangible assets with indefinite lives, such as goodwill from an acquisition.

HEART: Now the heavily tested part, the effect of capitalizing versus expensing. Picture two identical coffee chains that each spend the same large sum on new equipment. One capitalizes it. The other, for the sake of comparison, expenses it all at once.

LEWIS: Start with the income statement. The expensing chain takes the whole hit in the first year, so its profit drops sharply. The capitalizing chain shows only a slice as depreciation. In later years it flips. The expensing chain has no further charge, so its profit is higher, while the capitalizing chain keeps charging depreciation. Over the life of the equipment, total expense is identical. Capitalizing changes when the cost shows up, not how much, and it gives smoother profit from year to year.

HEART: Next, the balance sheet. The capitalizing chain carries the equipment as an asset, so its total assets are higher, and because its early profit was higher, its retained earnings and equity are higher too. The gap narrows every year as the asset is depreciated and the expensing chain's higher later profits catch up, and by the end of the asset's life the two balance sheets match.

LEWIS: Then the cash flow statement, home of the classic trap. Both chains paid exactly the same cash. The capitalizing chain shows the payment as an investing outflow. The expensing chain shows it as an operating outflow. So capitalizing gives higher operating cash flow and lower investing cash flow. Ignoring taxes, total cash flow is the same.

HEART: And with taxes?

LEWIS: If the tax deduction follows the accounting, the expensing chain gets its whole deduction in the first year, so it pays less tax up front and more later. If anything, expensing brings the cash benefit sooner. Over the asset's whole life, total cash is the same either way. So any answer saying capitalizing creates cash is wrong.

HEART: Now the ratios. Higher assets and higher equity mean the capitalizer's debt to assets and debt to equity ratios are lower, so it looks less leveraged. Its total asset turnover, sales divided by assets, is lower, because the asset base is bigger. Its net profit margin is higher in the first year and lower afterward. And its return on assets and return on equity are higher in the first year, then lower in later years, when its profit is lower and its asset and equity bases are larger.

LEWIS: One extension. That comparison is a single purchase. A company that keeps capitalizing year after year, like a chain opening new shops every year, keeps the profit boost going, as long as the amount it capitalizes each period is larger than the depreciation running off the earlier spending.

HEART: So this one choice quietly reduces comparability. Identical businesses can show different profit, assets, leverage and operating cash flow, so identify how significant spending is treated before comparing companies.

LEWIS: A special case is capitalized interest. Picture the coffee chain borrowing to build its own roasting plant, a project that takes well over a year. The interest that builds up during construction is added to the cost of the plant. The same applies, in limited circumstances, to assets built for resale. The logic is that financing the build is part of what the plant really cost, and that cost belongs in the years the plant is roasting beans, not while it is still a construction site. IFRS and US GAAP treat construction interest in similar ways.

HEART: Follow it through the statements. Capitalized interest never appears as interest expense. It reaches the income statement later, through depreciation if the asset is held for use, or through cost of goods sold if it is held for sale. In the cash flow statement, it sits in investing cash flow. Ordinary interest paid is an operating outflow under US GAAP, and either operating or financing under IFRS. So where interest paid would otherwise count as operating, capitalizing it makes operating cash flow look higher and investing cash flow lower, by the same amount.

LEWIS: For the analyst, the fix is in the interest coverage ratio, earnings before interest and taxes divided by interest expense. To see true solvency, add the interest capitalized this period back into interest expense. And add back to earnings the depreciation that comes from interest capitalized in earlier years. The adjusted coverage ratio usually comes out weaker, because the full interest burden is now counted.

HEART: Next, research and development. When a company builds an intangible asset itself, the spending is usually expensed as it happens. The two big exceptions are development costs under IFRS and the cost of developing software. Under IFRS, research, the search for new scientific or technical knowledge, is expensed as incurred. Development, the stage that turns those findings into a design or plan for a new product or process, may be capitalized, provided the company can show, among other criteria, that it can complete the asset and intends to use or sell it.

LEWIS: Under US GAAP, both research and development are generally expensed. The best known exception is software being developed to sell to others. Picture a company writing ordering software it plans to sell to coffee shops. Until the product is shown to be technologically feasible, the spending is expensed. From that point on, the cost of finishing it into something sellable is capitalized. So that one case under US GAAP looks a lot like development costs under IFRS.

HEART: To compare a company that capitalizes development costs with one that expenses them, put them on the same footing. On the income statement, charge all of this period's development spending as an expense, and take out the amortization of amounts capitalized in earlier years. On the balance sheet, remove the capitalized development costs, which lowers assets and equity. And on the cash flow statement, move the spending out of investing and into operating, which lowers operating cash flow.

LEWIS: Last, what all of this means for analysis. Expense recognition is full of estimates: bad debts, warranty costs, useful lives, which costs count as development. Estimates give a company room to speed up or delay expenses, and delaying them raises current profit, which is the aggressive direction.

HEART: So when an estimate moves, find the reason. A falling bad debt charge might mean customers really are paying more reliably, or a management team reaching for a better quarter. Peer comparison helps too. A warranty charge well below rivals' could mean sturdier products, or just thinner provisions. The notes and management's discussion and analysis are where those policies and estimates are laid out.

LEWIS: The takeaway. Expenses follow matching, period costs are expensed as incurred, and capitalization spreads a cost over the years it helps. Compared with expensing, capitalizing raises assets, equity, early profit and operating cash flow, and lowers investing cash flow and leverage ratios, while total cash and lifetime profit stay the same.

## Recall check

LEWIS Q: An analyst has just computed a set of ratios and common size statements for a company. Which step of the analysis framework is that?

HEART: Answer. Step three, processing the data. Here is why. Processing turns raw statements into usable exhibits, through adjustments, ratios, statistics and charts. Deciding what those numbers mean for the question in step one is the next step, analyzing and interpreting. Calculation comes before judgment.

HEART Q: Is IOSCO a standard setter, a regulator, or neither?

LEWIS: Answer. Neither, it is an association of national regulators. Here is why. Standard setters like the two accounting boards write the rules, and regulators like the S E C have the legal power to enforce them. IOSCO brings regulators together to make regulation and enforcement more consistent across borders, around three objectives: protecting investors, keeping markets fair, efficient and transparent, and reducing systemic risk.

LEWIS Q: In an annual report, which is audited, the notes to the financial statements or management's commentary?

HEART: Answer. The notes. Here is why. The notes are an integral part of the financial statements, so the auditor's opinion covers them along with the primary statements. Management commentary is management explaining results in its own words, and parts of it may be unaudited, so it deserves more skepticism.

HEART Q: A company's records were destroyed in a fire, and the auditor cannot gather enough evidence to form a view. Which opinion fits, and why not an adverse opinion?

LEWIS: Answer. A disclaimer of opinion. Here is why. A disclaimer means the auditor is unable to express any opinion, often because of a scope limitation like this one. An adverse opinion requires the auditor to have looked and concluded that the statements are not presented fairly. Here the auditor could not finish looking, so it cannot conclude anything either way.

LEWIS Q: Under which framework may a company capitalize product development costs and reverse an earlier inventory write down?

HEART: Answer. IFRS. Here is why. IFRS lets development costs be capitalized once certain criteria are met, such as the ability to complete the asset and the intent to use or sell it, while US GAAP generally expenses research and development. IFRS also allows an inventory write down to be reversed if the value recovers, which US GAAP prohibits.

HEART Q: A customer pays upfront for a full year of a software subscription. What does the company show on the day the cash arrives?

LEWIS: Answer. Cash and an equal liability for unearned revenue, with no revenue yet. Here is why. Revenue is recognized when the service is transferred, and the customer receives the service month by month over the year. So the liability shrinks and revenue appears as each month of service is delivered.

LEWIS Q: A food delivery app never owns the meals and is not responsible for providing them. Compared with reporting as a principal, what happens to its revenue, gross profit and gross margin?

HEART: Answer. Revenue is lower, gross profit is the same, and gross margin is higher. Here is why. As an agent, it reports only its fee as revenue, which is net reporting. A principal would report the full price as revenue and the cost of the meal as an expense, which leaves the same gross profit on a much bigger revenue base.

HEART Q: A shipyard builds a ferry to one city's exact design, and the contract entitles it to payment for work completed to date. Is revenue recognized at a point in time or over time?

LEWIS: Answer. Over time. Here is why. One of the three tests for over time recognition is met: the asset has no alternative use to the shipyard, and the shipyard has an enforceable right to payment for work done so far. Revenue then follows progress toward completion, measured by inputs such as costs incurred or outputs such as milestones, not by billing dates.

LEWIS Q: An online retailer with free returns knows some orders always come back. Why does it not count every shipped order as revenue?

HEART: Answer. Because revenue counts only to the extent it is highly probable that no significant part of it will be reversed later. Here is why. The orders it expects back are variable consideration that will likely be refunded. So for those, the retailer carries a liability for the money it expects to hand back and an asset for its right to the returned goods, instead of revenue that would later have to be reversed.

HEART Q: A company buys a machine and pays for delivery, installation and staff training. Which of those costs are capitalized?

LEWIS: Answer. Delivery and installation are capitalized, and training is expensed. Here is why. An asset's cost includes everything needed to get the asset ready for its intended use. Training gets the employees ready, not the machine, so it is expensed when incurred.

LEWIS Q: Compared with expensing a cost, what does capitalizing it do to operating cash flow and to the debt to equity ratio?

HEART: Answer. Operating cash flow is higher and debt to equity is lower. Here is why. The payment is classified as an investing outflow instead of an operating outflow, so operating cash flow rises while investing cash flow falls. Capitalizing also puts an asset on the balance sheet and raises early profit, so assets and equity are higher, and the same debt looks smaller against them.

HEART Q: A company capitalized a large amount of interest on a plant under construction. How should an analyst adjust interest coverage, and which way does it usually move?

LEWIS: Answer. Add the capitalized interest back into interest expense, and the coverage ratio usually falls. Here is why. Capitalized interest never shows up as interest expense, so the reported ratio understates the interest burden. Adding it back gives a truer picture of solvency. The analyst also adds back to earnings any depreciation that came from interest capitalized in earlier years, but the larger effect is usually the higher interest figure.

## Key takeaways

HEART: First, a few memory hooks.

LEWIS: For the six step framework: ask, gather, crunch, interpret, report, update. Ratios are crunching, step three, not interpreting.

HEART: For the rule makers: standard setters write the rulebook, regulators referee the game, and IOSCO is the referees' association.

LEWIS: For the filings: ten K, yearly and audited. Ten Q, quarterly and unaudited. Eight K, breaking news. S one, before selling new securities to the public. The proxy, before a vote. Forms three, four and five, the insiders.

HEART: For the audit opinions, the home inspection. Clean is sound. Qualified is sound except for the roof. Adverse is not sound. Disclaimer is could not get inside. And every opinion but clean is a modified opinion.

LEWIS: For the five revenue steps: Coffee Orders Pay All Rent. Contract, obligations, price, allocate, recognize.

HEART: For cash and revenue: cash before revenue is a liability, revenue before cash is a receivable, or a contract asset while payment still depends on more work. For over time revenue, any one of three tests: they benefit as you go, you build or improve something they control as you build it, or it has no other use to you and you have a right to be paid for the work so far.

LEWIS: For principal and agent: control it and report gross, just arrange it and report net. Same gross profit, different revenue.

HEART: For what goes into an asset's cost: getting the asset ready is capitalized, getting the people ready is expensed. Keeping it running is expensed, extending its life is capitalized.

LEWIS: For capitalizing versus expensing: capitalizing moves the cost into later years and moves the cash outflow from operating to investing. Expensing is the conservative choice, and delaying expenses is the aggressive one.

HEART: For research and development: under IFRS, research is expensed and development may be capitalized. Under US GAAP, both are expensed, except software for sale once it reaches technological feasibility.

LEWIS: Now, how you would say the big ideas to a senior leader.

HEART: On the audit: a clean audit opinion tells us the statements are fairly presented in all material respects. It does not tell us the business is healthy, and it cannot rule out fraud entirely. The jargon is an unqualified opinion and reasonable assurance.

LEWIS: On revenue: we count a sale when the customer actually gets what they paid for, not when the money arrives. Cash collected in advance is a promise we still owe. The jargon is transfer of control and unearned revenue.

HEART: On gross versus net: two companies with the same profit can report very different sales, depending on whether they own what they sell or just arrange the sale. The jargon is principal versus agent, and gross versus net reporting.

LEWIS: On big purchases: putting a purchase on the balance sheet spreads its cost over the years it helps us. That lifts this year's profit and operating cash flow, but it creates no cash. The jargon is capitalization, depreciation, and the shift from operating to investing cash flow.

HEART: And on estimates: when a company trims its assumptions for bad debts or warranty claims, profit rises without the business changing at all, so we ask why and compare with peers. The jargon is aggressive expense recognition and management estimates.
