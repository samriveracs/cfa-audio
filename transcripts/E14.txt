## The big picture

This episode finishes the income statement and starts the balance sheet. One question ties it together. Which numbers tell you about the future, and how do you compare them fairly across companies of very different sizes?

Module 28.3 sorts income into pieces that will repeat and pieces that will not. Discontinued operations, unusual items and accounting changes each get a specific treatment, and exam questions often ask where an item goes, whether it is shown before or after tax, and whether prior years are restated. Module 28.4 is earnings per share, or EPS, one of the most quoted numbers in equity analysis. Module 28.5 turns the income statement into percentages of revenue so you can compare margins.

Then the balance sheet. Module 29.1 covers the assets whose values depend most on accounting rules: intangible assets, goodwill and financial instruments, plus a short look at noncurrent liabilities. Module 29.2 turns the balance sheet into percentages of total assets and adds the liquidity and solvency ratios.

## Module 28.3, Nonrecurring Items

Analysts value a company on the earnings it can keep producing. A gain from selling a factory happened this year, but it says little about next year. So the standards separate certain items, and you decide how much weight to give them. Three categories matter: discontinued operations, unusual or infrequent items, and accounting changes.

First, discontinued operations. A discontinued operation is a part of the company that has been sold or is classified as held for sale, and that can be cleanly separated from the rest of the business, operationally and financially. Think of a major business line or a whole geographic region. Its results are pulled out of the normal revenue and expense lines and reported on a separate line, net of tax, below income from continuing operations. Both International Financial Reporting Standards, or IFRS, and U.S. generally accepted accounting principles, or GAAP, work this way. Because it will not be there next year, analysts leave it out of forecasts.

Second, unusual or infrequent items. These include gains and losses on the sale of assets, impairment write downs, restructuring charges and litigation settlements. Here is the key contrast. These items stay inside income from continuing operations, and they are reported before tax, not net of tax. Material items like these must be shown or disclosed separately. Neither IFRS nor U.S. GAAP allows a separate category for extraordinary items anymore.

The analyst's job is to judge whether an unusual item is really unusual. A company that books a restructuring charge every year is telling you restructuring is a normal cost of its business. Some analysts average such charges over several years and treat the average as a recurring expense.

Third, accounting changes, where the treatment is the whole point. A change in accounting policy, such as a voluntary switch in inventory method, is applied retrospectively. That means prior years in the report are restated as if the new policy had always applied, so the years stay comparable. A new accounting standard follows its own transition rules, which are often retrospective too.

A change in accounting estimate is different. A new useful life, a new salvage value, or a new estimate of bad debts is applied prospectively. Only the current and future periods change, and nothing is restated. Finally, the correction of an error in a prior period is handled by restating those prior periods.

Here is the trap. A change in depreciation method sounds like a policy change, but both IFRS and U.S. GAAP treat it as a change in estimate, so it is applied prospectively. Also, estimate changes are a quiet way to manage earnings, because stretching useful lives lowers depreciation and lifts profit with no change in cash. Error corrections are a warning sign about reporting quality and internal controls.

A quick example. A company earns 100 million dollars pretax from continuing operations, after a 10 million dollar restructuring charge. It also reports 15 million dollars of after tax income from a division it has agreed to sell. That 15 million dollars drops out of your forecast entirely. If you judge the restructuring charge to be truly one time, you might add it back and start from 110 million dollars of recurring pretax income.

Remember the rule. Discontinued operations go below continuing operations, net of tax. Unusual or infrequent items stay in continuing operations, before tax. Policy changes and error corrections are retrospective, and changes in estimates are prospective.

## Module 28.4, Earnings Per Share

Earnings per share is the profit available to common shareholders divided by the number of common shares. Two versions matter. Basic EPS uses the shares actually outstanding. Diluted EPS assumes that every security that could become common stock does so, whenever that would lower EPS.

A company with a simple capital structure has no potentially dilutive securities, such as convertible bonds, convertible preferred stock, options or warrants. Its diluted EPS simply equals its basic EPS. A company with a complex capital structure has at least one of those securities, so it must report both basic and diluted EPS, and the two can differ.

Here is basic EPS, slowly. Basic EPS equals net income minus preferred dividends, all divided by the weighted average number of common shares outstanding during the period. Preferred dividends are subtracted because that money belongs to preferred shareholders, not common shareholders. Common dividends are never subtracted, and subtracting them is a classic wrong answer.

The denominator has two rules. First, shares issued or repurchased during the year are weighted by the fraction of the year they were outstanding. Second, stock splits and stock dividends are treated as if they happened at the very start of the period, and prior years shown for comparison are restated too, because neither one brings in new money.

Example. A company starts the year with 1 million shares. On July 1 it issues 200,000 new shares. On December 1 it does a two for one stock split. Net income is 2.4 million dollars and preferred dividends are 200,000 dollars. Before the split, the weighted average is 1 million plus 200,000 times one half, or 1.1 million shares.

The split doubles everything retroactively, so the weighted average becomes 2.2 million shares. The numerator is 2.4 million minus 200,000, which is 2.2 million dollars. Basic EPS is 2.2 million dollars divided by 2.2 million shares, or exactly 1 dollar.

Look at the traps in that example. If you forget the preferred dividends, you get about 1.09 dollars. If you use the year end count of 2.4 million shares instead of the weighted average, you get about 92 cents. And if you weight the split as if it only counted from December 1, your share count is far too low and your EPS is far too high.

Now diluted EPS. There are two methods, one for convertible securities and one for options.

The if converted method handles convertible preferred stock and convertible bonds. Assume conversion at the start of the year, or on the issue date if the security was issued during the year. For convertible preferred stock, the company would no longer pay those preferred dividends, so you add them back to the numerator, and you add the new common shares to the denominator. For convertible bonds, the company would no longer pay interest, so you add back the interest after tax, because interest was tax deductible. After tax interest equals the interest expense times one minus the tax rate. Then you add the new shares to the denominator.

Example. A company has net income of 4 million dollars, no preferred stock, and 2 million shares, so basic EPS is 2 dollars. It also has convertible bonds with 1 million dollars of annual interest expense, its tax rate is 25 percent, and the bonds convert into 500,000 shares. The after tax interest is 1 million times 0.75, or 750,000 dollars. Diluted EPS is 4.75 million dollars divided by 2.5 million shares, or 1.90 dollars.

Now convertible preferred. Suppose instead the company earns 5 million dollars and pays 1 million dollars of dividends on convertible preferred stock. Basic EPS is still 5 million minus 1 million, divided by 2 million shares, or 2 dollars. If the preferred converts into 1 million common shares, you add back the 1 million dollars of preferred dividends, so the numerator is the full 5 million dollars, and the denominator is 3 million shares. Diluted EPS is about 1.67 dollars.

The treasury stock method handles options and warrants. Assume the holders exercise and pay the exercise price, and the company uses that cash to buy back its own shares at the average market price for the period. The net new shares are the shares issued minus the shares bought back. The numerator does not change, because options pay no interest or dividends.

Example. There are options on 100,000 shares with an exercise price of 20 dollars, and the average market price during the year was 25 dollars. Exercise brings in 2 million dollars, which buys back 80,000 shares at 25 dollars each. The net new shares are 100,000 minus 80,000, or 20,000.

A shortcut gives the same answer: the number of option shares times the gap between the average price and the exercise price, all divided by the average price. That is 100,000 times 5, divided by 25, or 20,000.

Now go back to the company with 4 million dollars of net income and 2 million shares. If these options were its only dilutive security, diluted EPS would be 4 million dollars divided by 2.02 million shares, or about 1.98 dollars.

Now the antidilution test. A security is antidilutive if including it would raise EPS, and antidilutive securities are left out. That is why diluted EPS can never be higher than basic EPS. For a convertible, a quick check is to divide what it adds to the numerator by the shares it adds to the denominator. If that amount is below basic EPS, the security is dilutive. If it is above basic EPS, it is antidilutive.

In the bond example, 750,000 dollars divided by 500,000 shares is 1.50 dollars, below the basic EPS of 2 dollars, so the bonds are dilutive. If those same bonds converted into only 250,000 shares, the figure would be 3 dollars, above 2 dollars. Including them would push EPS up to about 2.11 dollars, so you leave them out and diluted EPS stays at 2 dollars.

For options, the test is simpler. Options are dilutive only when the average market price is above the exercise price, and otherwise they are left out.

Three traps to finish. First, use the average market price for the period, not the year end price. Second, add back interest after tax, not before. Third, leave antidilutive securities out. One more point: IFRS does not use the name treasury stock method, but its approach reaches the same answer.

Here is the core rule once more. Basic EPS equals net income minus preferred dividends, divided by the weighted average number of common shares. Diluted EPS adds back what conversion would save, adds the shares conversion would create, and includes only the securities that lower EPS.

## Module 28.5, Ratios and Common-Size Income Statements

A company with 10 billion dollars of revenue and one with 100 million dollars cannot be compared line by line. Common size analysis fixes that. On a common size income statement, every line is divided by revenue, so each becomes a percentage of sales. This is vertical common size analysis, and it lets you compare a company with its rivals and with its own past.

The key ratios here are margins. Gross profit margin equals gross profit divided by revenue, where gross profit is revenue minus cost of goods sold. Operating profit margin equals operating profit divided by revenue. Pretax margin equals income before tax divided by revenue. Net profit margin equals net income divided by revenue. Each one captures one more layer of cost.

Example. Revenue is 1,000 dollars and cost of goods sold is 600 dollars, so gross profit is 400 dollars and the gross margin is 40 percent. Operating expenses are 250 dollars, so operating profit is 150 dollars and the operating margin is 15 percent. Interest expense is 50 dollars, so pretax income is 100 dollars and the pretax margin is 10 percent. Tax is 25 dollars, so net income is 75 dollars and the net profit margin is 7.5 percent.

Now read the margins like an analyst. Gross margin reflects pricing power and production costs. The gap between gross margin and operating margin reflects overhead, such as selling, general and administrative expenses and research spending. The gap between operating margin and pretax margin mostly reflects financing, because interest sits in between. The gap between pretax and net margins mostly reflects taxes. So if gross margin is stable while operating margin falls, the problem is overhead, not pricing.

Here is the trap. Tax divided by revenue, 2.5 percent in the example, tells you little. Analysts look instead at income tax expense divided by pretax income, which is the effective tax rate. In the example that is 25 dollars divided by 100 dollars, or 25 percent.

A second trap: reported margins include nonrecurring items, so one large gain or charge can distort them. Analysts often adjust margins for unusual items and discontinued operations, which links back to Module 28.3. The rule to keep: on a common size income statement, divide every line by revenue, and the four margins are gross, operating, pretax and net profit, each divided by revenue.

## Module 29.1, Intangible Assets and Marketable Securities

Now the balance sheet, starting with intangible assets. An intangible asset is a nonmonetary asset with no physical substance, such as a patent, a trademark, a license, software or a customer list. To be recognized on its own, it must be identifiable. That means it can be separated from the company and sold or licensed, or it comes from a contractual or legal right. Goodwill is not identifiable, so it gets its own treatment.

Recognition depends on how the company got the asset, and there are three routes. First, an intangible bought on its own is recorded at cost. Second, one acquired as part of buying another company is recorded at fair value on the acquisition date. Third, one developed internally is mostly expensed as the costs are incurred.

The internal route is where IFRS and U.S. GAAP differ. Under IFRS, research costs are expensed, but development costs are capitalized once the company can show, among other tests, that the project is technically feasible and will probably produce future economic benefits. Under U.S. GAAP, research and development costs are generally expensed, with narrow exceptions for certain software development costs. Under both frameworks, internally generated brands and customer lists are not capitalized.

That creates a comparability problem. A company that builds a famous brand through decades of advertising shows almost none of that value on its balance sheet. A rival that buys a similar brand shows it at fair value. Their balance sheets are not comparable, and the difference is accounting, not economics.

After recognition, the key question is useful life. An intangible with a finite useful life, such as a patent with 10 years left, is amortized over that life and tested for impairment when there are signs of trouble. An intangible with an indefinite useful life, such as a trademark the company can keep renewing, is not amortized. Instead, it is tested for impairment at least once a year. IFRS also permits a revaluation model for intangibles, but only when an active market exists, which is rare, and U.S. GAAP does not permit revaluation.

Now goodwill. Goodwill arises only when one company acquires another. It equals the purchase price minus the fair value of the identifiable net assets acquired, meaning identifiable assets minus liabilities, all measured at fair value. It captures things like expected synergies, an assembled workforce, and sometimes simple overpayment. A company can never record goodwill that it generated internally.

Example. A buyer pays 500 million dollars for a target. The target's identifiable assets have a fair value of 600 million dollars and its liabilities are 200 million dollars, so its identifiable net assets are 400 million dollars. Goodwill is 500 minus 400, or 100 million dollars.

Here is the trap. Suppose the target's book value of net assets is only 300 million dollars. A careless answer gives 200 million dollars of goodwill, so always use fair values, not book values. If the price is below the fair value of the net assets, there is no negative goodwill, and the buyer records a bargain purchase gain in profit or loss.

For listed companies, goodwill is not amortized under either IFRS or U.S. GAAP. It is tested for impairment at least annually, at the level of a cash generating unit under IFRS or a reporting unit under U.S. GAAP. An impairment loss reduces goodwill and net income, and it can never be reversed. For analysis, a large impairment often signals that the company overpaid for an acquisition, and it is a noncash charge. Because impairment testing relies on management's forecasts, its timing involves judgment, and many analysts remove goodwill when computing ratios.

Now financial instruments, which is where marketable securities live. Under IFRS, a financial asset falls into one of three measurement categories. The category depends on the company's business model for the asset and on whether its cash flows are solely payments of principal and interest.

First, amortized cost. This applies to debt instruments held to collect their contractual cash flows. The asset is carried at its purchase price, adjusted over time for any premium or discount, and changes in its market value are not recognized. Interest income goes to profit or loss.

Second, fair value through other comprehensive income. For debt, this applies when the business model is both to collect cash flows and to sell. The asset is carried at fair value, and unrealized gains and losses go to other comprehensive income, which sits in equity rather than net income. When the debt is sold, the cumulative gain or loss is moved, or recycled, into profit or loss, and interest income goes to profit or loss throughout.

Third, fair value through profit or loss. This is the category for everything else, including trading securities and derivatives. The asset is carried at fair value, and unrealized gains and losses go straight into net income.

Equity securities are a special case under IFRS. They have no principal and interest, so they cannot sit at amortized cost, and they normally go to fair value through profit or loss. But a company can make an irrevocable election, when it buys an equity investment that it does not hold for trading, to measure it at fair value through other comprehensive income. Then unrealized gains and losses go to other comprehensive income and are never recycled to profit or loss, even on sale. Dividends still go to profit or loss.

U.S. GAAP uses different labels for debt securities. Held to maturity means amortized cost. Available for sale means fair value, with unrealized gains and losses in other comprehensive income. Trading means fair value, with those gains and losses in net income. Equity securities are generally measured at fair value through net income.

A tiny example. A company buys a bond at par for 1,000 dollars, and by year end its fair value is 1,050 dollars. At amortized cost, the balance sheet still shows 1,000 dollars and the 50 dollar gain is not recognized. In both fair value categories, the balance sheet shows 1,050 dollars. Here is the trap: they differ only in where the 50 dollar gain lands, in other comprehensive income for one and in net income for the other.

In bank and credit union balance sheet work, this classification decides whether a rise in interest rates shows up in reported equity right away, through other comprehensive income, or stays out of reported equity because the securities sit at amortized cost. Financial liabilities, such as bonds and loans, are mostly measured at amortized cost, while derivatives and liabilities held for trading are measured at fair value through profit or loss.

Last, noncurrent liabilities. These are obligations due beyond one year, such as bonds, bank loans, leases, pensions and deferred taxes. Long term debt is usually carried at amortized cost, so its balance sheet value reflects the market rate when it was issued, not today's rate. The portion due within the next year is reclassified as a current liability. A deferred tax liability arises when taxable income is lower than accounting profit because of temporary differences, such as faster depreciation for tax purposes, so the company expects to pay more tax later. Leases, pensions and deferred taxes get fuller treatment later in this topic.

Here are the rules to keep. Goodwill equals the purchase price minus the fair value of identifiable net assets, and it is not amortized, is tested at least annually, and is never written back up. Financial assets sit at amortized cost, at fair value through other comprehensive income, or at fair value through profit or loss, and the category decides where unrealized gains land.

## Module 29.2, Common-Size Balance Sheets

A common size balance sheet divides every item by total assets. Because total assets equal total liabilities plus equity, both sides add up to 100 percent. It shows at a glance what the company owns and how it is funded.

A large and growing share of goodwill suggests the company grows by acquisition. A rising share of receivables can mean looser credit terms or aggressive revenue recognition. Here is the trap. A falling percentage does not prove the dollar amount fell, because cash can drop from 10 percent to 8 percent of total assets while still growing, if total assets grew faster.

The balance sheet ratios come in two families. Liquidity ratios measure the ability to meet short term obligations. The current ratio equals current assets divided by current liabilities. The quick ratio equals cash plus short term marketable investments plus receivables, all divided by current liabilities. The cash ratio equals cash plus short term marketable investments, divided by current liabilities.

Each liquidity ratio is stricter than the one before. The quick ratio drops inventory and prepaid expenses, because inventory may take a long time to turn into cash. The cash ratio drops receivables as well.

Take current assets of 400 dollars, made up of 50 dollars of cash, 50 dollars of marketable securities, 100 dollars of receivables and 200 dollars of inventory. Current liabilities are 200 dollars. The current ratio is 400 divided by 200, or 2.0. The quick ratio is 200 divided by 200, or 1.0. The cash ratio is 100 divided by 200, or 0.5. Higher means more cushion, but a very high current ratio can also signal idle cash or slow moving inventory.

Solvency ratios measure the ability to meet long term obligations. The long term debt to equity ratio equals total long term debt divided by total equity. The debt to equity ratio equals total debt divided by total equity. The total debt ratio equals total debt divided by total assets. The financial leverage ratio equals total assets divided by total equity.

Debt here usually means interest bearing borrowings, short term and long term, so check how each question defines it. With total assets of 1,000 dollars, total debt of 300 dollars and equity of 500 dollars, debt to equity is 0.6, the total debt ratio is 0.3, and financial leverage is 2.0. Higher solvency ratios mean more financial risk.

One more trap is a transaction that moves a ratio in a surprising direction. Start with a current ratio of 2.0, from 400 dollars of current assets and 200 dollars of current liabilities. The company uses 100 dollars of cash to pay its suppliers. Current assets fall to 300 dollars and current liabilities fall to 100 dollars, so the current ratio rises to 3.0. Paying down current liabilities with cash raises the current ratio whenever it starts above 1, and lowers it whenever it starts below 1.

Here is the rule to keep. A common size balance sheet divides every item by total assets, the current, quick and cash ratios measure liquidity, and the debt and leverage ratios measure solvency.

## Recall check

Q: Which is most likely applied prospectively, with no restatement of prior years: a voluntary change in inventory method, a change in the estimated useful life of equipment, or the correction of a prior period error?

Answer: The change in useful life. It is a change in accounting estimate, so it affects only the current and future periods. A voluntary policy change is applied retrospectively, and an error correction restates the prior periods.

Q: A company has options on 100,000 shares with an exercise price of 40 dollars, and the average share price for the year was 50 dollars. How many net new shares does the treasury stock method add?

Answer: 20,000 shares. Exercise raises 4 million dollars, which buys back 80,000 shares at the average price of 50 dollars. Issuing 100,000 shares and repurchasing 80,000 leaves 20,000 net new shares.

Q: Basic EPS is 2 dollars. A convertible bond would add 600,000 dollars of after tax interest to the numerator and 200,000 shares to the denominator. Should it be included in diluted EPS?

Answer: No. Its incremental effect is 600,000 dollars divided by 200,000 shares, or 3 dollars per new share, which is above basic EPS of 2 dollars. Including it would raise EPS, so it is antidilutive and is excluded.

Q: A company's operating margin is unchanged from last year, but its pretax margin has fallen. Which is the most likely cause: higher cost of goods sold, higher interest expense, or a higher tax rate?

Answer: Higher interest expense. Interest sits between operating profit and pretax income, so it can lower the pretax margin without touching the operating margin. Higher cost of goods sold would have lowered the operating margin too, and the tax rate does not affect pretax income at all.

Q: Under IFRS, a company elects to measure an equity investment it does not hold for trading at fair value through other comprehensive income. Where do its unrealized gains go, and what happens to them when the shares are sold?

Answer: Unrealized gains and losses go to other comprehensive income. When the shares are sold, those gains are not recycled into profit or loss. Dividends on the investment still go to profit or loss.

Q: On a common size balance sheet, inventory falls from 20 percent to 15 percent of total assets. Does this prove the company holds less inventory than a year ago?

Answer: No. Common size figures are shares of total assets. If total assets grew faster than inventory, the percentage can fall while inventory in dollars rises. Check the dollar amounts before drawing a conclusion.

## Recap

Discontinued operations sit on a separate line, net of tax, below continuing operations, while unusual or infrequent items stay in continuing operations, before tax. Policy changes and error corrections are retrospective, and estimate changes, including a new depreciation method, are prospective. Basic EPS equals net income minus preferred dividends, divided by the weighted average common shares, with splits and stock dividends applied retroactively. Diluted EPS uses the if converted method for convertibles, adding back preferred dividends or after tax interest, and the treasury stock method for options, at the average market price.

Antidilutive securities are excluded, so diluted EPS is never above basic EPS. Common size statements divide every line by revenue on the income statement and by total assets on the balance sheet, and they feed the margins and the liquidity and solvency ratios. Internally generated intangibles are mostly expensed, and goodwill is not amortized but is tested for impairment at least annually. Financial assets sit at amortized cost, at fair value through other comprehensive income, or at fair value through profit or loss, and the category decides where unrealized gains land.
