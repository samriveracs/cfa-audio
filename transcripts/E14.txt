LEWIS: Reading 28 finishes with income that will not repeat, accounting changes, earnings per share and dilution, and common size income statements. Reading 29 covers intangible assets and goodwill, how financial assets and long term liabilities are measured, and common size balance sheets with the liquidity and solvency ratios.

## Module 28.3, Nonrecurring Items

HEART: An analyst values a company on the profit it can keep earning. So the first job with any income statement is sorting the parts that will come back next year from the parts that will not. Picture a trucking company whose profit jumped this year because it sold an old depot for far more than it paid decades ago. Nobody should expect that depot sale again next year.

LEWIS: The standards help by putting certain items in special places. The first group is unusual or infrequent items. These are events that are either unusual in nature or infrequent in occurrence, and material, meaning big enough to change how a reader of the statements sees the company.

HEART: Typical examples. A gain or loss from selling assets or part of the business, when selling those things is not what the company normally does. Impairments, write offs and write downs, where an asset is marked down because it is worth less than its carrying value. And restructuring costs, like the severance and lease costs of shutting a call center.

LEWIS: Here is the placement to lock in. Unusual or infrequent items stay inside income from continuing operations, and they are reported before tax. They may appear on a line of their own, but they sit above the tax line, which also puts them above the income from continuing operations total, and they are taxed along with everything else.

HEART: The analyst's job is to decide whether unusual really means unusual. Picture a friend whose car breaks down unexpectedly every single winter. After a few winters it is not a surprise anymore. Some companies are like that, with an unusual loss every year or every few years. When that happens, the honest forecast treats those charges as a normal cost of doing business rather than leaving them out.

LEWIS: The second group is discontinued operations. A discontinued operation is a part of the business that management has decided to exit. The exit may still be ahead at year end, or it may have happened during the year, after the unit had already produced some profit or loss.

HEART: Picture a packaged food company that decides to sell its pet food division. For that division to count as a discontinued operation, it has to be physically and operationally distinct from the rest of the company. Separate assets, separate operations, and separate investing and financing activities. A product line that shares the same factories, sales force and financing as everything else would not qualify.

LEWIS: Two dates matter. The measurement date is the date the company adopts a formal plan to dispose of the operation. The phaseout period runs from the measurement date to the day the operation is actually disposed of.

HEART: And here is a lopsided rule worth knowing. The moment the plan is adopted, which is the measurement date, the company books the operating losses it expects while winding the unit down, plus any loss it expects on the sale. An expected gain on the sale, though, stays off the books until the sale has gone through. Bad news is booked when it is foreseen. Good news waits until it happens.

LEWIS: Where does it all go? The income or loss from discontinued operations is reported on its own line, net of tax, below income from continuing operations. And any prior year income statements shown alongside are restated to pull the discontinued results out of them, so the years still compare.

HEART: Set that beside unusual items, the favorite exam contrast. Unusual items ride inside continuing operations and are taxed with everything else. Discontinued operations sit below the tax line, so they carry their own tax.

LEWIS: For analysis, discontinued operations are the easy case. They do not affect net income from continuing operations, and the business will not be there next year, so they come out of the earnings forecast. But the decision itself carries information about future cash flows: what the company will no longer earn, and what cash it may soon have to put to work.

HEART: The third group is accounting changes, and there are three kinds: changes in accounting policies, changes in accounting estimates, and prior period adjustments. Each gets one of two treatments, so learn the treatments first.

LEWIS: Picture a running app that changes how it measures distance. One option is to recalculate every run you have ever logged with the new method. Now your old runs and new runs compare fairly. That is retrospective application. Prior period statements shown in the current report are restated as if the new method had always applied. The other option is to apply the new method only from today forward and leave the old runs alone. That is prospective application. Nothing in the past is restated.

HEART: A change in accounting policy can come from a standard setter issuing a new standard, or from the company itself switching to a different acceptable method, such as moving from one accepted inventory cost flow method to another. Unless it is impractical, a policy change is applied retrospectively. One variation: when the current revenue recognition standard arrived, companies were allowed a modified retrospective approach. Prior periods are not restated, but the opening balances of the affected accounts are adjusted for the cumulative effect of the change. One catch up adjustment at the start, instead of rewriting history.

LEWIS: A change in accounting estimate is different. It happens when management revises a judgment, typically because it has learned something new. Picture an airline that learns its jets will fly safely for years longer than first expected, so it extends their useful lives. That change is applied prospectively. Only the current and future periods change, and nothing is restated.

HEART: Estimate changes usually do not touch cash. Extending the jets' lives lowers depreciation and lifts profit, but not one more dollar comes in. So the analyst's question is how the change affects future operating results, and whether the new judgment is believable.

LEWIS: One related point. A change in depreciation method sounds like a policy change, but both IFRS and US GAAP handle it like a change in estimate, so it is applied prospectively.

HEART: The third kind is a prior period adjustment, the correction of an error in earlier statements, including a move from a method that was never acceptable to one that is. That one is retrospective. The prior periods are restated, and the company must disclose what the error was and how it affected net income.

LEWIS: Prior period adjustments usually do not affect cash either. But they deserve a hard look, because an error that slipped through can signal weak internal controls. If the books were wrong once, ask what else might be.

HEART: The trap is the sorting. Policy changes and error corrections look backward and restate. Estimate changes look forward and restate nothing.

LEWIS: Last, two things that can move the numbers even though companies are not required to disclose their effect. A change in scope means an acquisition has changed the size of the combined company. When a grocery chain buys a rival halfway through the year, this year's totals against last year's tell you more about the deal than about the business underneath.

HEART: And exchange rates. A company that sells abroad, buys abroad or owns foreign subsidiaries has to translate those results into its reporting currency. When the home currency strengthens, the same foreign sales translate into fewer units of home currency, and reported revenue can fall with no change in the business. The detail of both topics waits for Level II.

LEWIS: The takeaway. Unusual or infrequent items stay in continuing operations, before tax. Discontinued operations go below, net of tax, with losses booked when foreseen and gains only when realized. Policy changes and error corrections are retrospective, and estimate changes are prospective.

## Module 28.4, Earnings Per Share

HEART: Earnings per share, EPS, is the profit belonging to each common share, and it is one of the most quoted numbers for public companies. Two framing facts. EPS is reported only for common stock, also called ordinary shares. And companies that are not publicly traded are not required to report it.

LEWIS: Picture the company's profit as a pizza and each common share as a slice. EPS is the size of one slice. Everything in this module comes down to two questions. How big is the pizza that belongs to common shareholders? And how many slices is it cut into?

HEART: Start with capital structure. A simple capital structure contains no potentially dilutive securities. Just common stock, plus any debt or preferred stock that can never become common stock. A complex capital structure contains securities that could become common shares, such as employee stock options, warrants, convertible bonds or convertible preferred stock. A simple structure reports only basic EPS. A complex one must report both basic and diluted EPS.

LEWIS: Basic EPS first. In words, it is net income minus preferred dividends, divided by the weighted average number of common shares outstanding.

HEART: Take the top of that fraction. Preferred shareholders stand in line ahead of common shareholders. Their dividends for the year come first, so that money is not part of the common shareholders' pizza. Net income minus preferred dividends is called the income available to common shareholders.

LEWIS: And common dividends are never subtracted. That is the classic wrong answer. Common dividends are paid out of income that already belongs to common shareholders. A dividend just hands them part of the slice they already own. It does not shrink the slice.

HEART: Now the bottom, the weighted average number of shares. Picture a gym working out its average membership for the year. Someone who joins on the first day of the final quarter was a member for a quarter of the year, so counts as a quarter of a member. Shares work the same way. Each share counts for the fraction of the year it was outstanding.

LEWIS: The rules follow from that. Newly issued shares count from the date they are issued, including shares issued to pay for an asset. Shares the company buys back drop out from the date of the buyback. Strictly, the weighting runs on days, but exam questions usually count in months.

HEART: Now splits and stock dividends, the heavily tested part. In a stock split, every existing share is swapped for a larger number of new ones. In a stock dividend, the company pays its shareholders in extra shares instead of cash, in proportion to what each already holds. For the share count, the two are interchangeable. A stock dividend of one extra share for every share held lands exactly where a two for one split does.

LEWIS: And here is the key. Neither one brings in any money or changes anyone's percentage of ownership. Back to the pizza. A split cuts the same pizza into more slices. Each owner has more slices, but the same share of the pizza.

HEART: So a split or stock dividend is applied retroactively. Every share outstanding before the event is restated as if the split had happened at the very start of the year, no matter when during the year it actually happened. Prior years' share counts shown for comparison are restated too. Otherwise EPS would appear to collapse in the year of a split, for a change that leaves the business exactly as it was.

LEWIS: Two traps. First, a split late in the year still applies to the whole year for the shares that existed before it. Do not weight it only by the months after the split. Second, shares issued or bought back after the split date are not adjusted for it. They were already new shares. The split only recuts the slices that existed before it.

HEART: Now diluted EPS. A dilutive security is one that would lower EPS if it were converted or exercised. An antidilutive security is one that would raise EPS. Diluted EPS shows what EPS would have been if every dilutive security had become common stock.

LEWIS: Why would a security ever raise EPS? More shares sounds like it can only shrink the slices.

HEART: Because conversion changes the top as well as the bottom. Picture a class with an average test score. A new student joins. If the new student's score is below the class average, the average falls. If it is above, the average rises. Every potentially dilutive security is a new student. It brings some earnings to the top, money the company would no longer have to pay out, and it brings new shares to the bottom.

LEWIS: So the test is simple. Ask how much earnings the security brings for each new share it creates, and compare that with basic EPS. If it brings less per new share than basic EPS, it drags the average down, so it is dilutive and it goes in. If it brings more per new share than basic EPS, it would pull the average up, so it is antidilutive and it stays out.

HEART: Each security is tested on its own. And because the antidilutive ones are always left out, diluted EPS can never be higher than basic EPS. If an answer shows diluted EPS above basic EPS, something antidilutive was let in.

LEWIS: Convertible securities are handled with the if converted method. Assume conversion at the start of the year, whether or not the holders were actually allowed to convert during the year. If the security was issued partway through the year, count its new shares only for the part of the year it was outstanding.

HEART: Convertible preferred stock first. If it converted, the company would stop paying those preferred dividends. So they no longer come out of income available to common, which means you add them back to the top. And you add the new common shares to the bottom.

LEWIS: So for convertible preferred, the earnings it brings per new share is its preferred dividend spread over the new shares it creates. Picture preferred stock that pays a generous dividend but swaps into only a few common shares. Each new share arrives carrying a lot of dividend with it, likely more than basic EPS, so it is likely antidilutive. Preferred with a small dividend that swaps into many shares brings little per new share, so it is likely dilutive.

HEART: And if convertible preferred turns out to be antidilutive, nothing changes. Its dividends stay subtracted, exactly as in basic EPS.

LEWIS: Convertible bonds next. If the bonds converted, the company would stop paying the interest. But interest is tax deductible, so the company would also lose that tax saving. So you add back the interest after tax, which is the interest times one minus the tax rate, and you add the new shares to the bottom.

HEART: That is the trap. For bonds, add back the after tax interest, never the full interest. And there is no tax adjustment for preferred dividends, because preferred dividends were never tax deductible in the first place. They come out of profit after tax.

LEWIS: Options and warrants work differently. They pay no interest and no dividends, so there is nothing to add to the top. They only add shares. That means they are dilutive whenever they add any net new shares at all, and that happens only when the exercise price is below the average market price for the year.

HEART: That is where the treasury stock method comes in. Assume the option holders exercise and pay the exercise price. Then assume the company takes that cash and buys back its own shares in the market at the average market price for the year. The net new shares are the shares issued on exercise minus the shares bought back.

LEWIS: Why assume a buyback?

HEART: Because exercise brings the company real cash, and ignoring that cash would overstate the dilution. And here is the intuition for why only part of the options count. Split the option shares into two piles. One pile is shares the exercise cash effectively paid for at the full market price. Selling shares at a fair price takes nothing from existing owners. The other pile is shares handed over for nothing, because the holders paid less than the shares were worth. Only that free pile dilutes.

LEWIS: Picture an employee whose options let her buy shares below where the stock has been trading. Her cash could buy back some of those shares, but not all of them. The shortfall is the dilution.

HEART: Which gives a relationship in words. The fraction of the option shares that ends up as net new shares equals the gap between the average market price and the exercise price, divided by the average market price.

LEWIS: So the directions. A higher average share price, with the exercise price unchanged, means more net new shares and more dilution. If the average market price is below the exercise price, the exercise cash would buy back more shares than the options created, so the share count would fall and EPS would rise. Those options are antidilutive and are left out. The trap here is the price. The right one is the average market price for the year, not the year end price, because EPS covers the whole year, so the assumed buyback is priced at the year's average.

HEART: The takeaway. Basic EPS is income available to common over the weighted average shares, with splits and stock dividends rewound to the start of the year. Diluted EPS adds back what conversion would save and adds the new shares, but only for securities that bring less earnings per new share than basic EPS. Options dilute only when they are in the money on average. Antidilutive securities stay out, so diluted EPS never tops basic.

## Module 28.5, Ratios and Common-Size Income Statements

LEWIS: Picture a sandwich chain that has doubled its sales over five years. Comparing this year's costs with five years ago in dollars tells you almost nothing, because everything is bigger. A vertical common size income statement fixes that by expressing every line as a percentage of revenue. Size drops out, and what is left is the shape of the business.

HEART: That allows two kinds of comparison. Time series analysis compares a company with its own past. Cross sectional analysis compares it with other companies over the same period. If the sandwich chain's ingredient costs have crept up as a share of sales year after year, the common size statement shows it plainly, while the raw dollars hide it inside the growth.

LEWIS: Common size statements also reveal strategy. Picture a discount grocer and a premium grocer of similar size. The discount grocer's cost of goods sold eats a much bigger share of each sales dollar, so its gross margin is thin, but it runs bare bones stores. The premium grocer keeps much more of each sales dollar after the cost of its goods, but spends far more of it on staff, displays and prime locations. Side by side, the percentages show two different business models.

HEART: One line breaks the pattern. Income tax expense is more meaningful as a percentage of pretax income, not revenue, because tax is charged on profit, not on sales. That ratio is the effective tax rate. Tax as a share of revenue mixes the tax rate together with how profitable the company is, so on its own it tells you very little.

LEWIS: Now the ratios. Margin ratios are quick profitability checks, each one a profit figure divided by revenue. Gross profit margin is gross profit, which is revenue minus cost of goods sold, divided by revenue. Net profit margin is net income divided by revenue, the profit left after every expense. In fact, any subtotal on the income statement can become a margin. Operating profit divided by revenue is the operating profit margin. Pretax income divided by revenue is the pretax margin.

HEART: How does a company raise its gross margin? Two ways. Charge higher prices, or produce more cheaply. And the ability to charge more usually comes from differentiation: a brand people trust, better quality, superior technology or patent protection. Picture a maker of premium headphones. A strong brand and patented noise cancelling let it charge well above generic rivals for parts that cost about the same, so its gross margin is wide.

LEWIS: Common size statements can show how that edge is paid for, too. A company that spends a larger share of revenue on research than its rivals may be buying the technology that supports its higher prices.

HEART: Read the margins as a staircase, top to bottom. Gross margin reflects pricing and production costs. The step down to operating margin reflects operating expenses such as selling, administrative and research costs. The step down to pretax margin mostly reflects financing costs, since interest sits between the two. And the step down to net margin mostly reflects taxes. So if gross margin holds steady while operating margin shrinks, the problem is overhead, not pricing.

LEWIS: Three traps. First, margins should be compared over time and with industry peers, never judged alone, because normal margins differ hugely between, say, software and groceries. Second, reported margins include any unusual items from Module 28.3. One large restructuring charge can sink a year's operating margin, so analysts often look at margins with and without those items. Third, remember from Module 28.1 that an agent reporting revenue net shows a far higher gross margin than a principal earning the same gross profit.

HEART: The takeaway. A common size income statement divides every line by revenue, except tax, which is judged against pretax income as the effective tax rate. Gross margin reflects pricing power and production cost, and differentiation is the usual route to a higher one.

## Module 29.1, Intangible Assets and Marketable Securities

LEWIS: Now the balance sheet, starting with assets you cannot touch. An intangible asset is a nonmonetary asset that lacks physical substance. Patents, trademarks and copyrights are typical examples. One exclusion: securities are not intangible assets, even though you cannot touch them either. They are financial assets.

HEART: Intangibles come in two kinds. Identifiable intangibles can be bought or sold on their own, or they come from a legal or contractual right, such as a patent or a broadcast license. Unidentifiable intangibles cannot be split off from the business and traded by themselves, and they may last indefinitely. Goodwill is the main one, and it comes up shortly.

LEWIS: How are purchased identifiable intangibles carried? Under IFRS, a company can choose the cost model or the revaluation model, the same two choices it has for property, plant and equipment. But revaluation is allowed only if there is an active market for the intangible, and that is rare. Under US GAAP, only the cost model is allowed.

HEART: Internally created intangibles are a different story. Picture a software firm spending years building a product, or a shoe brand spending decades on advertising. Under US GAAP, the costs of creating intangibles internally, research and development included, are generally expensed as incurred, apart from narrow exceptions such as certain legal costs and the software case from Module 28.2.

LEWIS: Under IFRS, as Module 28.2 introduced, the company splits the work into two stages. The research stage is the search for new scientific or technical knowledge, and its costs are expensed. The development stage puts the research results to work planning or designing products, and its costs can be capitalized once certain criteria are met.

HEART: The criteria work as a checklist of four questions. Is it technically feasible, meaning can it really be done? Are the money and people in place to finish it? Is there a market that will buy it? And does the company mean to finish it and sell it, with the means to do so?

LEWIS: Picture a kitchen appliance maker designing a quieter dishwasher. Early on, its engineers are still testing whether a new motor material works at all. That is research, and it is expensed. Later the design works, retailers want it, the money is in place to finish it, and the company is committed to selling it. Now the costs of finishing the design can be capitalized under IFRS. Under US GAAP, they would still be expensed.

HEART: Some costs are expensed under both frameworks, no matter how much future value they seem to create. Start up and training costs. Administrative overhead. Advertising and promotion. Relocation and reorganization. And termination costs. When a hotel group opens in a new country, the launch campaign, the staff training and the managers it moves overseas may pay off for years, but all of it is expensed.

LEWIS: After recognition, the key question is useful life. An intangible with a finite life is amortized over that life and checked for impairment just as a machine would be, and its amortization method and useful life must be revisited every year at a minimum. A patent is the classic finite life intangible. An intangible with an indefinite life, such as a trademark that can be renewed again and again, is not amortized, but it is tested for impairment at least once a year.

HEART: Some analysts simply strip intangible assets out of a balance sheet. The better habit is to weigh what each one is really worth first. Patents on a best selling drug are not a stale customer list.

LEWIS: Now goodwill. Picture buying a busy neighborhood restaurant. You add up the fair value of everything you can identify, the kitchen equipment, the liquor license, the furniture, and subtract its debts. Then you pay more than that, because of the loyal regulars and the reputation built over twenty years. That extra is goodwill.

HEART: Formally, goodwill is the extra a buyer pays for a business over and above the fair value of its identifiable net assets, which are the identifiable assets it gets minus the liabilities it takes on. Fair value is the key phrase. Not the target's own book values.

LEWIS: Why do buyers pay more? Three reasons. Some of the target's value never made it onto its balance sheet, like the restaurant's loyal regulars and good name. Its own research and development may be missing too, since the standards we just covered make companies expense most of that spending. And the buyer expects synergies, savings or gains from combining, such as merging two delivery networks or buying supplies in bigger volumes.

HEART: Occasionally a buyer pays less than the fair value of the identifiable net assets. That is a bargain purchase, and the buyer reports the difference as a gain on its income statement right away.

LEWIS: Goodwill only reaches a balance sheet through an acquisition. Whatever a company spends building its own reputation is expensed as it goes, never put on its balance sheet.

HEART: After the deal, goodwill is treated as having an indefinite life, so it is never amortized. Instead, at least once a year, the company checks whether it is still worth what the books say. When it is not, the company writes it down and takes an impairment loss on the income statement, a noncash charge. The message is that the buyer has cut its forecast of the excess returns the acquired business will earn, so the business is now worth less than what was paid for it.

LEWIS: Here is the trap, and it is a manipulation risk. In an acquisition, the price is spread across the identifiable assets at fair value, and whatever is left over becomes goodwill. Equipment and identifiable intangibles with finite lives get depreciated or amortized. Goodwill does not. So a buyer that assigns lower fair values to the identifiable assets, and more of the price to goodwill, reports less depreciation and amortization in future years and higher net income. This is easiest when fair values are subjective.

HEART: Keep two kinds of goodwill apart. Accounting goodwill comes from past acquisitions. Economic goodwill comes from the company's expected future performance.

LEWIS: And analysts disagree about accounting goodwill. Supporters call it a real asset, the present value of the extra profit the acquired business should bring in. Skeptics point out that nobody can sell it by itself, and that it may just measure how much the buyer overpaid. Either way, impairments are a useful scorecard for past acquisitions.

HEART: For ratio analysis, the usual adjustment is to remove goodwill from the balance sheet and goodwill impairment charges from the income statement, so a company that grew by acquisition can be compared with one that grew on its own. And judge any future acquisition by the price paid compared with the earning power of what is being bought.

LEWIS: Now financial instruments. A financial instrument is a contract that creates a financial asset for one party and a financial liability or equity instrument for the other. A bond is the investor's asset and the issuer's liability. A share is the investor's asset and part of the issuer's equity. So financial instruments show up on both sides of balance sheets. Held as assets, they include stocks and bonds, derivatives, loans and receivables.

HEART: There are three ways to measure them: historical cost, amortized cost and fair value. Loans and notes receivable are carried at cost, and so are unquoted equity investments whose fair value cannot be reliably measured.

LEWIS: Amortized cost needs a picture. Buy a bond for less than its face value. Each year, part of that discount is amortized, so the carrying value climbs steadily toward face value by maturity. A bond bought above face value steps down toward face value the same way. Subtract any principal repaid and any impairment losses. And here is the defining feature. Changes in the bond's market value along the way are ignored. The path is set the day you buy it.

HEART: Under US GAAP, debt securities bought with the intent to hold them until they mature are called held to maturity securities, and they are carried at amortized cost.

LEWIS: Fair value accounting, also called mark to market, applies to trading securities, available for sale securities and derivatives. All of them show market value on the balance sheet. They differ in where the unrealized gains and losses land. An unrealized gain or loss, also called a holding period gain or loss, is the change in market value on a security you still own.

HEART: Trading securities, also called held for trading securities, are debt securities bought to sell in the near term. They are carried at fair value, and unrealized gains and losses go straight to the income statement. Equity holdings with quoted market prices are treated the same way, unless the holding gives significant influence over the other company, which is a Level II topic. Derivatives are treated like trading securities too.

LEWIS: Available for sale securities are debt securities the company does not expect to hold to maturity or to trade soon. They are also carried at fair value, but unrealized gains and losses skip the income statement. They go to other comprehensive income, which is part of shareholders' equity. And under US GAAP, equity securities cannot be classified as available for sale.

HEART: Under US GAAP, one rule covers every category. Dividends, interest, and realized gains and losses, the ones actually locked in when a security is sold, always go to the income statement. IFRS sends interest and dividends to profit and loss too. The exception is equity a company chose to carry at fair value through other comprehensive income, where even a gain or loss on sale never reaches profit.

LEWIS: Here is the picture that makes the classes stick. Three holders own the same Treasury bond when interest rates drop and its market price rises. An insurer that plans to hold it to maturity carries it at amortized cost, so neither its balance sheet nor its income shows the rise. A company that holds it to collect the interest but may sell it carries it as available for sale. Its balance sheet shows the higher value, and the gain lifts equity through other comprehensive income, while net income never sees it. A trading desk carries it as a trading security, so the gain lands in net income.

HEART: Same bond, same price move, three different reported results. Classification follows what the holder intends to do, not just what the security is. And when rates rise instead, the same choice decides whether a bank's or credit union's bond losses show up in reported equity right away or stay off the balance sheet, visible only in the notes.

LEWIS: IFRS has three matching classes. The treatments line up, but the rules for which security goes where are different. Amortized cost matches held to maturity. It requires that the cash flows are solely payments of principal and interest, and that the business model is to hold the security to maturity and collect those payments. Fair value through other comprehensive income matches available for sale. And fair value through profit and loss matches trading.

HEART: The sorting rules. Under both frameworks, debt held to maturity, loans, notes receivable and unlisted securities without a reliable fair value sit at amortized or historical cost. Under IFRS, debt held to collect interest but possibly sold before maturity goes to fair value through other comprehensive income. Debt bought to sell soon, equity securities, derivatives and anything that fits neither class go to fair value through profit and loss.

LEWIS: Then two irrevocable choices under IFRS, both made at the time of purchase. A company may choose to carry an equity security at fair value through other comprehensive income, which US GAAP does not allow, since equity there can never be available for sale. And a company may choose to carry a financial asset at fair value through profit and loss.

HEART: Last in this module, noncurrent liabilities. Long term financial liabilities include bank loans, notes payable, bonds payable and some derivatives. They are usually carried at amortized cost. When one is issued above or below its face value, the premium or discount is amortized through interest expense over its life, so the carrying value moves toward face value by maturity.

LEWIS: Picture a company issuing bonds below face value because their coupon is lower than the market wants. It owes the full face value at maturity, so the discount is really extra interest, spread over the bond's life. Interest expense each year is higher than the coupon cash paid, and the liability climbs toward face value. A bond issued at a premium works in reverse.

HEART: Some liabilities are carried at fair value instead. Held for trading liabilities, such as a short position in a stock, which may be a short term liability. Derivative liabilities. And liabilities that are not derivatives themselves but whose risks are hedged with derivatives.

LEWIS: Then deferred tax liabilities, taxes that will be payable in future periods because tax accounting and financial accounting time things differently. A deferred tax liability is created when income tax expense on the income statement is greater than the taxes payable to the government now.

HEART: Two ways that happens. Expenses or losses can be deducted for tax before they hit the income statement. The classic case is a company using accelerated depreciation on its tax return and straight line depreciation in its financial statements. Or revenues and gains can hit the income statement before they are taxable, such as a subsidiary's earnings recognized before it pays any dividends to the parent.

LEWIS: Either way, the tax is postponed, not forgiven. The deferred tax liability reverses when the taxes are actually paid. The income taxes reading covers the mechanics.

HEART: The takeaway. Internally created intangibles are expensed, except development costs under IFRS that meet the criteria. Goodwill is the price paid above the fair value of identifiable net assets, is not amortized, and is tested at least yearly. Financial assets sit at amortized cost, at fair value through other comprehensive income, or at fair value through profit and loss, and the class decides where unrealized gains land.

## Module 29.2, Common-Size Balance Sheets

LEWIS: A vertical common size balance sheet expresses every item as a percentage of total assets. Because total assets equal total liabilities plus equity, both sides add up to the whole. Like the income statement version, it strips out size for the same time series and cross sectional comparisons.

HEART: It answers two questions at a glance. What does the company own, and how does it pay for it?

LEWIS: Here is how to read one. Picture a hardware store chain. If its current liabilities are a large share of total assets while its current assets are barely larger, it may struggle to pay bills as they come due. If cash is a thin slice while inventory is a fat one, it may need to sell goods and collect receivables before it can pay its suppliers. And a heavy share of inventory deserves a closer look, because goods may be going obsolete on the shelves.

HEART: But more liquidity is not automatically better. A big cushion of working capital can mean money sitting idle, since cash, receivables and inventory typically earn a lower return than the plants, equipment and other long term assets that run the business.

LEWIS: On the funding side, when long term debt pays for a large share of the assets, the company faces heavy obligations stretching years ahead, which is a solvency warning. And on the asset side, a growing share of goodwill over the years points to a company that buys its growth rather than building it, and if debt climbs alongside, it may be borrowing to pay for the deals.

HEART: One trap. A falling percentage does not prove a falling dollar amount. Cash can shrink as a share of total assets while growing in dollars, if total assets grow faster.

LEWIS: Those common size percentages are themselves balance sheet ratios, one balance sheet item compared with another. The named ratios come in two families, liquidity and solvency.

HEART: Liquidity ratios measure the ability to meet short term obligations as they come due. Picture three ways of answering a friend who asks whether you could cover a surprise bill. The generous answer counts everything you could turn into cash within the year, including the furniture you plan to sell. The stricter answer counts only your bank balance, your investments and the money people owe you. The strictest counts only your bank balance and investments.

LEWIS: Those are the three liquidity ratios, each with current liabilities on the bottom. The current ratio puts all current assets on top. The quick ratio, also called the acid test ratio, puts cash, marketable securities and receivables on top, so it leaves out inventory. The cash ratio puts only cash and marketable securities on top, so it leaves out receivables as well.

HEART: Read them together. If one company has a higher current ratio than a peer but a lower quick ratio, the difference must be inventory, because inventory is what the quick ratio drops. Comparing the quick ratio with the cash ratio isolates receivables the same way.

LEWIS: One directional trap. Paying off a current liability with cash lowers current assets and current liabilities by the same amount. When the current ratio starts above one, that raises it. When it starts below one, that lowers it.

HEART: Solvency ratios measure the ability to meet long term obligations, and there are four. Long term debt to equity is long term debt divided by total equity. Total debt to equity is total debt divided by total equity. The debt ratio is total debt divided by total assets. And the financial leverage ratio is total assets divided by total equity. For all four, higher means more financial risk.

LEWIS: Two definitions matter. In these ratios, debt usually means interest bearing obligations, the money actually borrowed. But the financial leverage ratio captures every obligation, interest bearing or not, because whatever assets equity does not pay for are funded by all the liabilities, including accounts payable and deferred taxes.

HEART: Read these together too. If one company has a higher long term debt to equity ratio than a peer but a lower total debt to equity ratio, the peer must be leaning more on short term debt.

LEWIS: Last, the limits of balance sheet ratios. Comparisons with peers are weakened by differences in accounting standards and estimates. Ratios may not compare across industries, since a software company and a railroad will never look alike. Interpreting them takes significant judgment. And a balance sheet is a snapshot of a single day, which may not be typical of the rest of the year.

HEART: The takeaway. A common size balance sheet divides every item by total assets. Liquidity runs from the current ratio to the quick ratio to the cash ratio, each stricter than the last. Solvency is measured with long term debt to equity, total debt to equity, the debt ratio and financial leverage, and higher means more risk.

## Recall check

LEWIS Q: A retailer reports a large restructuring charge, and also the results of a division it has agreed to sell. Where does each go, and which one is shown before tax?

HEART: Answer. The restructuring charge stays in income from continuing operations, before tax, and the division goes below continuing operations, net of tax. Here is why. A restructuring charge is an unusual or infrequent item, part of the ongoing business, so it sits above the tax line and is taxed with everything else. The division is a discontinued operation, separated out because it will not be part of future earnings, and because it sits below the tax line it carries its own tax.

HEART Q: A company decides its delivery vans will last longer than it first thought. Does it restate prior years?

LEWIS: Answer. No, it applies the change prospectively. Here is why. A new useful life is a change in accounting estimate, driven by new information. Estimates are judgments that get updated as facts change, so only current and future depreciation changes. Restatement is reserved for policy changes and error corrections.

LEWIS Q: Why are preferred dividends subtracted in basic EPS while common dividends are not?

HEART: Answer. Preferred dividends belong to preferred shareholders, while common dividends are paid out of income that already belongs to common shareholders. Here is why. EPS measures the income available to each common share. Preferred holders are paid first, so their dividends are not available to common. Common dividends simply hand common shareholders part of what is already theirs, so they do not shrink the amount.

HEART Q: A company does a two for one split in the final month of the year. How are the shares outstanding before the split treated in the weighted average?

LEWIS: Answer. They are doubled as if the split happened at the start of the year, and prior years are restated too. Here is why. A split brings in no money and changes no one's share of ownership. It just cuts the same pizza into more slices. Applying it retroactively keeps EPS comparable instead of showing a false collapse, and only shares issued or bought back after the split date are left unadjusted.

LEWIS Q: What makes a convertible security antidilutive?

HEART: Answer. It brings more earnings per new share than basic EPS. Here is why. Conversion adds earnings to the top, the dividends or after tax interest the company would no longer pay, and adds shares to the bottom. Like a new student who scores above the class average, a security bringing more than the current EPS per new share would raise the average. Antidilutive securities are left out, which is why diluted EPS never exceeds basic EPS.

HEART Q: For a convertible bond, why is the interest added back after tax, while convertible preferred dividends are added back in full?

LEWIS: Answer. Because interest is tax deductible and preferred dividends are not. Here is why. If the bonds converted, the company would save the interest but lose the tax deduction on it, so its income rises only by the after tax amount. Preferred dividends are paid out of profit after tax, so removing them adds back the full dividend.

LEWIS Q: A company's options have an exercise price above the stock's average market price for the year. Are they included in diluted EPS?

HEART: Answer. No, they are antidilutive. Here is why. Under the treasury stock method, the exercise cash would buy back more shares at the average price than the options create, so the share count would fall and EPS would rise. Options add nothing to the top, so they dilute only when they are in the money on average.

HEART Q: The exercise price stays the same, but the average share price rises. Does the treasury stock method add more net new shares or fewer?

LEWIS: Answer. More. Here is why. The exercise cash is fixed, and at a higher share price it buys back fewer shares. The net new shares are the free part of the options, the gap between the market price and the exercise price as a fraction of the market price, and that gap has grown.

LEWIS Q: On a common size income statement, which expense is better judged against something other than revenue, and why?

HEART: Answer. Income tax expense, judged against pretax income as the effective tax rate. Here is why. Tax is charged on profit, not on sales. Two companies facing the same tax rate but earning different margins would show very different tax as a share of revenue, so only the comparison with pretax income tells you how heavily profit is taxed.

HEART Q: In an acquisition, a buyer assigns a low fair value to the target's equipment and more of the price to goodwill. What happens to its future net income?

LEWIS: Answer. It is higher. Here is why. Equipment is depreciated over its life, but goodwill is not amortized. It is only tested for impairment. Shifting value from equipment into goodwill means less depreciation expense in future years, which is why analysts watch the price allocation when fair values are subjective.

LEWIS Q: Interest rates rise, and a US GAAP company's available for sale bond portfolio falls in value. Where does the unrealized loss show up?

HEART: Answer. In other comprehensive income, within equity, not in net income. Here is why. Available for sale securities are carried at fair value, so the balance sheet shows the lower value. But their unrealized gains and losses bypass the income statement and go to other comprehensive income. Had the bonds been held to maturity, the loss would stay off the balance sheet and out of income, showing up only in the notes, and had they been trading securities, it would hit net income.

HEART Q: One company has a higher current ratio than a rival but a lower quick ratio. What most likely explains the difference?

LEWIS: Answer. It holds more inventory. Here is why. The current ratio counts all current assets, while the quick ratio drops inventory and keeps only cash, marketable securities and receivables. A company that looks more liquid on the broad measure but less liquid once inventory is removed must be carrying more of its current assets as inventory.

## Key takeaways

HEART: First, a few memory hooks.

LEWIS: For nonrecurring items: unusual stays upstairs, before tax. Discontinued moves downstairs, after tax, with losses booked when foreseen and gains only when the sale is done.

HEART: For accounting changes: policies and errors look back and restate, estimates look forward.

LEWIS: For basic EPS: preferred dividends come out, common dividends never do. Splits and stock dividends rewind to the start of the year, since a split just cuts the pizza into more slices.

HEART: For dilution, the class average test. A security bringing less earnings per new share than basic EPS pulls the average down, so it is dilutive and goes in. One that brings more is antidilutive and stays out. So diluted EPS never tops basic.

LEWIS: What each security brings back to the top: bonds, their interest after tax. Convertible preferred, its dividends in full. Options and warrants, nothing.

HEART: For the treasury stock method: only the free part dilutes. Options count only when the average price beats the exercise price, and a higher average price means more dilution. Average price, never year end price.

LEWIS: For common size: income statement lines over revenue, except tax over pretax income. Balance sheet lines over total assets.

HEART: For intangibles: finite lives are amortized, indefinite lives are tested every year.

LEWIS: For goodwill: bought, never built. Price over the fair value of identifiable net assets, never amortized, tested at least yearly. Shift value from equipment into goodwill, and reported profit rises.

HEART: For financial assets: amortized cost ignores market moves, fair value through other comprehensive income sends them to equity, and fair value through profit and loss sends them to net income. The US GAAP names are held to maturity, available for sale and trading.

LEWIS: For liquidity, a ladder: current, then quick, which drops inventory, then cash, which drops receivables too. For solvency, higher means riskier, and the financial leverage ratio counts every liability.

HEART: Now, how you would say the big ideas to a senior leader.

LEWIS: On one time items: strip out results that will not repeat, but a one time charge that shows up every year is a cost of doing business. The jargon is unusual or infrequent items and discontinued operations.

HEART: On per share earnings: we report profit per share as things stand, and again as if every right to new shares that would shrink each slice were used. The jargon is basic and diluted EPS, the if converted method and the treasury stock method.

LEWIS: On acquisitions: whatever we pay above the value of what we can identify sits on the balance sheet as goodwill. If the deal disappoints, we write it down, which hits profit but not cash. The jargon is goodwill and goodwill impairment.

HEART: On the investment portfolio: the same bond can show its market swings in profit, in equity, or nowhere at all, depending on how we classify it when we buy it. The jargon is amortized cost, fair value through other comprehensive income, and fair value through profit and loss.

LEWIS: And on comparing companies: turn every line into a percentage so size drops out, then check whether short term assets cover short term bills and how much of the business runs on borrowed money. The jargon is common size statements, liquidity ratios and solvency ratios.
