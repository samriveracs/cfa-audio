LEWIS: Reading 30 covers how the cash flow statement ties to the income statement and the balance sheet, building operating cash flow with the direct and indirect methods, rebuilding investing and financing cash flows, converting an indirect statement to the direct format, and the classification choices where IFRS and US GAAP part ways. Reading 31 covers how to judge a cash flow statement, common size cash flow statements, free cash flow to the firm and to equity, and the cash flow ratios.

## Module 30.1, Cash Flow Introduction and Direct Method CFO

HEART: Picture a small furniture maker that lands a huge order from a hotel chain. It builds the tables, ships them in December and sends the invoice. The income statement says December was the best month in the company's history. But the hotel chain pays in March. Meanwhile the lumber supplier wants its money in thirty days, and payroll comes every two weeks. On paper the company is thriving. In the bank account it is running on fumes.

LEWIS: That gap is accrual accounting at work. Revenue is booked when it is earned and expenses when they are incurred, whatever day the cash actually moves. The income statement measures performance on that accrual basis. The statement of cash flows shows the cash that actually came in and went out over the same period.

HEART: Both are flow statements, sometimes called dynamic statements, covering the stretch between two balance sheet dates. But plenty of cash flows never touch the income statement when they happen. Borrow from a bank and you have more cash but no revenue. Buy a building and the cash leaves today, while only a slice of its cost reaches the income statement each year as depreciation.

LEWIS: So the statement shows four things: the actual cash receipts and payments, the split into operating, investing and financing activities, how accrual entries pulled earnings away from cash, and a check on earnings quality.

HEART: It also answers questions about liquidity, solvency and financial flexibility. Does the everyday business bring in enough cash to keep itself going? Can the company repay its debts as they come due? Will it need more financing? Could it handle a surprise bill, or jump on a good opportunity?

LEWIS: The statement's basic job is to explain the change in cash. Beginning cash, plus or minus the cash flow from operating activities, plus or minus investing, plus or minus financing, gives you ending cash. Any of the three sections can be positive or negative. And because both cash balances come straight off the balance sheet, the statement carries its own check figure.

HEART: The three sections line up with different parts of the balance sheet. Operating activities mostly show up in current assets and current liabilities: receivables, inventory, payables and the like. Investing activities show up in noncurrent assets, such as property, plant and equipment and long term investments. Financing activities show up in noncurrent liabilities and equity, the money that comes from lenders and owners.

LEWIS: A simple hook for the three. Operating runs the business. Investing builds it. Financing funds it. Run it, build it, fund it.

HEART: One naming point. Cash flow from operations, cash flow from operating activities and operating cash flow all mean the same thing. The short form is CFO, sometimes O C F, and here it has nothing to do with the chief financial officer. Investing cash flow is CFI, and financing cash flow is CFF.

LEWIS: Now earnings quality. When operating cash flow is near or above net income, earnings are considered high quality, because the profit is backed by cash. When net income keeps running above operating cash flow, year after year, quality is lower. Those accrual profits are not turning into cash.

HEART: So how do you get from the income statement to cash? Through the balance sheet. Whenever revenue or an expense is booked on a different day than the cash moves, the difference waits in a balance sheet account until it clears.

LEWIS: Accounts receivable is the cleanest case. Think of a garden center that lets landscapers buy on account. Every credit sale adds to what the landscapers owe. Every payment they send in reduces it. So the receivables balance at year end equals where it started, plus credit sales, minus cash collected.

HEART: That is a roll forward, the most useful tool in this reading. Beginning balance, plus what adds to it, minus what takes it away, equals ending balance. The two balances come from the balance sheet, sales from the income statement, and cash collected is what the cash flow statement wants. Know any three and you can find the fourth.

LEWIS: Rearrange it and you get the rule in words. Cash collected from customers equals revenue minus the increase in receivables. If receivables grew, customers bought more than they paid for, so cash collected was less than revenue. If receivables shrank, customers paid down old balances, so cash collected was more than revenue.

HEART: Payables mirror that on the other side. When the company buys more from its suppliers than it pays them, accounts payable grow. When it pays more than it buys, payables shrink.

LEWIS: Then there is cash that arrives before the revenue does. Think of a gym that sells annual memberships paid up front in January. The cash is in the bank on day one, so it shows up in the cash flow statement then. But the revenue is earned a month at a time across the year. Until it is earned, the gym owes its members the rest of the year, and that obligation is a liability called unearned revenue.

HEART: So when unearned revenue rises, new advance payments came in faster than the company worked off old ones, and the rise adds to cash collected on top of revenue. That rise is a source of cash, and you add it. And when reported revenue keeps racing ahead of cash collected, it can be an early warning of the aggressive accounting covered in the financial reporting quality reading.

HEART: Now the direct method. It reports operating cash flow as a list of actual cash receipts and cash payments, and their sum is CFO. Think of it as a bank statement sorted into categories.

LEWIS: The usual lines are cash collected from customers, normally the biggest one. Cash paid to suppliers for the inputs to the company's goods and services. Cash operating expenses, such as wages and salaries. Cash paid for interest. And cash paid for taxes.

HEART: Its advantage is information. You see the actual cash behind each source and each purpose, which makes it easier to track how the business is doing from year to year and to predict the cash it will bring in next.

LEWIS: The method is a walk down the income statement. Start at the top with revenue. For each line, find the balance sheet asset or liability that relates to it, usually a current one. See how much it changed. Adjust the income statement figure for that change, then move to the next line.

HEART: The adjustment rules all come from one idea, sources and uses of cash. A rise in an asset is a use of cash. A fall in an asset is a source. A rise in a liability is a source. A fall in a liability is a use. Sources are positive numbers, cash coming in. Uses are negative numbers, cash going out.

LEWIS: And one housekeeping step makes those rules work every time. Treat every expense as a negative number before you adjust it. Cost of goods sold, wages and the rest all start out negative. Then you add sources and subtract uses, and the signs take care of themselves.

HEART: Why does that matter?

LEWIS: Take wages. Suppose wages payable fell during the year. That means the company paid its staff more than this year's wage expense, because it also caught up on wages owed from last year. A fall in a liability is a use of cash, so it pushes the negative number further down. Cash paid to employees comes out bigger than the expense, which is exactly what happened.

HEART: Cash paid to suppliers takes two steps. First, turn cost of goods sold into purchases with the inventory change. Second, turn purchases into cash with the payables change.

LEWIS: Picture a ski shop at the end of its fiscal year. It sold plenty of gear, but it has also filled the back room with next season's stock, so it bought more than it sold, and purchases beat cost of goods sold. That is the inventory step. Then it bought much of that new stock on thirty day terms, so some of it is not paid for yet, and cash paid falls short of purchases. That is the payables step. The cash that actually left is cost of goods sold, plus the stock build, minus the bills not yet paid.

HEART: Interest paid is interest expense adjusted for the change in interest payable. Taxes need a little more care. Tax expense is adjusted for taxes payable, and also for changes in deferred tax assets and deferred tax liabilities, which usually sit among the noncurrent items. If the deferred tax liability grew, part of this year's tax expense has not been paid yet, so cash paid for taxes was less than the expense. Deferred taxes get their own reading later.

LEWIS: And then there is what the direct method leaves out. Depreciation is not a cash payment, so it never appears. A gain or loss on selling equipment or land is not an operating cash flow either, because the cash from that sale belongs in investing. In the direct method, noncash charges and disposal gains and losses are simply skipped.

HEART: Here is the trap. Unearned revenue is a liability, so a rise in it is added, even though the word unearned sounds like it ought to reduce something. And a fall in wages payable makes cash paid bigger than the expense, not smaller. Run every line through the same rule and keep expenses negative.

LEWIS: The takeaway. The cash flow statement explains the change in cash in three sections: run it, build it, fund it. The direct method turns each income statement line into cash with its related balance sheet change.

## Module 30.2, Indirect Method CFO

HEART: The indirect method reaches the same operating cash flow from the other end. Instead of listing receipts and payments, it starts with net income and adjusts it for everything that made profit and cash differ.

LEWIS: Picture the owner of a busy restaurant asking her accountant a simple question. We made a nice profit this year, so why did the bank balance barely move? The accountant's answer, line by line, is the indirect method. It is a bridge from profit to cash.

HEART: In words, operating cash flow equals net income, plus noncash charges, minus the investment in working capital. So there are two kinds of adjustment.

LEWIS: Noncash charges first. Depreciation is the classic. It was subtracted to arrive at net income, but no cash left the company for it this year, so you add it back. The same goes for depletion and amortization.

HEART: The name is a little loose, because the group includes items that raised income as well as items that lowered it. It means anything that passed through the income statement without being an operating cash flow.

LEWIS: Here is the add back list. Depreciation, depletion and amortization. Losses on disposing of assets. Impairments and write downs. Losses on retiring debt early. Amortization of a bond discount. A rise in deferred tax liabilities, or a fall in deferred tax assets. And a share of losses from companies accounted for with the equity method.

HEART: The subtract list mirrors it. Gains on disposing of assets. Gains on retiring debt early. Reversals of earlier impairments and write downs. Amortization of a bond premium. A fall in deferred tax liabilities, or a rise in deferred tax assets.

LEWIS: Two of those deserve a picture. Say a moving company sells an old truck for more than its value on the books. Net income includes that gain. But the whole price the buyer paid is reported as an investing inflow. Leave the gain in operating cash flow as well and you count part of the same money twice. So you subtract the gain.

HEART: A loss works the other way. It reduced net income, but no operating cash left the company because of it. So you add it back. Gains out, losses back, and the full sale price goes to investing. Retiring debt early follows the same logic, except the cash paid to the lenders sits in financing.

LEWIS: Then the bond discount. A bond issued below its face value pays a cash coupon, but the interest expense the company books is the coupon plus a slice of the discount. That extra slice is not cash. So interest expense overstates the cash interest, and you add the difference back. For a bond issued at a premium, interest expense is the coupon minus a slice of the premium. Now the expense understates the cash, so you subtract the difference.

HEART: Then why are these items skipped in the direct method but adjusted in the indirect method?

LEWIS: Because of the starting point. The direct method builds up from cash lines, so noncash items never get in. The indirect method starts from net income, and net income already contains them. So you have to take them back out.

HEART: Now the second kind of adjustment, the investment in working capital. Working capital here means operating working capital, also called noncash working capital: operating current assets minus operating current liabilities.

LEWIS: That definition matters, because it differs from the one in the ratio material, where working capital is simply total current assets minus total current liabilities. For cash flow purposes you leave out cash itself and short term investments, except trading securities, whose cash flows are operating. And you leave out dividends payable and short term interest bearing debt, because those belong to financing.

HEART: Then come the direction rules, the heart of this module. Subtract an increase in an operating current asset. Add a decrease. Add an increase in an operating current liability. Subtract a decrease.

LEWIS: The memory hook: assets move against cash, liabilities move with it. Asset up, cash down. Liability up, cash up.

HEART: And the picture behind it. Receivables are money sitting in your customers' pockets instead of yours. Inventory is cash you have already spent and parked on a shelf. Payables are your suppliers' money still sitting in your account. So more receivables or a fuller shelf means less cash. A bigger stack of unpaid bills means more cash, for now.

LEWIS: Prepaid expenses and unearned revenue fit the same rule, and a short line keeps them straight. More paid early by them, add. More paid early by you, subtract. And when either balance shrinks, flip the sign. Unearned revenue rising means customers paid you early. Prepaid expenses rising means you paid someone else early, such as a year of insurance up front.

HEART: You can also do the working capital step in one go. Measure noncash working capital at the start and the end of the year and subtract the increase. If noncash working capital grew, the business tied up more cash in it, and operating cash flow comes out lower by exactly that increase.

LEWIS: Done correctly, the indirect method lands on exactly the same operating cash flow as the direct method. Only the layout of the operating section differs. Investing and financing look the same either way.

HEART: So which do companies choose? Both IFRS and US GAAP encourage the direct method. Both allow the indirect method, and most companies choose indirect. Under US GAAP, a company that does choose the direct method must also disclose a reconciliation of net income to operating cash flow, which is the indirect method in all but name.

LEWIS: Each has its own strength. The direct method shows where the cash actually came from and where it went. The indirect method shows why net income and operating cash flow differ, which makes it a quick indicator of earnings quality. If the bridge from profit to cash shows a big subtraction for growing receivables year after year, profit is running ahead of cash.

HEART: The trap. A rise in accounts payable tempts people to subtract, because owing more sounds bad. But the company held on to the cash, so it is added. And a gain left in operating gets counted twice once the full sale price lands in investing.

LEWIS: The takeaway. Indirect operating cash flow equals net income, plus noncash charges, minus the investment in working capital. Gains out, losses back. Assets move against cash, and liabilities move with it.

## Module 30.3, Investing and Financing Cash Flows and IFRS/U.S. GAAP Differences

HEART: Now the other two sections. Cash flow from investing activities covers buying and selling long term assets and certain investments. Cash flow from financing activities covers transactions that change the capital structure: borrowing and repaying, issuing shares and buying them back, and paying dividends.

LEWIS: Here is the US GAAP map. Operating inflows are cash from customers, interest and dividends received, and cash from selling trading securities. Operating outflows are payments to employees and suppliers and for other expenses, purchases of trading securities, interest paid on debt, and taxes.

HEART: Investing inflows are cash from selling property, plant and equipment and intangible assets, cash from selling debt and equity investments, and principal collected on loans the company made to others. Investing outflows are the reverse: buying those assets and investments, and making loans to others.

LEWIS: Financing inflows are the principal of new debt and the cash from issuing shares. Financing outflows are principal repaid on debt, payments to buy back shares, and dividends paid to the company's own shareholders.

HEART: A few placements trip people up. Trading securities are held for quick resale, a lot like inventory, so their cash flows are operating. Buying a bond or shares in another company as a longer term investment is investing, but the interest and dividends that investment pays are operating under US GAAP. Borrowed principal is financing, but the interest on it is operating. Leases treated as borrowing split the same way: principal repaid is financing, and interest is operating.

LEWIS: The one people mix up most is dividends. Under US GAAP, dividends received are operating, and dividends paid are financing. Same word, opposite direction, different section.

HEART: There is a clean way to remember the US GAAP rule. Of the four interest and dividend flows, three normally pass through net income: interest received, dividends received and interest paid. Those are operating. Dividends paid never touch the income statement. They come out of retained earnings as a payout to owners, so they are financing.

LEWIS: One more placement point. Some deals move no cash at all, such as buying a warehouse by handing the seller newly issued shares. Those significant noncash investing and financing activities stay out of the three sections and are disclosed separately instead.

HEART: Now rebuilding investing cash flows. In an asset sale, the only cash is what the buyer hands over, called the proceeds. The gain or loss is pure accounting. The company drops the asset's carrying value from its books and compares it with the proceeds. Get more than carrying value and you book a gain. Get less and you book a loss.

LEWIS: Turn that around and you have the rule for the exam. The cash from selling an asset equals its carrying value, also called book value, plus any gain, or minus any loss.

HEART: Picture selling your car to a neighbor. Your depreciation schedule says the car is worth a certain amount on paper. The neighbor hands you more than that, so you book a gain. The cash in your hand is the paper value plus the gain. Nothing else.

LEWIS: The hard part is usually finding the carrying value of what was sold, because the balance sheet only shows totals. That is where roll forwards come back. Think of a parking garage counting cars. Cars at the start of the day, plus cars that came in, minus cars that left, equals cars at the end. Know three and you can find the fourth.

HEART: For equipment you run two of them. First, gross property, plant and equipment. The beginning balance, plus purchases, minus the original cost of what was sold, equals the ending balance. That gives you the original cost of the disposal.

LEWIS: Second, accumulated depreciation. The beginning balance, plus this year's depreciation expense, minus the accumulated depreciation on what was sold, equals the ending balance. That gives you the depreciation that left along with the asset. Original cost minus that depreciation is the carrying value of what was sold.

HEART: There is a shortcut that does both in one pass, working with net book value. Beginning net book value, plus purchases, minus depreciation expense, minus the carrying value of what was sold, equals ending net book value.

LEWIS: And how do you know a sale happened at all? Three clues. Gross equipment rose by less than the company spent on new equipment. Accumulated depreciation rose by less than the depreciation expense. Or a gain or loss on disposal shows up on the income statement.

HEART: Land is easier, because land is not depreciated. Its carrying value is simply its cost. If the land balance fell and no land was bought, the drop is the carrying value of what was sold.

LEWIS: Investing cash flow then equals the proceeds from everything sold, minus the cash spent on everything bought.

HEART: Now financing, which has two parts. The net flow with creditors is new borrowing minus principal repaid. The net flow with shareholders is new shares issued, minus shares bought back, minus cash dividends paid.

LEWIS: Each comes from its own roll forward. The bond balance gives net borrowing. Contributed capital, which is common stock at par plus additional paid in capital, gives the net amount of shares issued or bought back.

HEART: Two simplifications keep the exam work clean. Bonds are assumed issued at par, so no discount or premium amortization moves the balance. And shares bought back are assumed to cost exactly what the company first sold them for.

LEWIS: Dividends take two steps. If dividends declared are not given, find them with retained earnings. Beginning retained earnings, plus net income, minus dividends declared, equals ending retained earnings. Then remember that declared is not the same as paid.

HEART: So treat the dividend declared as a negative and adjust it for the change in dividends payable, using the liability rule. If dividends payable rose, some of what was declared has not been paid yet, so cash paid was less than the amount declared.

LEWIS: Finally, add up all three sections and compare the total with the change in cash on the balance sheet. If they do not match, something is missing.

HEART: Next, converting from indirect to direct. Only the operating section differs between the two methods, so an analyst can rebuild the direct version from the income statement and an indirect statement.

LEWIS: It takes three steps. First, add up all the revenues and gains, and all the expenses and losses. Second, strip out the noncash items, such as depreciation, disposal gains and losses, and the deferred tax movement, and break what remains into its lines: revenue, cost of goods sold, wages, interest and taxes.

HEART: Third, convert each line to cash with its own working capital change. Receivables and unearned revenue go with revenue. Inventory and payables go with cost of goods sold. Wages payable goes with wages, interest payable with interest, and taxes payable with taxes. It is the direct method again, with a different starting point.

LEWIS: Now the differences between IFRS and US GAAP, which are tested heavily and are mostly about which section a cash flow sits in.

HEART: US GAAP, as we said, gives no choice: interest received, dividends received and interest paid are operating, and dividends paid are financing.

LEWIS: IFRS gives a choice. Interest and dividends received can be operating or investing. Interest and dividends paid can be operating or financing. The company picks a policy and applies it consistently from year to year.

HEART: The hook for IFRS: receipts can invest, payments can finance, and operating is always allowed. A receipt can move to investing, because it is a return on an investment. A payment can move to financing, because it is a cost of raising capital.

LEWIS: Taxes next. Under US GAAP, all income taxes paid are operating, even when the tax comes from an investing or financing transaction. Under IFRS, taxes are operating unless they can be tied to a specific investing or financing transaction, and then they can go with that transaction.

HEART: Picture a regional newspaper that sells its old downtown office building at a big gain and owes tax on that gain. Under US GAAP, the sale price goes to investing and the tax goes to operating, so operating cash flow looks weaker that year for a reason that has nothing to do with selling newspapers. Under IFRS, the tax can sit in investing right beside the sale.

LEWIS: Bank overdrafts. Under US GAAP, an overdraft is treated as debt, a borrowing. Under IFRS, an overdraft that is repayable on demand and swings back and forth as part of everyday cash management is treated as part of cash, a negative piece of it.

HEART: And the format. Both standards prefer the direct method and allow the indirect. Only US GAAP adds the requirement to disclose the net income reconciliation when a company chooses the direct method.

LEWIS: Why does all this matter to an analyst? Comparability. Take two identical companies. One reports under US GAAP, so its interest paid comes out of operating cash flow. The other reports under IFRS and puts interest paid in financing. The IFRS company shows higher operating cash flow, by the amount of its interest, for exactly the same business.

HEART: So before you compare operating cash flow, or any ratio built on it, move the items into the same sections. And the trap runs both ways. An IFRS company putting dividends paid in operating is allowed. A US GAAP company must put dividends received in operating, even though owning shares in another company feels like an investment.

LEWIS: The takeaway. Cash from a sale is carrying value plus the gain, and roll forwards find whatever is missing. Under US GAAP, the interest and dividend flows that pass through net income are operating, and dividends paid are financing. Under IFRS, receipts can invest and payments can finance.

## Module 31.1, Analyzing Statements of Cash Flows II

HEART: Reading 31 turns from building the statement to judging it, with the same goal as before: liquidity, solvency and financial flexibility.

LEWIS: Start with the major sources and uses of cash in each section, and keep in mind where the company is in its life cycle. Picture a young electric bike maker that sells through independent bike shops. It is building inventory for every model, and the shops pay on long credit terms. Operating cash flow is negative, and the company fills the gap by borrowing and selling shares.

HEART: That is normal early on, but lenders and investors will not fund an operating cash drain forever. Eventually operating activities have to bring in cash. Given enough time, a healthy business pays its own way. Its operating cash flow runs above its capital spending, with cash to spare for the people who lent to it and the people who own it.

LEWIS: Next, ask how the operating cash flow is being produced. The healthy source is the business itself: sales, margins and collections. But a company can also boost operating cash flow for a while by shrinking its working capital. It can sell down inventory, collect receivables faster, or pay its suppliers later.

HEART: That last one is called stretching payables, and none of these moves can repeat forever. Inventory can only shrink until the shelves are bare, and receivables bottom out at zero. And suppliers who keep getting paid later will eventually stop offering credit. So a jump in operating cash flow that comes mostly from working capital is a one time lift, not a new level.

LEWIS: Then compare operating cash flow with net income. When the two keep a steady relationship over the years, that points to quality earnings. When profit keeps pulling well ahead of cash, the accounting may be aggressive, or even improper, for example counting sales before they are truly made or pushing costs into later years. And the more either number jumps around from year to year, the more risk you are looking at.

HEART: The investing section tells a story too. Rising capital spending usually means growth. Falling capital spending, or selling off long term assets to raise cash, deserves a question. A company that skips replacing old equipment saves cash today and may face a bigger replacement bill later. Investing also shows acquisitions and purchases of securities.

LEWIS: The financing section shows where outside money came from and where cash went back out. Is the company issuing debt or shares? Is it repaying debt, buying back stock, paying dividends? One combination always deserves a second look: borrowing money and handing it straight to shareholders through buybacks or dividends.

HEART: Now common size cash flow statements. As with the other statements, you scale the numbers so that different years, and companies of different sizes, can be compared.

LEWIS: There are two ways to do it. The first states every line as a percentage of revenue. The second states each inflow as a share of total cash inflows, and each outflow as a share of total cash outflows.

HEART: The revenue version is the handy one for spotting trends and forecasting. Picture a regional grocery chain whose dividend payments have grown faster than its sales for several years running, so dividends take a bigger slice of every sales dollar. Seeing every line against revenue makes that drift obvious. And once you forecast revenue, any cash flow that tends to move with revenue follows from it.

LEWIS: Now free cash flow. Free cash flow is the cash a company has available for discretionary use after paying for the capital spending it needs to keep and grow the business. It is a key input for valuation, and there are two main versions.

HEART: Free cash flow to the firm, FCFF, is the cash available to all of the company's capital providers, both shareholders and lenders. Free cash flow to equity, FCFE, is the cash available to common shareholders alone, after the lenders have been taken care of.

LEWIS: Picture a pizza shop funded partly by its owners and partly by a bank loan. FCFF is the whole pizza that is left after paying for the ovens. FCFE is what the owners keep after the bank takes its slices, adjusted for any fresh loan the bank hands over.

HEART: Start with FCFF from operating cash flow. In words, FCFF equals operating cash flow, plus cash interest paid after tax, minus fixed capital investment.

LEWIS: Why add the interest back?

HEART: Because interest goes to lenders, and lenders are part of the firm in this measure. Under US GAAP, interest paid was already subtracted inside operating cash flow, so you put it back.

LEWIS: And why only after tax?

HEART: Because paying interest also cut the tax bill, and that tax saving stayed inside operating cash flow. So the real drag on operating cash flow from paying interest was only the interest net of that saving. That is the amount you add back: interest times one minus the tax rate.

LEWIS: Fixed capital investment is cash spent on fixed assets minus cash received from selling fixed assets, in other words net capital spending. It is not the same as investing cash flow. The investing section can also include buying securities and lending to others, and those are not fixed capital.

HEART: You can also start from net income. FCFF equals net income, plus noncash charges, plus cash interest paid after tax, minus fixed capital investment, minus working capital investment. Notice that net income, plus noncash charges, minus working capital investment, is just operating cash flow by the indirect method. So the two versions are the same calculation.

LEWIS: Then the IFRS adjustments. If an IFRS company put interest paid in financing, its operating cash flow never had interest taken out, so there is nothing to add back. If it put dividends paid in operating, add the dividends back, because dividends go to shareholders, who are also part of the firm. And there is no tax adjustment for dividends, because dividends are not tax deductible.

HEART: Now FCFE. In words, FCFE equals operating cash flow, minus fixed capital investment, plus net borrowing. Net borrowing is new debt issued minus debt repaid. If the company repaid more than it borrowed, net borrowing is negative and FCFE goes down.

LEWIS: Notice there is no interest add back here. Shareholders get only what is left after the lenders are paid, so the interest stays out. And if an IFRS company subtracted dividends paid in operating, add them back, because dividends are paid out of FCFE, not a cost to deduct before you get there.

HEART: The link between the two: FCFE equals FCFF, minus after tax interest, plus net borrowing. Start with the whole pizza, take out the interest that went to lenders net of the tax saving, then add net borrowing, the new money lent minus the principal repaid.

LEWIS: The trap. When you work out FCFE from operating cash flow, do not add interest back out of habit from FCFF. And do not drop in investing cash flow as a stand in for fixed capital investment when the investing section includes securities or loans.

HEART: Last, the cash flow ratios. They come in two families, performance ratios and coverage ratios, and you can compare them over time or across companies. Here is a hook for all of them. Operating cash flow sits on top, and the name tells you what sits underneath.

LEWIS: Performance ratios first. Cash flow to revenue is operating cash flow over net revenue, the operating cash generated by each dollar of sales. Cash return on assets is operating cash flow over average total assets, the cash return to all providers of capital. Cash return on equity is operating cash flow over average total equity, the cash return to shareholders.

HEART: Cash to income is operating cash flow over operating income, a measure of how well operations turn profit into cash. And cash flow per share is a cash version of basic earnings per share: operating cash flow minus preferred dividends, over the weighted average number of common shares. If an IFRS company put common dividends in operating, add them back first.

LEWIS: Coverage ratios measure the ability to meet obligations. Debt coverage is operating cash flow over total debt, a measure of financial risk and leverage. Higher means more cash for each dollar owed.

HEART: Interest coverage is the one with a twist on top. It is operating cash flow, plus interest paid, plus taxes paid, all over interest paid. Interest and taxes go back in because operating cash flow is already after both, and the question is how much cash was available to pay interest. If an IFRS company put interest in financing, its operating cash flow was never reduced by interest, so you skip the interest add back.

LEWIS: Then four more, each asking whether operating cash can pay for one particular thing. The reinvestment ratio is operating cash flow over cash paid for long term assets. The debt payment ratio is operating cash flow over cash paid to repay long term debt. The dividend payment ratio is operating cash flow over dividends paid. And the investing and financing ratio is operating cash flow over all the cash outflows for investing and financing activities. For each one, a value above one means operating cash alone could cover it.

HEART: Lenders lean hard on these coverage measures. A bank or credit union underwriting a business loan cares most about whether the borrower's cash covers the payments, because loans are repaid from cash, not from accounting profit.

LEWIS: The takeaway. Judge operating cash flow by where it comes from and how well it tracks net income. FCFF adds back after tax interest and takes out fixed capital investment. FCFE takes out fixed capital investment and adds net borrowing. And in every cash flow ratio, operating cash flow sits on top.

## Recall check

LEWIS Q: Receivables rose during the year, and no other balance tied to sales changed. Is cash collected from customers higher or lower than revenue?

HEART: Answer. Lower. Here is why. Receivables grow when customers buy on credit faster than they pay. Part of this year's revenue is still in customers' pockets, so cash collected equals revenue minus the rise in receivables.

HEART Q: Why is depreciation skipped entirely in the direct method but added back in the indirect method?

LEWIS: Answer. Because the indirect method starts from net income, which already has depreciation subtracted. Here is why. The direct method builds operating cash flow from actual receipts and payments, and depreciation is never a payment, so it never gets in. The indirect method starts from profit, and profit was reduced by depreciation. Adding it back undoes a charge that cost no cash this year.

LEWIS Q: Under the indirect method, accounts payable fell during the year. Do you add or subtract the change?

HEART: Answer. Subtract it. Here is why. When payables shrink, the company paid off some of last year's bills on top of this year's purchases, so cash paid to suppliers ran ahead of purchases. Liabilities move with cash, so a falling liability means less cash.

HEART Q: A software company sells annual subscriptions paid up front, and its unearned revenue rose sharply. All else equal, how does operating cash flow compare with net income?

LEWIS: Answer. Operating cash flow comes out above net income. Here is why. Customers paid cash for service the company has not yet delivered, so the cash arrived before the revenue. Unearned revenue is a liability, and a rising liability is a source of cash. More paid early by them, add.

LEWIS Q: A company sells a machine for more than its carrying value. How does the sale show up on an indirect method statement?

HEART: Answer. The gain is subtracted in the operating section, and the full sale price appears as an investing inflow. Here is why. Net income already includes the gain, but the cash from the sale belongs in investing. If the gain stayed in operating as well, part of the same money would be counted twice. The cash from the sale equals the carrying value plus the gain.

HEART Q: Under US GAAP, where do dividends received and dividends paid go?

LEWIS: Answer. Dividends received are operating, and dividends paid are financing. Here is why. Dividends received pass through net income as investment income, and under US GAAP the interest and dividend flows that pass through net income are operating. Dividends paid never touch the income statement. They are a payout to the company's own owners.

LEWIS Q: Two identical companies differ in one choice. The IFRS company puts interest paid in financing, while the US GAAP company must put it in operating. Which shows higher operating cash flow?

HEART: Answer. The IFRS company. Here is why. Its operating section never has the interest payment subtracted, because that payment sits in financing. The businesses are the same, so the gap is purely classification, and an analyst should line the sections up before comparing.

HEART Q: A company sells a building at a gain and pays tax on that gain. Under US GAAP, which section holds the tax, and what can IFRS do differently?

LEWIS: Answer. Under US GAAP the tax is operating, while IFRS can put it in investing beside the sale. Here is why. US GAAP classifies all income taxes paid as operating, whatever caused them. IFRS keeps taxes in operating unless they can be tied to a specific investing or financing transaction, and a tax on the gain from selling a building can be. So under US GAAP the sale lifts investing while its tax weighs on operating.

LEWIS Q: A company's operating cash flow jumped this year, and nearly all of the jump came from paying suppliers much later than before. How should an analyst view that?

HEART: Answer. As a one time lift, not a lasting improvement. Here is why. Stretching payables keeps cash in the business for a while, but suppliers will not extend credit forever. Sustainable operating cash flow comes from sales, margins and collections, not from shrinking working capital.

HEART Q: Why does FCFF add back interest paid after tax rather than the full interest payment?

LEWIS: Answer. Because paying interest also lowered taxes, and that saving is already inside operating cash flow. Here is why. Interest is tax deductible, so each dollar of interest cost the company less than a dollar once the tax saving is counted. The net drag was interest times one minus the tax rate, so that is exactly what goes back.

LEWIS Q: A company repays more debt this year than it borrows. Is its FCFE above or below its FCFF?

HEART: Answer. Below. Here is why. FCFE equals FCFF, minus after tax interest, plus net borrowing. Here net borrowing is negative, so both adjustments push FCFE down. Shareholders only get what is left after the lenders receive their interest and the extra principal repaid.

HEART Q: Why does the cash interest coverage ratio add interest paid and taxes paid back to operating cash flow?

LEWIS: Answer. Because operating cash flow under US GAAP is already after both, and the ratio asks how much cash was available before paying them. Here is why. Interest is the obligation being tested, so taking it out of the cash meant to pay it would understate coverage. Taxes are worked out after interest is deducted, so the cash available to pay interest is the cash before taxes as well.

## Key takeaways

HEART: First, a few memory hooks.

LEWIS: For the three sections: run it, build it, fund it. Beginning cash plus all three must equal ending cash.

HEART: For working capital: assets move against cash, liabilities move with it. Inventory on the shelf is cash you have spent. Unpaid bills in the drawer are cash you have kept. And for advance payments: more paid early by them, add. More paid early by you, subtract.

LEWIS: For the two methods: the direct method is the bank statement, the indirect method is the bridge from profit to cash. Same operating cash flow either way.

HEART: For asset sales: gains out, losses back, and the full price goes to investing. Cash from a sale is the carrying value plus the gain, or minus the loss.

LEWIS: For any missing number: beginning, plus what came in, minus what went out, equals ending. Know three, find the fourth.

HEART: For US GAAP: if an interest or dividend flow passes through net income, it is operating, which leaves dividends paid alone in financing. Taxes are always operating, and an overdraft is debt.

LEWIS: For IFRS interest and dividends: receipts can invest, payments can finance, and operating is always allowed. Taxes can follow the transaction that caused them, and an overdraft repayable on demand can count as cash.

HEART: For free cash flow: FCFF is the whole pizza, before anything goes to lenders. FCFE is the owners' share, after lenders are paid, plus net borrowing. Interest goes back into FCFF after tax, because the tax saving was already counted.

LEWIS: For the ratios: operating cash flow is the starting point of every numerator, and the ratio's name tells you the denominator.

HEART: Now, how you would say the big ideas to a senior leader.

LEWIS: On profit and cash: profit and cash can drift apart, and when profit keeps running ahead of cash, someone should ask why. The jargon is accrual accounting, operating cash flow and earnings quality.

HEART: On growth: a fast growing business often burns cash before it makes cash, because it has to stock up and wait for customers to pay. That is fine for a while, but not forever. The jargon is investment in working capital and negative operating cash flow early in the life cycle.

LEWIS: On a sudden cash improvement: if cash flow jumped because we paid suppliers later or ran inventory down, that is a one time lift, not a new run rate. The jargon is stretching payables and an unsustainable reduction in working capital.

HEART: On comparing companies: check where each one puts interest, dividends and taxes, because international rules allow choices that the US rules do not. The jargon is IFRS versus US GAAP classification of operating, investing and financing cash flows.

LEWIS: On free cash flow: it is what is left after keeping the business running and growing, first for everyone who funded us, then for the owners once the lenders are paid. The jargon is free cash flow to the firm, free cash flow to equity and fixed capital investment.

HEART: And on borrowing capacity: lenders care less about reported profit than about whether cash covers the payments. The jargon is cash flow coverage ratios, such as debt coverage and cash interest coverage.
