## The big picture

Inventory is where accounting choices quietly move profit from one period to another. A company buys goods at different prices during the year, sells some of them, and keeps the rest. The total it paid is a fact. The real question is how that total gets split between the income statement, as cost of goods sold, and the balance sheet, as ending inventory. Two things decide the split. The first is the cost formula the company chooses, and the second is whether the inventory has lost value.

This episode covers three modules. Module 32.1 covers how inventory is measured, at the lower of cost and net realizable value, and what a writedown does to the statements and the ratios. Module 32.2 is the heavy one. It shows how rising and falling prices push FIFO and LIFO results apart, how the LIFO reserve lets you restate a LIFO company onto a FIFO basis, and what a LIFO liquidation does to profit. Module 32.3 covers what companies disclose about inventory, how an analyst reads those disclosures, and the inventory ratios.

The pieces connect in a simple way. Measurement caps the number on the balance sheet. The cost formula decides how much cost reaches the income statement, and when. Disclosure is how you, as the analyst, find out which choices were made, so you can undo them when you compare companies. On the exam, expect direction of effect questions, at least one LIFO to FIFO conversion, and IFRS versus U.S. GAAP contrasts on writedown reversals and on which methods are allowed.

## Module 32.1, Inventory Measurement

Start with the identity that drives this whole episode. Beginning inventory plus purchases equals the cost of goods available for sale. That pool of cost then goes to exactly two places. Cost of goods sold goes to the income statement, and ending inventory stays on the balance sheet. So beginning inventory plus purchases equals cost of goods sold plus ending inventory, and any choice that puts more cost in one bucket must put less in the other.

What counts as cost in the first place? Inventory cost includes the purchase price after trade discounts, plus import duties, taxes the company cannot recover, and the freight and handling needed to bring the goods in. For a manufacturer it also includes conversion costs, meaning direct labor and a systematic share of production overhead. Some costs are expensed right away instead. These include abnormal waste of materials or labor, storage costs that are not a necessary step in production, administrative overhead, and selling costs. The line matters because capitalizing a cost delays the expense until the goods are sold, which raises current profit and current assets.

Next come the cost formulas, also called cost flow assumptions. Specific identification tracks the actual cost of each item, which suits unique, high value goods. Weighted average cost spreads the total cost evenly across all the units available. FIFO, which stands for first in, first out, assumes the oldest units are sold first, so ending inventory holds the newest costs. LIFO, which stands for last in, first out, assumes the newest units are sold first, so ending inventory holds the oldest costs. IFRS allows the first three, and U.S. GAAP allows all four, so LIFO is a U.S. GAAP only method.

Now the heart of this module. Inventory is a current asset, and an asset should not sit on the balance sheet above the cash it can bring in. So under IFRS, inventory is measured at the lower of cost and net realizable value, or N R V. N R V equals the estimated selling price in the ordinary course of business, minus the estimated costs to complete the goods, minus the estimated costs to sell them. If N R V falls below cost, the company writes the inventory down to N R V and recognizes the loss in that period.

Why does the rule exist? The loss in value happened now, when selling prices fell or the goods became damaged or obsolete. It belongs in this period's profit, not in some later period when the goods finally sell. And it keeps the balance sheet honest about what the inventory can really bring in.

U.S. GAAP splits the rule by cost formula. For FIFO, weighted average and specific identification, the rule matches IFRS, the lower of cost and N R V. For LIFO and for the retail method, which retailers use to estimate cost from selling prices, U.S. GAAP uses the lower of cost or market. Market here means current replacement cost, but with a ceiling and a floor. The ceiling is N R V, and the floor is N R V minus a normal profit margin. So market is simply the middle value of three numbers, replacement cost, N R V, and N R V minus the normal margin.

Try a quick example. A unit of LIFO inventory cost 100 dollars. Its N R V is 85 dollars, and the normal profit margin is 10 dollars, so the floor is 75 dollars. Replacement cost has dropped to 70 dollars. The middle of 70, 75 and 85 is 75, so market is 75 dollars, and the lower of cost and market is 75 dollars, a writedown of 25 dollars per unit. A FIFO company holding the same unit, under either IFRS or U.S. GAAP, would write it down only 15 dollars, to its N R V of 85 dollars.

Now the difference between the frameworks that is tested most often. Under IFRS, if N R V later recovers, the company reverses the writedown, but only up to the amount of the original writedown. So inventory can never climb back above its original cost. The reversal is recognized in profit, as a reduction in the inventory expense for that period. Under U.S. GAAP, reversals are prohibited, and the written down amount becomes the new cost basis.

Here is a worked example with round numbers. A company holds 1,000 units that cost 100 dollars each, so 100,000 dollars in total. At year end the expected selling price drops to 95 dollars, and finishing and selling each unit will cost 10 dollars, so N R V is 85 dollars a unit. The company writes inventory down by 15 dollars a unit, records a 15,000 dollar loss, and carries the inventory at 85,000 dollars. The next year, before the goods are sold, prices recover and N R V rises to 105 dollars a unit. Under IFRS, inventory goes back up to 100,000 dollars, a reversal of 15,000 dollars, and not to 105,000 dollars, while under U.S. GAAP it stays at 85,000 dollars.

Here is the trap. The tempting answer writes inventory up to the new, higher N R V. That does not happen for ordinary inventory under either framework, because the ceiling on any reversal is original cost. There is one narrow exception you should recognize. Producers of agricultural, forest and mineral products, and commodity broker traders, may carry inventory at N R V or at fair value less costs to sell, even above cost, with changes in value running through profit or loss. Treat that as an industry exception, not the general rule.

Now the financial statement effects, which is what this module really tests. In the period of a writedown, inventory falls, so current assets, total assets and equity all fall. The loss is often reported inside cost of sales, so gross profit, operating profit and net income fall too. The ratios follow. The current ratio falls, and debt to equity rises, because equity shrank while debt did not. Inventory turnover and total asset turnover rise, because their denominators got smaller.

Here is the subtle part. Because the inventory now carries a lower cost, cost of sales is lower when those goods are sold next period, so next period's gross margin looks better. A large writedown hurts this year and flatters next year. That is why analysts look hard at a writedown that seems bigger than the facts justify, since an oversized writedown shifts profit into the future.

Hold onto this rule. Under IFRS, inventory is carried at the lower of cost and N R V, where N R V is the expected selling price minus the costs to complete and sell. A writedown can be reversed only up to original cost, and U.S. GAAP never allows the reversal.

## Module 32.2, Inflation Impact on FIFO and LIFO

Here is the core idea. When the cost of the goods a company buys is rising, the newest units are the most expensive. FIFO sends the old, cheap units to cost of goods sold and leaves the new, expensive units on the balance sheet. LIFO does the opposite. It sends the newest, most expensive costs to cost of goods sold and leaves the oldest, cheapest costs in inventory. Weighted average lands in between.

Walk through a tiny example. A company starts the year with no inventory. It buys 100 units at 10 dollars, then another 100 units at 12 dollars, so the goods available cost 2,200 dollars. It sells 100 units at 20 dollars each, so revenue is 2,000 dollars. Under FIFO, the units sold are the first 100 at 10 dollars, so cost of goods sold is 1,000 dollars and ending inventory is 1,200 dollars. Under LIFO, the units sold are the last 100 at 12 dollars, so cost of goods sold is 1,200 dollars and ending inventory is 1,000 dollars.

Under weighted average, the average cost is 2,200 dollars divided by 200 units, or 11 dollars a unit, so cost of goods sold and ending inventory are both 1,100 dollars. Gross profit is 1,000 dollars under FIFO, 900 dollars under weighted average, and 800 dollars under LIFO. In every case, cost of goods sold plus ending inventory equals 2,200 dollars. The method never changes the total, only the split.

Now add taxes and cash. Suppose the tax rate is 25 percent and the company uses the same method for taxes as for reporting. LIFO's taxable profit is 200 dollars lower than FIFO's, so its taxes are 50 dollars lower and its cash flow is 50 dollars higher. Before taxes, the choice of method changes no cash at all, because the company paid the same amount for its goods either way. In the United States, a company that uses LIFO for taxes must also use it in its financial statements. So LIFO's lower reported profit is the price of its tax saving.

Here is the full rule set for rising prices with stable or growing quantities. Compared with FIFO, LIFO gives higher cost of goods sold, lower gross profit, lower net income and lower income taxes. On the balance sheet, LIFO gives lower inventory, lower current assets, lower working capital and lower equity. Operating cash flow is higher under LIFO because of the tax saving. Weighted average sits between the two on every line.

Now the ratios, which is where most exam questions live. Under LIFO in a period of rising prices, gross margin and net margin are lower. The current ratio is lower, because inventory is lower. Inventory turnover is higher and days of inventory on hand are lower, because the numerator, cost of goods sold, is bigger and the denominator, inventory, is smaller. Debt to equity is higher, because equity is lower.

Two traps hide here. First, the quick ratio leaves inventory out, so LIFO's low inventory does not hurt it, and LIFO's tax saving adds cash, so the quick ratio can actually be higher under LIFO. Second, LIFO's higher turnover is not a sign of better inventory management. It comes from the method, because old, low costs sit in the denominator. And for return on assets and return on equity, both the numerator and the denominator are lower under LIFO, so the direction depends on the numbers.

Which method gives better information? FIFO ending inventory is close to current cost, so FIFO gives the more useful balance sheet. LIFO cost of goods sold is close to current cost, so LIFO gives the better measure of current profit margins. The analyst wants the best of both, and the tool for that is the LIFO reserve.

When prices fall, every direction flips, as long as quantities are stable or growing. LIFO now puts the newest, cheapest costs into cost of goods sold, so LIFO shows the higher profit and pays the higher taxes. FIFO shows the lower profit and the lower inventory, and its inventory is still the figure closer to current cost. On the exam, read the direction of prices first, and only then answer.

The LIFO reserve is the gap between inventory on a FIFO basis and inventory as reported under LIFO. In words, the LIFO reserve equals FIFO inventory minus LIFO inventory. In the tiny example, it was 1,200 minus 1,000, or 200 dollars. U.S. companies on LIFO disclose the reserve, and when prices keep rising it is positive and tends to grow. Like securities carried at amortized cost on a bank or credit union balance sheet, LIFO inventory can sit far from current value, with the gap visible only in a note.

Three conversions follow, and they are worth saying slowly. First, FIFO inventory equals LIFO inventory plus the LIFO reserve. Second, FIFO cost of goods sold equals LIFO cost of goods sold minus the change in the LIFO reserve, where the change is the ending reserve minus the beginning reserve. Third, FIFO pretax income is higher than LIFO pretax income by that same change in the reserve, and FIFO net income is higher by the change in the reserve times one minus the tax rate.

The balance sheet needs one more step. When you add the whole reserve to inventory, something must balance it. Equity rises by the reserve times one minus the tax rate, because those extra cumulative profits would have been taxed. The rest, the reserve times the tax rate, goes to taxes, either as a higher deferred tax liability or as lower cash, depending on the assumption you are given.

Here is a worked example. A company reports LIFO inventory of 600 million dollars. Its LIFO reserve was 80 million dollars at the start of the year and 100 million dollars at the end. LIFO cost of goods sold is 900 million dollars, and the tax rate is 25 percent. FIFO inventory is 600 plus 100, or 700 million dollars.

The reserve rose by 20 million dollars, so FIFO cost of goods sold is 900 minus 20, or 880 million dollars. FIFO pretax income is 20 million dollars higher, and FIFO net income is 20 times 0.75, or 15 million dollars higher. On the balance sheet, equity is 100 times 0.75, or 75 million dollars higher, and the remaining 25 million dollars sits in deferred taxes or comes out of cash.

Here is the trap. To restate inventory and equity, you use the ending reserve, the whole 100 million. To restate cost of goods sold and income, you use only the change in the reserve, the 20 million. Mixing these up gives an answer that is far off, and it will be among the choices. Also watch the sign. When the reserve grows, FIFO cost of goods sold is lower than LIFO, so you subtract the increase.

Converting to FIFO moves the ratios as well. Inventory and current assets rise, so the current ratio rises. Cost of goods sold falls while inventory rises, so inventory turnover falls. Equity rises, so debt to equity falls. When you compare a LIFO company with a FIFO company, convert the LIFO company first, and this comes up often because IFRS does not permit LIFO.

Now LIFO liquidation. Under LIFO, inventory builds up in layers, each carried at the cost of the year it was added. As long as the company buys at least as many units as it sells, the old layers are never touched. A LIFO liquidation happens when the company sells more units than it buys, so inventory quantities fall and old, low cost layers flow into cost of goods sold.

With rising prices, those old costs are far below current costs. So cost of goods sold is understated, and gross profit, net income and income taxes all jump. That profit boost is real in accounting terms, but it is not sustainable, because an old layer can be used up only once. The company also gives back part of its past tax deferral, because it now pays tax on gains that LIFO had been postponing.

A quick example. A LIFO company sells 1,000 units from a layer bought years ago at 20 dollars each, while the current cost is 50 dollars. Cost of goods sold is 30 dollars a unit below current cost, so pretax profit is 30,000 dollars higher than it would be if the company had replaced those units. Strip that 30,000 dollars out when you judge the earnings trend.

Why do liquidations happen? Sometimes for real reasons, such as a strike, a supply shortage, or a deliberate plan to shrink inventory when demand falls. But management can also cause one on purpose, by delaying purchases near year end to pull cheap old costs into earnings. Here is the signal to watch. In a period of rising prices, a LIFO reserve that falls is a red flag for a LIFO liquidation.

Hold onto these rules. FIFO inventory equals LIFO inventory plus the LIFO reserve. FIFO cost of goods sold equals LIFO cost of goods sold minus the change in the reserve. And when prices rise, LIFO means higher cost of goods sold, lower profit, lower taxes, lower inventory and higher cash flow.

## Module 32.3, Presentation and Disclosure

Inventory sits in current assets on the balance sheet, and the notes tell you how it got there. Under IFRS, the key disclosures come in a short list. First, the accounting policy, including the cost formula used. Second, the total carrying amount, and the carrying amount by category, such as raw materials, work in progress and finished goods. Third, the amount of inventory recognized as an expense during the period, which is essentially cost of goods sold.

Fourth, the amount of any writedown recognized as an expense. Fifth, the amount of any reversal of a writedown, and the circumstances that caused it. Sixth, the carrying amount of inventory pledged as security for liabilities. A lender reads that last item closely, because inventory pledged to one creditor is not available to the others. U.S. GAAP disclosures are similar, except that there are no reversals to report, and LIFO users disclose the LIFO reserve.

Consistency is the theme behind these rules. Under IFRS, a company must use the same cost formula for all inventories of a similar nature and use, although inventories that differ in nature or use may justify different formulas. That stops a company from choosing a method warehouse by warehouse to steer its profit.

Next, changes in inventory method. Under IFRS, a company may change its cost formula only if a new standard requires it or the change gives reliable and more relevant information. The change is applied retrospectively, which means prior periods are restated as if the new method had always been used. U.S. GAAP also requires the company to justify the change as preferable, and it also applies changes retrospectively, with one important exception. A change to LIFO is applied prospectively, because rebuilding years of past LIFO layers is usually impractical, so the inventory carrying amount at the date of the change simply becomes the first LIFO layer.

Here is the trap. The tempting answer says every method change is restated retrospectively under both frameworks, which misses the U.S. GAAP exception for a change to LIFO. Remember too that a change in method breaks the comparison across years, and it can be used to manage earnings. When you see one, read the restated figures, and ask whether the stated reason makes economic sense.

The breakdown by category is one of the most useful disclosures, so think about what each pattern means. If raw materials and work in progress are growing faster than sales, the company may be gearing up for higher demand. If finished goods are piling up faster than sales while raw materials shrink, demand may be weakening and production may be getting cut. In that second case, future writedowns become more likely.

Other warning signs follow the same logic. Inventory growing much faster than sales means turnover is falling, and slow moving goods risk becoming obsolete. Frequent or large writedowns suggest weak inventory management or products with short lives. Under IFRS, a reversal of a writedown raises profit, so check whether a good period was helped by one. Analysts also look beyond the notes, to management commentary, industry data and the sales trend, to judge whether inventory levels make sense.

Now the inventory ratios. Inventory turnover equals cost of goods sold divided by average inventory. Days of inventory on hand equals 365 divided by inventory turnover, which is the same as average inventory divided by one day's worth of cost of goods sold. The gross profit margin equals gross profit divided by revenue.

A quick example. Cost of goods sold is 1,825 million dollars and average inventory is 365 million dollars. Turnover is 1,825 divided by 365, or 5 times a year. Days of inventory on hand is 365 divided by 5, or 73 days.

Here is the trap. Turnover uses cost of goods sold, not revenue, because inventory is carried at cost. Dividing revenue by inventory mixes a selling price with a cost, so it overstates turnover and understates days on hand. Also use average inventory, meaning beginning plus ending inventory, divided by two, when you are given both balances.

The gross profit margin belongs with these ratios because inventory cost is the main expense behind it. Read the two together. A falling gross margin alongside rising inventory can mean the company is discounting to move goods that are not selling. A rising gross margin right after a big writedown may simply reflect the lower cost basis, not better pricing.

How do you read these numbers? High turnover, or few days on hand, can mean efficient management. But it can also mean the company holds too little inventory and is losing sales when shelves run empty. So compare with sales growth. If turnover is high but sales are growing more slowly than the industry, inventory may be too thin. If turnover is low compared with peers, inventory may be slow moving or obsolete, and writedowns may follow.

And remember the method effects from the last module. A LIFO company in a period of rising prices shows higher turnover and lower gross margin than an otherwise identical FIFO company. Before comparing two companies, put them on the same method.

Hold onto this rule. Inventory turnover equals cost of goods sold divided by average inventory, and days on hand equals 365 divided by turnover. The disclosures that matter most are the cost formula, the breakdown by category, writedowns and reversals, and inventory pledged as collateral.

## Recall check

Q: Under IFRS, inventory that cost 50 dollars a unit was written down to an N R V of 40 dollars. A year later, before it is sold, N R V recovers to 55 dollars. What is the new carrying amount per unit?

Answer: 50 dollars. IFRS allows the reversal, but only up to the original writedown, so the carrying amount returns to cost and not to 55 dollars. Under U.S. GAAP, it would stay at 40 dollars, because reversals are not allowed.

Q: Prices are rising and quantities are stable. Compared with FIFO, which is most likely higher for a company using LIFO, its operating cash flow, its current ratio, or its gross margin?

Answer: Operating cash flow. LIFO puts the newest, highest costs into cost of goods sold, which lowers taxable income and taxes, assuming the same method is used for taxes. The current ratio and gross margin are both lower under LIFO, because inventory and gross profit are lower.

Q: A LIFO company's LIFO reserve fell during a year when its purchase costs rose. What is the most likely explanation, and what does it mean for earnings quality?

Answer: A LIFO liquidation. The company sold more units than it bought, so old, low cost layers flowed into cost of goods sold and inflated gross profit. That boost cannot be repeated, so it lowers the quality of reported earnings.

Q: A company reports LIFO inventory of 500 million dollars and a LIFO reserve of 100 million dollars, and its tax rate is 25 percent. On a FIFO basis, what is inventory, and how much higher is equity?

Answer: Inventory is 600 million dollars, and equity is 75 million dollars higher. You add the whole reserve to inventory, but equity rises only by the reserve times one minus the tax rate. The other 25 million dollars goes to deferred taxes or comes out of cash.

Q: A manufacturer's finished goods grew 30 percent while sales grew 5 percent and raw materials fell. Which interpretation is most likely?

Answer: Demand is weakening. Unsold finished goods are piling up, and falling raw materials suggest the company is cutting production in response, so watch for lower turnover and possible writedowns. The tempting wrong answer is that the company is gearing up for growth, but that pattern shows up as rising raw materials and work in progress.

Q: A U.S. GAAP company switches from FIFO to LIFO. How is the change applied, and how would an IFRS company handle the same switch?

Answer: Under U.S. GAAP, a change to LIFO is applied prospectively, and the inventory carrying amount at the change becomes the first LIFO layer. Most other changes in inventory method are applied retrospectively. An IFRS company could not make this switch at all, because IFRS does not permit LIFO.

## Recap

Beginning inventory plus purchases equals cost of goods sold plus ending inventory, so the cost formula changes only the split, never the total. Under IFRS, inventory is carried at the lower of cost and N R V, and a writedown can be reversed only up to original cost. U.S. GAAP uses the lower of cost and N R V for most methods but the lower of cost or market for LIFO and the retail method, and it never allows reversals. A writedown lowers inventory, profit, equity and the current ratio, and it raises turnover and debt to equity.

When prices rise with stable quantities, LIFO gives higher cost of goods sold, lower profit, lower taxes, lower inventory, higher cash flow and higher turnover. FIFO inventory equals LIFO inventory plus the LIFO reserve, and FIFO cost of goods sold equals LIFO cost of goods sold minus the change in the reserve. A falling LIFO reserve while prices rise signals a LIFO liquidation and a profit boost that will not repeat. Inventory turnover equals cost of goods sold divided by average inventory, and you should read the category breakdown, writedowns and method changes in the notes before you trust any comparison.
