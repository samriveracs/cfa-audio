LEWIS: Reading 32 covers how inventory is measured at the lower of cost and net realizable value, and what a writedown does to the statements and the ratios. It then covers how rising and falling prices pull FIFO, LIFO and weighted average cost apart, the LIFO reserve and LIFO liquidation, and what the inventory notes tell an analyst.

## Module 32.1, Inventory Measurement

HEART: Picture a company that makes winter coats. It spent the summer building parkas for a cold season, and then the winter turns out mild. The racks are full, a batch of half sewn coats is still on the line, and the price stores will pay has dropped. The coats cost what they cost. The question is what they are worth on the balance sheet now.

LEWIS: Under IFRS the answer is the lower of cost and net realizable value. Net realizable value, or N R V, is the expected selling price minus two things still to be spent: the costs to complete the goods and the costs to sell them. For the coat maker, that is the marked down price, minus the sewing still needed on the unfinished coats, minus shipping and sales commissions.

HEART: In plain English, N R V is what the goods will actually put in your pocket after everything you still have to spend on them. If that number falls below what the inventory is carried at, the company writes the inventory down to N R V.

LEWIS: Why not wait until the coats sell and let the loss show up then?

HEART: Two reasons. An asset should not sit on the balance sheet at more than it can bring in. And the loss happened this period, when the warm winter wiped out the demand, so it belongs in this period's profit, not in some later period when the coats finally leave the warehouse.

LEWIS: The loss goes through the income statement, either on its own line or folded into cost of goods sold, which is also called cost of sales. A writedown large enough to matter typically gets its own line. The mechanics usually run through a valuation allowance. That is a contra asset account, which works like accumulated depreciation. The original cost stays on the books, the allowance sits beside it, and the difference is the carrying amount. So you can always see how far below cost the inventory has been marked.

HEART: Now suppose the next winter is brutal and coat prices bounce back. Under IFRS the inventory is written back up, and that reversal is required, not optional. The gain also runs through the income statement, either on its own line or as a reduction in cost of sales. But there is a cap. The reversal can only undo the earlier writedown. The inventory can climb back to its original cost and no higher.

LEWIS: Think of it as a ladder. When value recovers, IFRS puts you back up the rungs you slid down, but never above the rung you started on. So the trap answer is the one that writes the coats up to the new, higher N R V. Lower of cost and N R V means cost is always the ceiling.

HEART: US GAAP splits the rule by cost method. A company on FIFO, weighted average, or anything other than LIFO and the retail method follows the same test as IFRS, the lower of cost or N R V. A company on LIFO or the retail method follows an older test, the lower of cost or market.

LEWIS: Market starts out as replacement cost, what it would cost to buy or make the same item today. But market is boxed in. Its ceiling is N R V. Its floor is N R V minus a normal profit margin. If replacement cost sits above the ceiling, market is N R V. If replacement cost sits below the floor, market is the floor. Otherwise market is simply replacement cost.

HEART: So market is always the middle of three values: replacement cost, N R V, and N R V minus the normal margin. And the gap between the ceiling and the floor is exactly one normal profit margin.

LEWIS: Why put a box around it?

HEART: The ceiling keeps inventory from being carried at more than it can bring in, which is the whole point of the rule. The floor stops a company from writing inventory down so far that it books an unusually fat profit when the goods finally sell. And here is a consequence worth knowing. Market can never sit above N R V, so for the same item at the same cost, the market test produces a writedown at least as large as the N R V test, and sometimes larger.

LEWIS: Under US GAAP, a small decline is usually folded into cost of sales, and a large one shows up as its own loss line. Either way, the written down amount becomes the new cost basis. And US GAAP never allows a reversal, under either test. If the coats recover in value, nothing is written back up. The recovery shows up only as a fatter profit when the coats are eventually sold.

HEART: One more wrinkle. When costs have been rising, a LIFO company is less likely to need a writedown at all. Its inventory carries old, low costs, so there is more room before N R V drops below the carrying amount. A FIFO company holding identical goods carries them at recent, higher costs and hits the line sooner.

LEWIS: There is also an industry exception, under both IFRS and US GAAP. Producers and dealers of commodities, such as agricultural and forest products, mineral ores and precious metals, may carry inventory at N R V even when that is above historical cost. Think of a large wheat farm with silos full of harvested grain. The unrealized gains and losses from price changes go straight to the income statement. If an active market exists, its quoted price sets the value. If not, recent transactions do. Treat this as the exception, not the rule.

HEART: Now the part this module tests hardest, what a writedown does to the statements and the ratios. Assume the writedown runs through cost of sales. Start with the balance sheet. Inventory falls, so current assets and total assets fall. The loss lowers net income, so equity falls too.

LEWIS: Then liquidity. The current ratio falls, because inventory is part of current assets. The quick ratio does not move at all, because it leaves inventory out.

HEART: Activity ratios go the other way. Inventory turnover is cost of sales divided by average inventory. The writedown raises the top and shrinks the bottom, so turnover rises. Days of inventory on hand fall, and so does the cash conversion cycle. Total asset turnover rises too, because total assets are smaller.

LEWIS: And the trap is treating that higher turnover as better management. The company did not get any better at selling coats. It shrank the inventory figure that cost of sales is divided by.

HEART: Solvency. Debt does not change, but assets and equity shrink, so debt to assets and debt to equity both rise. Profitability. Higher cost of sales means lower gross, operating and net margins. And return on assets and return on equity fall for a typical company, because the hit to profit is proportionally bigger than the hit to assets or equity.

LEWIS: Then comes the twist in the following period. The coats now sit at a lower carrying amount, so when they sell, cost of sales is lower than it would have been, and margins look better. Pair that higher profit with the smaller asset and equity base left by the writedown, and return on assets and return on equity rise. A writedown hurts this year and flatters next year.

HEART: Which is why writedowns make ratios hard to compare across periods. And it is why an analyst looks hard at a writedown that seems bigger than the facts justify. Write down too much today and you move profit into tomorrow.

LEWIS: The takeaway. Inventory is carried at the lower of cost and N R V, with US GAAP using the lower of cost or market for LIFO and the retail method. IFRS requires a writedown to reverse when value recovers, but never above original cost, and US GAAP never allows a reversal. And a writedown cuts this year's margins, assets and equity, lifts turnover and leverage ratios, and flatters next year's margins.

## Module 32.2, Inflation Impact on FIFO and LIFO

HEART: Picture a lumber yard. It starts the year with a stack of two by fours in the shed, bought last winter. Then a building boom arrives, and the price the sawmill charges climbs month after month. The yard keeps buying fresh loads all spring and summer, sells a great many boards to builders, and ends the year with a stack still in the shed.

LEWIS: Add the starting stack to everything the yard bought during the year and you have the cost of goods available for sale. That total is a fact. The checks to the sawmill have been written. The accounting question is only how to split it. Part goes to the income statement as cost of goods sold. The rest stays on the balance sheet as ending inventory. Every dollar lands in one bucket or the other, so anything that puts more cost in one bucket puts less in the other.

HEART: The rule for the split is the cost flow assumption. FIFO stands for first in, first out. The cost of the oldest boards goes to cost of sales first, so ending inventory holds the newest and most expensive loads.

LEWIS: LIFO stands for last in, first out. The cost of the newest boards goes to cost of sales first, so ending inventory holds the oldest costs, sometimes from years back.

HEART: Weighted average cost blends everything. Pool all the costs, spread them evenly over all the boards available, and both buckets get the same average cost per board. And for completeness, specific identification tracks the actual cost of each individual item. That suits unique goods, like a single slab of rare walnut, more than a pile of identical two by fours.

LEWIS: Here is the point people miss. The method is about costs, not boards. Picture the stack in the shed. The forklift grabs from the top, so physically the newest boards go out first. The yard can still report on FIFO. The method decides which costs go where, not which pieces of wood leave the building.

HEART: And a scope point. IFRS does not allow LIFO. US GAAP does. So LIFO is essentially a US story, and that matters when you compare companies across the two frameworks.

LEWIS: Now the directions, and they all assume the quantity of inventory is stable or growing. When prices are rising, LIFO sends the newest, highest costs to the income statement. So cost of sales is highest under LIFO, lowest under FIFO, with weighted average in between.

HEART: Gross profit is the mirror image. Highest under FIFO, lowest under LIFO. And that difference flows all the way down: operating profit, income before taxes and net income are all higher under FIFO. Revenue does not change at all. The yard sold the same boards to the same builders at the same prices whatever method it chose.

LEWIS: Ending inventory is the other bucket. Under FIFO it holds the latest, costliest loads, so it is highest. Under LIFO it holds the oldest, cheapest costs, so it is lowest.

HEART: Then taxes and cash. Lower profit before tax under LIFO means a lower income tax bill. Now think about cash. Before taxes, the method changes nothing. The yard paid the sawmill the same amount and collected the same amount from customers. The only cash difference is the tax bill, and LIFO's is smaller. So operating cash flow is higher under LIFO.

LEWIS: That is the paradox to remember. In a period of rising prices, LIFO reports lower profit and produces more cash.

HEART: Why would a company accept lower reported profit?

LEWIS: Because in the United States, a company that chooses LIFO for its tax return must also report on LIFO in its financial statements. That is called the LIFO conformity rule. The lower reported profit is the price of the tax saving.

HEART: So which method tells the truth? Each gets half of it right. FIFO ending inventory is made of the latest purchases, so it sits near current replacement cost. That gives the better balance sheet number. LIFO cost of sales is made of the latest purchases, so it matches today's costs against today's revenue. That gives the better income statement number.

LEWIS: A short way to hold it. FIFO for the balance sheet, LIFO for the income statement.

HEART: When prices are flat, it does not matter. FIFO, LIFO and weighted average give the same cost of sales, the same inventory and the same gross profit. The methods only part ways when prices trend.

LEWIS: And when prices fall, every direction flips, again assuming stable or growing quantities. LIFO now sends the newest, cheapest costs to cost of sales, so LIFO shows the lowest cost of sales, the highest gross profit, the higher tax bill and the highest ending inventory. What does not flip is which method is more current. FIFO inventory still holds the latest costs, and LIFO cost of sales still holds the latest costs.

HEART: So the trap is the reflex that LIFO always means lower profit. It means lower profit only when prices are rising. Before you answer any of these questions, check which way prices are moving.

LEWIS: Now the ratios, assuming rising prices and stable or growing quantities. Start with profitability. Any margin built on cost of sales, so gross margin, operating margin and net profit margin, is higher under FIFO.

HEART: Liquidity. FIFO carries more inventory, so current assets are higher, the current ratio is higher, and working capital is higher. The quick ratio leaves inventory out, so the method does not touch it directly. If anything, LIFO's tax saving leaves a little more cash in the bank.

LEWIS: Activity. Inventory turnover is cost of sales divided by average inventory. Under LIFO the top is bigger, because it holds the newest costs, and the bottom is smaller, because it holds the oldest costs. So LIFO shows higher turnover and fewer days of inventory on hand. Restate a LIFO company to FIFO and its turnover falls and its days on hand rise.

HEART: Same trap as with writedowns. The higher turnover under LIFO is an accounting effect, not faster selling. The boards did not move any quicker.

LEWIS: Solvency. FIFO shows more inventory, so more total assets and more equity. So the solvency ratios, debt to assets and debt to equity, are both lower under FIFO. FIFO makes the company look less leveraged.

HEART: And return on assets and return on equity have no fixed rule here, because under LIFO both the profit on top and the assets or equity underneath are lower.

LEWIS: That brings us to the comparison problem. Say you want to compare a US lumber dealer on LIFO with a European competitor reporting under IFRS, where LIFO is not allowed. You need them on the same footing, and the tool is the LIFO reserve.

HEART: The LIFO reserve is the gap between what inventory would be under FIFO and what it is under LIFO. US companies on LIFO disclose it in the notes. Picture the stack in the shed wearing two sets of price tags, the old LIFO tags and the newer FIFO tags. The reserve is the total difference between them.

LEWIS: In a period of rising prices the reserve is positive, and as long as prices keep rising and quantities hold steady, it keeps growing. It also tells a story about the past. It is the cumulative extra cost that LIFO has pushed through the income statement over the years compared with FIFO, which is the same as the cumulative profit before tax that LIFO has pushed into the future.

HEART: Restating then takes two ideas. For the balance sheet, add the whole reserve to LIFO inventory and you have FIFO inventory. For one year's income statement, what matters is how much the reserve changed during that year. If it grew, LIFO's cost of sales was higher than FIFO's by that growth, so FIFO's profit before tax is higher by the same amount.

LEWIS: That is the trap in restating. The whole reserve goes with the balance sheet. Only the change in the reserve goes with the income statement. Mix them up and you are far off.

HEART: Equity gets one refinement. Restating to FIFO raises equity, but not by the whole reserve, because part of those extra past profits would have gone to taxes. Equity rises by the after tax part, and the rest shows up as taxes.

LEWIS: After the restatement, the ratios move the way you would expect. Inventory and the current ratio rise. Turnover falls. Debt to equity falls. Now the two lumber dealers can be compared fairly.

HEART: Now LIFO liquidation. Under LIFO, inventory builds up in layers. In any year the yard buys more boards than it sells, the extra boards form a new layer at that year's cost, and the old layers sit untouched underneath. A LIFO yard that has been in business for decades can have layers costed at prices from twenty years ago.

LEWIS: A LIFO liquidation happens when inventory quantities fall, because the yard sells more boards than it buys. Then LIFO has to reach down into those old layers, and ancient, low costs flow into cost of sales.

HEART: The effects follow directly. Cost of sales is lower than it would have been, so gross margin, operating margin and profit all jump, and income taxes rise with them. In effect, the company is booking years of past inflation gains as this year's income.

LEWIS: And none of it is sustainable. A layer can only be used up once, and no business can keep selling stock it never replaces. A big enough liquidation can even push LIFO's gross margin above FIFO's in a year of rising prices, which looks backwards until you see what caused it.

HEART: Why do liquidations happen? Some are forced. A strike at the sawmill, or a wildfire season that shuts down a key supplier, can leave the yard unable to restock. Some are voluntary. Management expects orders to fall, and deliberately slims down inventory to fit the market. And some are opportunistic. Management can hold off on purchases near year end to pull cheap old costs into this year's earnings.

LEWIS: How does an analyst spot one? Through the LIFO reserve in the notes. When prices are rising, the reserve should normally hold steady or grow. If it falls, suspect a liquidation, and if the effect is material, strip that boost out before judging the margins.

HEART: The takeaway. When prices rise and quantities hold steady, LIFO means higher cost of sales, lower profit, lower taxes, lower inventory, more cash and higher turnover. FIFO gives the better balance sheet, LIFO the better income statement. Add the LIFO reserve to restate inventory to FIFO, and treat a falling reserve in a period of rising prices as a sign of LIFO liquidation.

## Module 32.3, Presentation and Disclosure

LEWIS: The notes to the financial statements are where an analyst finds out how inventory was measured, and they are similar under IFRS and US GAAP. Seven disclosures matter.

HEART: First, the cost flow method, FIFO, LIFO, weighted average and so on. Second, the total carrying amount of inventory, plus the carrying amount by category where that applies, typically raw materials, work in progress and finished goods. Third, the carrying amount of any inventory reported at fair value less costs to sell, which is where commodity dealers covered by the exception in Module 32.1 show up.

LEWIS: Fourth, the cost of inventory recognized as an expense during the period, which is essentially cost of sales. Fifth, the amount of writedowns during the period, which is what builds the valuation allowance. Sixth, any reversals of writedowns and the circumstances behind them, which is IFRS only, since US GAAP does not allow reversals. Seventh, the carrying amount of inventory pledged as collateral.

HEART: That last one matters to lenders. Picture a credit union lending to a small manufacturer with inventory as collateral. Inventory already pledged to another lender is not available to it. And what protects a lender is what the goods would actually sell for, not what they cost.

LEWIS: The category split depends on the business. Merchandisers, meaning wholesalers and retailers, buy goods ready to sell, so they usually show inventory as one line. The lumber yard is a merchandiser. Manufacturers usually show three.

HEART: Picture a bicycle maker. Raw materials are the steel tubing, tires and chains waiting to be used. Work in progress is the frames moving down the assembly line. Finished goods are the boxed bikes waiting to ship.

LEWIS: Those three lines are signals about future revenue and earnings. If raw materials and work in progress are rising, the company is stocking up and building, which can mean management expects demand to grow. That points to higher revenue and earnings ahead.

HEART: If finished goods are piling up while raw materials and work in progress shrink, the story turns. Bikes are not selling, so the company is cutting back production. That suggests falling demand, and future writedowns become more likely.

LEWIS: Set finished goods against sales as well. If the pile of boxed bikes grows faster than bike sales, demand may be slipping and some models may be going stale. Obsolete inventory eventually gets written down, and that comes out of future earnings.

HEART: And excess inventory costs money even before any writedown. Someone pays to store and insure all those bikes, some places tax inventory, and the cash tied up in them cannot be put to better work.

LEWIS: The notes are not the only source. Analysts also lean on management's discussion and analysis, industry data, trade publications, other sections of the annual report, conversations with management, media coverage, and the accounts of competitors.

HEART: Now the ratios for judging inventory management. Inventory turnover is cost of sales divided by average inventory, so it measures how quickly the company sells through its stock. Days of inventory on hand is the number of days in the year divided by turnover, so it measures how long the typical item sits before it sells.

LEWIS: High turnover, meaning few days on hand, is usually a good sign. But there is such a thing as too fast. A bicycle maker that runs so lean it keeps running out of the popular frame sizes sends its dealers to a competitor. And as Module 32.1 showed, high turnover can also come from writedowns shrinking the inventory balance, and writedowns usually point to weak inventory management.

HEART: So how do you tell efficient from too thin? Set the company's sales growth against its industry's. If its sales lag the pack, the fast turnover probably means empty shelves and orders lost to rivals. If its sales keep pace with the pack or beat it, the fast turnover more likely reflects genuine efficiency.

LEWIS: And at the other end, low turnover, meaning many days on hand, can signal slow moving or obsolete stock, the kind that ends up written down.

HEART: The gross profit margin is the third tool. Read it next to the inventory numbers. If sales are rising but the gross margin is falling, the cost of making or buying the product is climbing faster than prices.

LEWIS: Here is how the pieces work together. Say the bicycle maker's turnover jumps in a single year. Before you call it efficiency, you have three stories to test: better management, fading demand, or trouble getting parts.

HEART: Start with the mix. Fading demand shows up as finished goods growing as a share of inventory while raw materials and work in progress shrink. Then set the valuation allowance against the cost of finished goods. Stock going out of style pushes N R V below cost, so a rising share points to obsolescence, and a falling or steady share argues against it. And look at sales. Bikes flying out the door sit awkwardly with a demand story.

LEWIS: Then bring in the gross margin. If sales are rising but the margin is shrinking, input costs are climbing faster than prices. Put that beside a thin inventory and the likely story is supply, say a tire supplier that cannot keep up, rather than efficiency. Confirm it in management's discussion, industry reports and the accounts of competitors.

HEART: Last, liquidity. The current ratio includes inventory. The quick ratio leaves it out, keeping only cash, marketable securities and receivables. Some analysts prefer the quick ratio because inventory is among the least liquid current assets. Before inventory becomes cash, it has to sit for its days on hand, and then customers take their own days to pay.

LEWIS: So when the current ratio and the quick ratio move in opposite directions, look at inventory first, because it is the main thing one includes and the other leaves out.

HEART: The takeaway. The notes give you the method, the categories, the writedowns and reversals, and anything pledged as collateral. Raw materials rising points to expected growth, finished goods piling up points to weakening demand. And high turnover is good only when sales growth keeps pace with the industry.

## Recall check

LEWIS Q: Under IFRS, a company wrote its coats down to N R V. The next year, their N R V rises above the original cost. Where does the carrying amount end up?

HEART: Answer. At original cost, and no higher. Here is why. IFRS requires a reversal when value recovers, but only of the earlier writedown. The rule is the lower of cost and N R V, so cost is always the ceiling. Under US GAAP there would be no reversal at all, and the written down amount would stay as the cost basis.

HEART Q: Under US GAAP, which test does a LIFO company apply, and what is market?

LEWIS: Answer. The lower of cost or market, where market is replacement cost kept between a ceiling and a floor. Here is why. The ceiling is N R V, so inventory is never carried above what it can bring in. The floor is N R V minus a normal profit margin, so the company cannot write inventory down far enough to manufacture a big profit later. Market is therefore the middle of three values: replacement cost, N R V, and N R V minus the normal margin.

LEWIS Q: A writedown runs through cost of sales. What happens to the current ratio and the quick ratio?

HEART: Answer. The current ratio falls, and the quick ratio does not change. Here is why. Inventory is part of current assets, so writing it down shrinks the top of the current ratio. The quick ratio leaves inventory out entirely, so the writedown never touches it.

HEART Q: The year after a large writedown, a company's gross margin improves. What is the likely reason?

LEWIS: Answer. The written down goods are now flowing through cost of sales at their lower carrying amount. Here is why. The writedown moved part of the cost into last year's loss. When those goods sell, less cost is left to charge, so cost of sales is lower and margins look better. The improvement comes from the earlier writedown, not necessarily from better pricing.

LEWIS Q: Prices are rising and inventory quantities are stable. Which method shows the highest cost of sales and the lowest ending inventory?

HEART: Answer. LIFO on both counts. Here is why. LIFO sends the newest, most expensive costs to the income statement first. That leaves the oldest, cheapest costs sitting in inventory. FIFO does the opposite, and weighted average lands in between.

HEART Q: In a period of rising prices, a LIFO company reports lower profit than a FIFO company but higher operating cash flow. How can both be true?

LEWIS: Answer. LIFO's smaller tax bill explains both. Here is why. Before taxes, the cost method moves no cash, because the company pays its suppliers and collects from customers the same amounts either way. LIFO's higher cost of sales lowers taxable profit, so the tax bill is smaller. The only cash difference is that saving, so operating cash flow is higher under LIFO.

LEWIS Q: Prices are falling and quantities are stable. Which method shows the higher gross profit?

HEART: Answer. LIFO. Here is why. When prices fall, the newest purchases are the cheapest. LIFO sends those newest costs to cost of sales, so cost of sales is lower and gross profit is higher. Each direction from the rising price case simply flips.

HEART Q: In a period of rising prices, a LIFO company shows higher inventory turnover than a FIFO peer. Does that prove it manages inventory better?

LEWIS: Answer. No, it is largely an accounting effect. Here is why. Turnover is cost of sales divided by average inventory. Under LIFO the top holds the newest, higher costs and the bottom holds the oldest, lower costs, so turnover comes out higher even if the two companies sell at exactly the same pace. Restate the LIFO company to FIFO before comparing.

LEWIS Q: To put a LIFO company's inventory on a FIFO basis, what do you add, and what do you use to restate one year's cost of sales?

HEART: Answer. Add the whole LIFO reserve to inventory, and apply only the change in the reserve to cost of sales. Here is why. The reserve is the total gap, built up over many years, between FIFO and LIFO inventory, so the balance sheet needs all of it. One year's income statement only reflects how much of that gap was added during the year. When the reserve grows, LIFO's cost of sales was higher than FIFO's by that growth.

HEART Q: A LIFO company's reserve fell during a year when its purchase costs rose. What most likely happened, and what should an analyst do about it?

LEWIS: Answer. A LIFO liquidation, and the analyst should strip the boost out of margins if it is material. Here is why. With rising prices and steady quantities, the reserve should hold steady or grow. It falls when inventory quantities drop and old, low cost layers flow into cost of sales. That lifts margins and taxes for one period only, because an old layer can only be used once.

LEWIS Q: A manufacturer's finished goods are piling up while its raw materials and work in progress are shrinking. What is the most likely reading?

HEART: Answer. Demand is weakening, and writedowns may follow. Here is why. Unsold goods are stacking up at the end of the line, and the company is buying and building less in response. A company expecting growth would show the opposite, with raw materials and work in progress rising.

HEART Q: A company has very high inventory turnover. How do you tell whether that is efficiency or too little inventory?

LEWIS: Answer. Compare its sales growth with the industry's. Here is why. A company that runs too lean misses orders, so its sales tend to grow more slowly than its competitors'. If sales are growing at or above the industry pace, the high turnover more likely reflects genuine efficiency. Also check whether recent writedowns shrank the inventory balance, since that raises turnover too.

## Key takeaways

HEART: First, a few memory hooks.

LEWIS: For measurement: lower of cost and N R V, where N R V is what the goods will put in your pocket. When value recovers, IFRS puts you back up the ladder, but never above the rung you started on. US GAAP takes the ladder away.

HEART: For the US GAAP market test, used only for LIFO and the retail method: market is the middle value of three. Replacement cost, N R V as the ceiling, and N R V minus a normal margin as the floor.

LEWIS: For writedowns: hurts this year, flatters next year. The current ratio falls, the quick ratio ignores it, and turnover rises without anyone selling a single extra coat.

HEART: For the cost methods: the checks are already written. The method only decides which bucket each dollar lands in, cost of sales or ending inventory. It moves costs, not boards.

LEWIS: For rising prices with steady quantities: LIFO sends the latest, priciest costs to the income statement. So LIFO means higher cost of sales, lower profit, lower taxes, lower inventory, more cash and higher turnover. When prices fall, flip every one of those.

HEART: For which number to trust: FIFO for the balance sheet, LIFO for the income statement.

LEWIS: For the LIFO reserve: the reserve is the gap between the two sets of price tags. Whole reserve for the balance sheet, change in the reserve for the income statement. And a falling reserve while prices rise is a smoke alarm for liquidation.

HEART: For liquidation: selling the old layers is a one time gift that raises profit and taxes once.

LEWIS: For the notes: inputs rising signals expected growth, finished goods piling up signals fading demand. And fast turnover is good only if sales keep pace with the industry.

HEART: Now, how you would say the big ideas to a senior leader.

LEWIS: On cost methods: when our costs are rising, the method we choose decides how much of the rise hits this year's profit and how much sits in the value of the warehouse. The jargon is cost flow assumptions, FIFO, LIFO and weighted average cost.

HEART: On LIFO and cash: in a period of rising costs, LIFO shows lower profit but a smaller tax bill, so we actually keep more cash. The jargon is the LIFO conformity rule and its effect on operating cash flow.

LEWIS: On comparing competitors: before we compare ourselves with a company on a different method, we add back the gap they disclose, so we compare like with like. The jargon is the LIFO reserve and restating LIFO to FIFO.

HEART: On a sudden margin jump: part of this year's margin came from selling old stock carried at very old costs, and that will not repeat. The jargon is LIFO liquidation and earnings sustainability.

LEWIS: On writedowns: when stock is worth less than we paid, we take the hit now, and part of next year's margin bounce is just the echo. The jargon is the lower of cost and net realizable value, an inventory writedown and a valuation allowance.

HEART: And on inventory health: watch which inventory is piling up and how fast it turns, against how fast sales are growing. The jargon is raw materials, work in progress, finished goods, inventory turnover and days of inventory on hand.
