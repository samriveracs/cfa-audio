## The big picture

This episode is about resources and promises that stretch across many years, and about a question at the heart of financial statement analysis. Does a cost hit the income statement now, or does it sit on the balance sheet and flow through over time? Reading 33 deals with long lived assets: intangibles first, then impairment and derecognition, meaning how assets lose value or leave the books, then the disclosures an analyst uses to judge how old the asset base is.

Reading 34 turns to the other side of the balance sheet. Leases now put most rented assets and the matching obligations on the balance sheet, but IFRS and U.S. GAAP split the expense and the cash flows differently. Deferred compensation covers pensions and share based pay, two promises to employees that are earned now and paid later.

Expect three kinds of exam questions. First, classification: capitalize or expense, finance lease or operating lease, profit or other comprehensive income. Second, direction of effect on net income, operating cash flow and ratios. Third, small calculations, such as an impairment loss, the age of equipment, or the first year of a lease. IFRS is the default throughout, and U.S. GAAP differences are flagged where they are testable.

## Module 33.1, Intangible Long-Lived Assets

An intangible asset is an identifiable, nonmonetary asset without physical substance. Identifiable means it can be separated from the business and sold or licensed, or it arises from a contractual or legal right. Patents, copyrights, trademarks, licenses and customer lists all qualify. Goodwill does not, because it cannot be separated from the business.

How an intangible reaches the balance sheet depends on how the company got it, and there are three routes. First, purchased on its own. A patent bought from an inventor is recorded at cost, meaning the price paid plus any costs to get it ready for use, just like a machine.

Second, acquired in a business combination. The buyer allocates the purchase price to every identifiable asset and liability of the target at fair value, including intangibles the target never recorded, such as a brand it built itself. Whatever is left of the price is goodwill. Goodwill is not amortized, and it is tested for impairment at least once a year under both IFRS and U.S. GAAP.

Third, developed internally. The default is to expense, because the link between today's spending and a future benefit is uncertain, and internally generated brands and customer lists are never capitalized. Under IFRS, research costs are expensed as incurred. Development costs are capitalized once the company can show a set of conditions, such as technical feasibility, the intent and ability to complete and use or sell the asset, probable future benefits, and reliable measurement of the costs.

Under U.S. GAAP, research and development is generally expensed in full. The main exception is software. Software to be sold is expensed until technological feasibility is reached and capitalized after that. Software for internal use is capitalized once the project reaches the application development stage.

Here is the trap. Two companies with identical development spending, one under IFRS and one under U.S. GAAP, do not report the same results.

Say the spending is 100 dollars, it qualifies under IFRS, and it is amortized straight line over 5 years with a full year in year one. The IFRS company's year one expense is 20 dollars, leaving an asset of 80, while the U.S. GAAP company expenses all 100, so the IFRS company's pretax profit is 80 dollars higher. Its assets and equity are higher too. Its operating cash flow is 100 dollars higher and its investing cash flow 100 dollars lower, because capitalized spending is an investing outflow. Total cash is identical, and only the labels and timing differ.

A finite life intangible, like a patent with 10 years left, is amortized over its useful life, usually straight line. An indefinite life intangible, like a trademark the company expects to renew indefinitely, is not amortized but is tested for impairment at least annually. IFRS also permits a revaluation model for intangibles, but only where an active market exists, which is rare. U.S. GAAP requires the cost model.

So remember the rule. Research is always expensed. Development is capitalized under IFRS once the conditions are met, while U.S. GAAP expenses both, except for certain software costs.

## Module 33.2, Impairment and Derecognition

Depreciation spreads cost on a schedule. Impairment is an unscheduled write down when an asset can no longer justify its carrying amount, meaning cost less accumulated depreciation and earlier impairments. The principle is that an asset should not be carried at more than the company can recover from it.

Under IFRS, the company looks for signs of impairment at every reporting date, such as physical damage or a worse business outlook. The asset is impaired when its carrying amount exceeds its recoverable amount. The recoverable amount is the higher of two figures: fair value less costs to sell, also called costs of disposal, and value in use, which is the present value of the asset's expected cash flows. The impairment loss equals the carrying amount minus the recoverable amount. Why the higher of the two? A sensible owner either sells the asset or keeps using it, whichever is worth more, so it is impaired only if both options fall short.

U.S. GAAP uses two steps for assets held for use, and it tests only when events suggest the carrying amount may not be recoverable. Step one is the recoverability test, which compares the carrying amount with the undiscounted future cash flows. If those cash flows are at least as large, there is no impairment. If not, step two measures the loss as the carrying amount minus fair value.

Here is a worked example. A machine has a carrying amount of 1,000 dollars. Its fair value is 750 and selling costs are 50, so fair value less costs to sell is 700. Its value in use is 800, and the same cash flows undiscounted total 1,050. Under IFRS, the recoverable amount is the higher of 700 and 800, so the loss is 1,000 minus 800, or 200 dollars. Under U.S. GAAP, undiscounted cash flows of 1,050 exceed the carrying amount, so the machine passes step one and no loss is recorded.

Now suppose the undiscounted cash flows were only 950. The machine fails step one, and the U.S. GAAP loss is 1,000 minus the fair value of 750, or 250 dollars. Here is the trap. Undiscounted cash flows are only a trigger, and they never measure the loss.

Reversals differ too. Under IFRS, if the recoverable amount later rises, impairments on assets other than goodwill can be reversed, but only up to the carrying amount the asset would have had, after normal depreciation, if it had never been impaired. Goodwill impairments are never reversed. Under U.S. GAAP, impairments of assets held for use can never be reversed.

Assets held for sale follow their own rule. Once management commits to a sale that is highly probable, the asset is reclassified as held for sale, stops being depreciated, and is carried at the lower of its carrying amount and fair value less costs to sell. If that value later recovers, both IFRS and U.S. GAAP allow a reversal, but only up to the losses already recognized.

In the year of an impairment, assets, net income and equity all fall, but cash does not, so the loss is added back in an indirect method cash flow statement. Debt to equity rises because equity shrinks. In later years, depreciation is lower and the asset base is smaller, so profit, ROA and asset turnover look better. Management has discretion over the timing and size of write downs, and a large impairment can also signal that past depreciation was too slow.

Derecognition means the asset leaves the balance sheet, by sale, abandonment or exchange. In a sale, the gain or loss equals the sale proceeds minus the carrying amount. If the asset is abandoned, the loss equals the full carrying amount. In an exchange, the old asset comes off at its carrying amount, the new asset generally goes on at fair value, and the difference is a gain or loss.

Equipment cost 500 dollars and has accumulated depreciation of 300, so its carrying amount is 200. It sells for 260, so the gain is 60. Here is the trap. The cash inflow is the full 260, reported in investing activities. The 60 dollar gain is not cash, so the indirect method subtracts it from net income within operating cash flow.

Remember the core rule. Under IFRS, the impairment loss equals the carrying amount minus the recoverable amount, which is the higher of fair value less costs to sell and value in use. Under U.S. GAAP, undiscounted cash flows decide whether there is a loss, and fair value decides how big it is.

## Module 33.3, Long-Term Asset Disclosures

The notes tell you how a company measures and depreciates its long lived assets. For property, plant and equipment, which you will hear as PPE, companies disclose the measurement basis, depreciation methods, useful lives or rates, gross carrying amounts, accumulated depreciation, and a reconciliation of opening and closing balances. For intangibles, they disclose whether lives are finite or indefinite and how the assets are amortized. For impairments, they disclose the amounts, where the losses appear, and the events behind them.

The most testable use of these notes is estimating the age of the asset base. Three ratios do it, and all three assume straight line depreciation with no salvage value.

First, estimated total useful life equals gross PPE, meaning historical cost before accumulated depreciation, divided by annual depreciation expense. Second, average age equals accumulated depreciation divided by annual depreciation expense. Third, estimated remaining useful life equals net PPE, meaning gross PPE minus accumulated depreciation, divided by annual depreciation expense. Average age plus remaining life equals total useful life.

Here is a worked example. A company reports gross PPE of 1,000 dollars excluding land, accumulated depreciation of 400, and depreciation expense of 100 a year. Total useful life is 1,000 divided by 100, or 10 years. Average age is 400 divided by 100, or 4 years. Remaining life is 600 divided by 100, or 6 years. Four years plus six years gives ten, which is your check.

Why does this matter? An old asset base, meaning a high average age relative to total life, suggests heavy capital spending is coming, and it can flatter ROA because the assets are heavily depreciated. Comparing capital spending with depreciation tells a similar story, and spending well below depreciation for years means the asset base is aging or shrinking.

Now the traps. Land is not depreciated, so remove it from gross PPE before dividing, or the total and remaining lives will come out too long. The estimates are also rough. Acquisitions, disposals, impairments and a mix of asset types with very different lives all distort them. Use them to compare companies in the same industry, not as precise figures.

So remember. Average age equals accumulated depreciation divided by depreciation expense, and remaining life equals net PPE divided by depreciation expense.

## Module 34.1, Leases

A lease is a contract that gives the lessee the right to use an identified asset for a period of time in exchange for payments. The lessee controls the use of the asset, meaning it gets substantially all the economic benefits and directs how the asset is used. The owner is the lessor.

Why lease rather than buy? Leasing needs less cash up front and can be cheaper than borrowing to buy. It shifts the risk of obsolescence, and of the asset's value at the end of the term, to the lessor, and its terms can be flexible. Leasing once kept debt off the balance sheet, but current standards have largely ended that.

Start with the lessee under IFRS, because IFRS uses a single model. At the start of almost every lease, the lessee records a lease liability equal to the present value of the lease payments. The discount rate is the rate implicit in the lease, or the lessee's incremental borrowing rate if the implicit rate cannot be determined. The lessee also records a right of use asset, usually equal to the lease liability, adjusted for items like payments made at or before the start and initial direct costs.

After that, two things happen each period. The right of use asset is depreciated, usually straight line. The lease liability accrues interest at the discount rate, and each payment covers that interest and reduces the principal. There are two exemptions. Leases of 12 months or less, and leases of low value assets such as laptops, may be expensed straight line with no asset or liability recorded.

U.S. GAAP keeps two types of lessee lease. Both put a right of use asset and a lease liability on the balance sheet at the present value of the payments, and the difference shows up in the income statement and the cash flow statement.

A lease is a finance lease if it meets any one of five tests. First, ownership transfers to the lessee by the end of the term. Second, the lessee has a purchase option it is reasonably certain to exercise. Third, the lease term covers the major part of the asset's remaining economic life. Fourth, the present value of the payments, including any residual value the lessee guarantees, equals or exceeds substantially all of the asset's fair value. Fifth, the asset is so specialized that the lessor has no alternative use for it.

If none of the five is met, it is an operating lease. Notice the theme: each test asks whether the lessee has effectively bought the asset. A U.S. GAAP finance lease works just like the IFRS model, with amortization of the asset plus interest on the liability. An operating lease instead shows a single straight line lease expense within operating expenses. Interest still accrues on the liability, but the asset's amortization is set each period so that the total expense stays level.

Cash flows follow the same split. For a U.S. GAAP finance lease, the interest part of each payment is an operating outflow and the principal part is a financing outflow. For a U.S. GAAP operating lease, the entire payment is an operating outflow. Under IFRS, principal is financing, and interest is operating or financing, depending on the company's policy.

Now a worked example with the calculator, since measuring the liability is a time value of money problem. A lessee signs a 3 year lease with payments of 1,000 dollars at the end of each year, and the discount rate is 10 percent.

On the B A two plus, press second, then F V, to clear the T V M worksheet. Press second, then I Y, to check that P Y is 1, and if it is not, type 1 and press enter, which also sets C Y to 1. Press second, then C P T, to quit. Make sure no B G N indicator shows, which means end mode.

Now enter 3 and press N. Enter 10 and press I Y. Enter 1,000, press the plus minus key to make it negative because the lessee pays out, and press P M T. Enter 0 and press F V. Press C P T, then P V, and the display shows 2,486.85, a positive number, which is the lease liability.

If the payments were due at the start of each year, switch to begin mode. Press second, then P M T, then second and enter to set it, then second and C P T to quit. Press C P T, then P V again, and the same inputs give about 2,736 dollars, higher because every payment arrives one year sooner. Switch back to end mode when you are done.

Back to payments at the end of each year. Year one interest is 10 percent of about 2,487, or about 249 dollars. Straight line depreciation of the right of use asset is 2,487 divided by 3, or about 829. So under IFRS or a U.S. GAAP finance lease, year one expense is about 1,078 dollars, against a flat 1,000 under a U.S. GAAP operating lease. Both total exactly 3,000 dollars over the lease, but the finance lease pattern is front loaded, because interest is highest when the liability is largest.

Of the first 1,000 dollar payment, about 249 is interest and about 751 is principal, so the liability ends the year at about 1,736 dollars. Under a U.S. GAAP finance lease, only the 249 reduces operating cash flow, while an operating lease puts the full 1,000 there.

Here are the traps. Compared with a U.S. GAAP operating lease, a finance lease, like a lease under the IFRS single model, gives higher operating cash flow, higher EBIT and higher EBITDA. That is because interest sits below operating income, and depreciation is added back in EBITDA. Its net income, though, is lower in the early years and higher in the later years. The tempting wrong answer is that the operating lease looks better everywhere because its early expense is lower. In fact it reports lower operating cash flow and operating income every year.

Now the lessor. Under both frameworks, the lessor classifies each lease using tests like the five you just heard. In a finance lease, the lessor removes the asset and records a lease receivable, equal to the present value of the lease payments plus any residual value it expects to recover, also discounted, and then earns interest income on that receivable. If the asset's fair value exceeds its carrying amount, as when a manufacturer leases its own product, the lessor also books a selling profit at the start. In an operating lease, the lessor keeps the asset on its books, keeps depreciating it, and recognizes lease revenue, usually straight line.

Remember the key rule. Under IFRS, nearly every lessee lease creates a right of use asset and a lease liability equal to the present value of the payments, followed by depreciation and interest. U.S. GAAP gives the same balance sheet, but an operating lease shows one straight line expense, with the whole payment in operating cash flow.

## Module 34.2, Deferred Compensation and Disclosures

Deferred compensation means employees earn something now that is paid later. The accounting principle is matching, so the cost is recognized while the employee works, not when the cash is paid. Two kinds matter here: post employment benefits such as pensions, and share based compensation.

Start with the two types of pension plan. In a defined contribution plan, the employer promises to pay an agreed amount into the employee's account each period, and that is the whole promise. The employee bears the investment risk. Pension expense equals the contribution owed for the period, and a liability appears only for contributions not yet paid.

In a defined benefit plan, the employer promises a future benefit, usually based on years of service and final salary. Now the employer bears the investment risk, and the risk that employees live longer or earn more than expected. The obligation is the present value of the benefits employees have earned so far. IFRS calls it the defined benefit obligation, and U.S. GAAP calls it the projected benefit obligation. The employer usually sets aside plan assets in a trust to pay it.

The balance sheet shows the funded status. Funded status equals the fair value of plan assets minus the present value of the obligation. An underfunded plan shows a net pension liability, and an overfunded plan shows a net pension asset.

Under IFRS, the periodic pension cost has three parts. First, service cost, which is the present value of benefits earned this year plus any past service cost from plan changes, and it goes to profit or loss. Second, net interest, which equals the discount rate times the net pension liability or asset at the start of the year, and it also goes to profit or loss. Third, remeasurements, which go to other comprehensive income and are never reclassified to profit. They include actuarial gains and losses, and the difference between the actual return on plan assets and the return implied by the discount rate.

Here is a worked example. Plan assets are 900 dollars and the obligation is 1,000, so the net pension liability is 100. With service cost of 40 and a discount rate of 5 percent, IFRS pension expense is 40 plus 5 percent of 100, or 45 dollars. Now suppose plan assets actually earned 70, while the discount rate implied 5 percent of 900, or 45. The extra 25 dollars is a remeasurement gain in other comprehensive income. Here is the trap: a great year in the markets does not lower IFRS pension expense in profit or loss at all.

U.S. GAAP builds the cost differently. Profit or loss includes service cost, interest cost on the full obligation, and an expected return on plan assets, which lowers the cost. Past service cost and actuarial gains and losses generally go to other comprehensive income first and are then amortized into profit or loss over time. Because the expected return is an assumption, a higher expected return lowers reported pension expense under U.S. GAAP. IFRS has no such lever.

Three assumptions drive the obligation. A higher discount rate lowers it, while higher assumed salary growth and longer life expectancy raise it. A defined benefit obligation behaves like a long duration liability, so its value swings with interest rates the way long dated funding does in a bank's interest rate risk work. Other post employment benefits, such as retiree health care, follow the same logic but are usually unfunded. On the cash flow statement, what matters is the cash the employer contributes, not the expense.

Now share based compensation. Paying employees in shares or options aligns their interests with shareholders, conserves cash and, through vesting, encourages them to stay. There are drawbacks. An individual employee has limited influence on the share price, options can encourage excessive risk taking, and existing shareholders are diluted when new shares are issued.

For awards settled in shares, measure the award at its fair value on the grant date, and expense that amount over the service period, which is usually the vesting period. For stock grants, including restricted stock and performance shares, fair value is the share price on the grant date. For options, fair value comes from a model such as Black Scholes or a binomial model, using the share price, exercise price, expected volatility, expected life, risk free rate and dividend yield. Higher volatility, a longer expected life or a higher risk free rate raise the option value and the expense, and a higher dividend yield lowers them.

Here is a worked example. A company grants 1,000 options with a grant date fair value of 6 dollars each, vesting over 3 years. Total compensation cost is 6,000 dollars, expensed at 2,000 dollars a year. If the share price doubles next year, the expense does not change, because awards settled in shares are fixed at grant date fair value. The expense is noncash, so it is added back in an indirect method cash flow statement. And the entry that records it also credits paid in capital, so total equity does not change because of the expense itself.

Stock appreciation rights pay the employee the increase in the share price, often in cash, so there is no dilution and no cash outlay at grant. Phantom shares work the same way but track a hypothetical share, which suits private companies. When these awards are settled in cash, they are liabilities, and their fair value is remeasured each period until settlement, which makes the expense more volatile.

Finally, disclosures. Pension notes give the key assumptions, such as the discount rate, salary growth and, under U.S. GAAP, the expected return on plan assets. Share based pay notes give the plan types, the valuation model and its assumptions, and the expense for the period. Notes on long term debt and leases give maturity schedules, interest rates and covenants. A company using a higher discount rate or a lower volatility assumption than its peers reports a smaller obligation or expense, so adjust before comparing.

Remember the core rule. Under IFRS, service cost and net interest go to profit or loss, remeasurements go to other comprehensive income, and share based awards are expensed at grant date fair value over the vesting period.

## Recall check

Q: Which is most likely to be capitalized: research costs under IFRS, qualifying development costs under IFRS, or drug development costs under U.S. GAAP?

Answer: Qualifying development costs under IFRS. IFRS always expenses research and capitalizes development only once conditions such as technical feasibility and reliable measurement are met. U.S. GAAP expenses research and development as incurred, with software as the main exception.

Q: Under IFRS, a plant has a carrying amount of 500 dollars, fair value less costs to sell of 380 and value in use of 420. What is the impairment loss?

Answer: 80 dollars. The recoverable amount is the higher of 380 and 420, which is 420. The loss is the carrying amount of 500 minus 420.

Q: Under U.S. GAAP, an asset held for use has a carrying amount above its fair value but below its undiscounted future cash flows. Is an impairment loss most likely recognized?

Answer: No. The asset passes the recoverability test because its undiscounted cash flows cover the carrying amount, so no loss is recorded even though fair value is lower. Fair value only measures the loss after the asset fails that first step.

Q: A company reports gross PPE of 600 dollars excluding land, accumulated depreciation of 240 and annual depreciation of 60. What are the average age and the remaining useful life?

Answer: Average age is 240 divided by 60, or 4 years. Remaining life is net PPE of 360 divided by 60, or 6 years. Together they give a total useful life of 10 years, which matches 600 divided by 60.

Q: Under U.S. GAAP, which lessee reports higher cash flow from operations: one with a finance lease or one with an otherwise identical operating lease?

Answer: The lessee with the finance lease. Only the interest part of each payment is an operating outflow, and the principal part is a financing outflow. Under an operating lease, the entire payment is an operating outflow.

Q: Under IFRS, a defined benefit plan's assets earn 12 percent in a year when the discount rate is 5 percent. Where does the extra return appear?

Answer: In other comprehensive income, as a remeasurement gain, and it is never reclassified to profit. Profit or loss includes only service cost and net interest at the discount rate, so the strong return does not reduce reported pension expense.

## Recap

Research is always expensed, IFRS capitalizes development once its conditions are met, and U.S. GAAP expenses both except certain software costs. Under IFRS, an impairment loss equals the carrying amount minus the recoverable amount, which is the higher of fair value less costs to sell and value in use, and reversals are allowed except for goodwill. Under U.S. GAAP, undiscounted cash flows decide whether an asset held for use is impaired, fair value measures the loss, and the loss is never reversed. When an asset is sold, the gain equals proceeds minus carrying amount, but the full proceeds are the investing inflow.

Average age equals accumulated depreciation divided by depreciation expense, and remaining life equals net PPE divided by depreciation expense. Under IFRS, lessees put nearly every lease on the balance sheet and report depreciation plus interest, while U.S. GAAP operating leases show one straight line expense and put the whole payment in operating cash flow. Under IFRS pension accounting, service cost and net interest go to profit or loss, and remeasurements go to other comprehensive income. Share awards are expensed at grant date fair value over the vesting period, while cash settled awards are remeasured every period.
