LEWIS: This episode opens Equity with what the financial system does, how assets and markets are sorted, and what each kind of middleman does for whom. Then come long and short positions, buying on margin and margin calls, and finally orders, new issues, trading venues, and what good markets and good regulation look like.

## Module 39.1, Markets, Assets, and Intermediaries

HEART: Picture four neighbors. A retired teacher has savings she will not touch for twenty years. A young couple wants a house today. A baker wants a second shop and would rather take on partners than debt. And a coffee roaster fears bean prices will jump before winter. The financial system connects people like these and moves money and risk across time, and companies, governments and charities rely on it too.

LEWIS: It has three main functions. The first is letting people get things done: save, borrow, raise equity capital, manage risk, swap one asset for another today or later, and act on information.

HEART: The teacher is saving, and she wants a return that pays her for risk and for giving up the use of her money. Companies save too, setting aside part of today's sales for future spending. The couple is borrowing, as companies and governments do, and a lender protects itself with collateral, an equity stake, or a hard look at the borrower's credit.

LEWIS: The baker is raising equity, selling partners a share of future profits, with investment banks selling the shares, analysts valuing them, and regulators and accountants getting the information out.

HEART: The roaster is managing risk by locking in today a price for beans delivered next winter. Someone who takes a position to reduce a risk they already carry is a hedger, and hedging lets businesses take on deals they would otherwise avoid.

LEWIS: Exchanging assets is the plain swap. A Canadian ski resort full of American tourists collects American dollars but pays its staff in Canadian dollars, so it trades one for the other. And acting on information is what an information motivated trader does. She trades because she thinks the market has a price wrong, and when she is right, she expects a return above the normal reward for the risk.

HEART: Here is the trap. A hedger and a speculator can place the identical order. The roaster buying bean futures to protect its costs is hedging, not trading on information. Ask why someone trades, not what they bought.

LEWIS: The second function is setting returns. An interest rate is the price of using money. Low rates encourage borrowing and spending now and discourage saving, and high rates do the reverse. The equilibrium interest rate is the level at which total planned borrowing by households, businesses and governments just equals total planned lending, and each kind of loan finds its own equilibrium, set by its risk, liquidity and maturity.

HEART: The third function is allocating capital. Savings are limited, so investors weigh risk and return, and when they are well informed and markets work, money flows to the most valuable uses, like the company with the better battery. The system does all this best when markets are liquid, trading is cheap, information is easy to get, and contracts are enforced.

LEWIS: Next, sorting assets and markets. Financial assets are claims: securities like stocks and bonds, derivative contracts, and currencies. Real assets are physical things: real estate, equipment, commodities and other tangible property. A share of a trucking company is a financial asset. The truck is a real asset.

HEART: Securities split into debt, a promise to repay borrowed money, and equity, which is ownership. They also split into public and private. Public securities trade on exchanges or with dealers and fall under securities regulation. Private securities do not trade in public markets, and they are often hard to sell and largely outside that regulation.

LEWIS: Derivatives are contracts whose value comes from something else. If that something is financial, like a bond or a stock index, it is a financial derivative. If it is a physical good, like copper or natural gas, it is a physical derivative.

HEART: Then timing. A spot market is for immediate delivery, like buying coffee at the counter. Forwards, futures and options are contracts for delivery later, more like ordering a phone that ships next month at a price agreed today.

LEWIS: Then primary versus secondary. The primary market is where new securities are first sold, and the issuer collects the money. The secondary market is every later trade between investors. Buying a concert ticket at the box office pays the band. Buying one from another fan pays the band nothing.

HEART: Then money markets, for debt maturing in one year or less, versus capital markets, for longer term debt and for equity, which never matures. The trap is that the line is maturity, not the issuer. Commercial paper from a company and a bill from the government are both money market instruments, and stock always sits in the capital market.

LEWIS: Last, traditional markets for stocks and bonds versus alternative markets for hedge funds, commodities, real estate, collectibles, gemstones, leases and equipment. Because alternatives are hard to value, slow to sell and need real homework, buyers often insist on a discount.

HEART: The things that trade come in five types: securities, currencies, contracts, commodities and real assets.

LEWIS: Securities come mostly from corporations and governments, and a first sale to the public is called an issue. Fixed income first. By maturity, short term usually means under one or two years, long term beyond five to ten years, and intermediate in between. Loosely, bonds are long term and notes intermediate. At the short end, companies issue commercial paper, governments issue bills, and banks issue certificates of deposit.

HEART: A repurchase agreement, or repo, works like a pawn shop deal with a guaranteed buyback. The borrower hands over a high quality security for cash and is both entitled and obliged to buy it back later for a little more. The price gap is the interest, and the term can be as short as one day. Convertible debt is a bond the investor can swap for a set number of the issuer's shares.

LEWIS: Equity comes in three forms. Common stock is a residual claim. If a restaurant chain fails, lenders are paid first, then preferred holders, and common owners get what is left, often nothing. The same order holds for regular payouts. Preferred stock pays a set dividend that rarely changes, and no common dividend can be paid until it is. Warrants work like call options issued by the company, letting the holder buy shares at a fixed exercise price until they expire.

HEART: Pooled investment vehicles combine many investors' money. Your slice may be called shares, units, depository receipts or limited partnership interests. With an open end mutual fund, you buy from and sell back to the fund itself. A closed end fund has a fixed number of shares that investors trade with each other in the secondary market. Think of a bakery that bakes as many loaves as people order, versus a fixed block of season tickets that fans resell, sometimes above and sometimes below what they are really worth.

LEWIS: Exchange traded funds and exchange traded notes trade like closed end funds, but conversion features keep the price near the value behind each share. Big players can swap fund shares for the underlying securities, and a note, which is really the issuer's debt, can be cashed in with the issuer at about the value of the index it tracks. These are sometimes called depositories, with shares called depository receipts.

HEART: Asset backed securities are claims on a pool of loans like mortgages, car loans or credit card balances, with the payments passed through and the claims cut into tranches of different risk. Hedge funds are limited partnerships. Investors are the limited partners, the manager is the general partner, and they are usually open only to wealthy, knowledgeable investors. They often employ leverage, and managers are paid on both assets managed and results.

LEWIS: Currencies are issued by central banks. Reserve currencies are held by governments and central banks worldwide, with the dollar and the euro on top, followed by the British pound, the Japanese yen and the Swiss franc. Most currencies trade spot, and some also trade in forward and futures markets.

HEART: Contracts require someone to act later. A forward is a private agreement to buy or sell an asset later at a price fixed today, like a jeweler agreeing in March to buy silver in June at a set price. A forward is a custom deal between two parties, so it does not trade on an exchange and is hard to pass on to anyone else. A futures contract does the same job but is standardized in amount, asset and delivery date and trades on an exchange, which makes it liquid.

LEWIS: In a swap, the two sides trade payments over time. An interest rate swap trades floating rate payments for fixed ones. A currency swap is a loan in one currency for a loan in another. An equity swap trades the return on a stock index for interest on a debt amount.

HEART: An option gives its buyer a right without an obligation. A call is the right to buy, and a put is the right to sell, at a set exercise price. The seller, or writer, collects a premium and takes on the obligation. Standard options on currencies, stocks, indexes, futures and precious metals trade on exchanges, and dealers sell custom ones over the counter.

LEWIS: Insurance contracts pay cash if an event happens, like a death or a car crash. Some can be sold to others, and payouts are often tax advantaged. A credit default swap pays if a bond issuer defaults. Bondholders buy it to hedge, businesses exposed to the issuer buy it for protection, and speculators trade it to bet the issuer's troubles will turn out worse, or better, than expected.

HEART: Commodities include metals, farm products, energy and carbon credits, and they trade in spot, forward and futures markets. Futures and forwards let hedgers and speculators take part without handling the goods. A trader betting on cocoa does not want a warehouse full of beans.

LEWIS: Real assets like buildings and machinery are increasingly owned by institutional investors. Owning directly can bring income, tax advantages and diversification, but it takes heavy management, each asset is different, or heterogeneous, so every purchase needs homework, and specialized assets have few buyers, so they are illiquid. That is why many investors own them indirectly, through a real estate investment trust, a REIT, a master limited partnership, or shares of companies rich in real assets, all easier to sell.

HEART: Now the intermediaries, the firms that stand between buyers and sellers. For each one, ask what it does and for whom.

LEWIS: Brokers work for the client, finding someone to take the other side of a trade cheaply, like a real estate agent who finds a buyer but never buys your house. A block broker handles very large trades, keeping the client's plans quiet so other traders cannot push the price the wrong way before a huge order is done. Investment banks help companies sell stock and bonds, and advise on mergers, acquisitions and raising capital.

HEART: Exchanges give traders a place to meet, and many also match orders electronically, which is really a broker's job. They regulate too. They set rules for members and require listed companies to disclose financial information on time and give shareholders a real voice, a power that comes from member agreements or from governments. Alternative trading systems do the trading without the regulating. They are also called electronic communication networks or multilateral trading facilities, and the ones that hide their clients' orders are dark pools.

LEWIS: Brokers, exchanges and alternative trading systems connect buyers and sellers of the same security at the same place and time. Dealers connect them across time. A dealer buys from you today, holds the security in its own inventory, and sells to someone else next week, like a used car lot that buys your car now and sells it later at a higher sticker. The dealer buys at the bid, sells at the ask, and earns the bid ask spread for supplying liquidity.

HEART: A firm that is both is a broker dealer, and that is a conflict of interest. Your agent should win you the best price, but the same firm trading for itself profits when your price is worse. So traders often restrict how a broker dealer may fill their orders. And primary dealers are those that trade with a central bank when it buys or sells government securities to change the money supply.

LEWIS: Securitizers serve borrowers and investors at once. They pool loans, such as mortgages, car loans, credit card receivables, bank loans or equipment leases, and sell interests in the pool, passing the returns through after fees. A big mixed pool pays out more predictably than any single loan, its slices are easier to price and trade, it is cheaper per dollar to run, and the manager may pick loans well. The biggest prize is cheaper funding, which means cheaper credit.

HEART: A company securitizing its own assets often sells them to a separate legal entity, a special purpose vehicle, which takes them off its balance sheet and walls them off, so its other creditors cannot reach them if it gets into trouble. And the cash flows can be split into tranches. Picture water poured into the top glass of a stack. The senior tranche fills first, so its cash flows are most certain. The junior tranche gets what spills over last, so it carries the most risk.

LEWIS: Depository institutions are banks, credit unions and savings and loans. Savers get interest and checking accounts, borrowers get loans, and depositors get diversification across thousands of loans plus the lender's credit skill. Other lenders fill corners. Payday lenders and factoring companies lend against wages or receivables, often funded with commercial paper. Brokers lend to margin buyers, and when they lend to hedge funds and institutions they are called prime brokers.

HEART: And here is the point about capital. Loan losses hit the owners' equity before depositors or other lenders lose anything. So more equity means safer depositors, and a thinly capitalized lender, with little of its own money at stake, has less reason to rein in risk. In bank and credit union capital planning, that is the whole case for a healthy capital cushion.

LEWIS: Insurance companies collect premiums from people who want to shed a risk. One house fire is unpredictable, but fires across a hundred thousand unrelated houses are quite predictable, just as a bank's loan losses steady out across a big portfolio. Insurers also manage three built in problems. Moral hazard is the insured taking more risk once covered, like a driver getting careless after buying full coverage. Adverse selection is the likeliest losers being the keenest buyers, like homeowners who know their basement floods. And fraud is staging or inventing a loss.

HEART: Arbitrageurs connect markets. Pure arbitrage, the riskless kind, means buying where an asset is cheap and selling at once where it is dear, adding a buyer in one market and bringing the asset to the other. Think of a stock listed in two countries that is cheaper in one after converting the currency. Where information flows well, that is rare, so more often they rely on pricing models to chase gaps between similar instruments, like buying a company's convertible bond and selling its stock short. Building a similar position from different assets is called replication, and it moves the same risk between markets in different forms.

LEWIS: Clearinghouses stand in the middle of trades. They act as escrow, guarantee that trades are completed, check that margin traders have enough capital, and limit each member's net orders, buys minus sells. All of that limits counterparty risk, the risk that the other side fails to deliver. In some markets the clearinghouse guarantees only its member brokers and dealers, who then guarantee their own retail customers. Custodians hold securities for clients, so if a broker or manager fails or commits fraud, the assets are safe, like jewelry in a bank's safe deposit box rather than at the shop that sold it.

HEART: The takeaway. The financial system lets people save, borrow, raise equity, hedge, trade and act on information, it sets the rates that balance saving and borrowing, and it steers capital to its best uses. Brokers find the other side, dealers are the other side, arbitrageurs bridge markets, and clearinghouses and custodians make it safe to deal with strangers.

## Module 39.2, Positions and Leverage

LEWIS: Every position comes down to one question. Do you make money when the price goes up, or when it goes down?

HEART: You are long if you own the asset now, or if a contract gives you the right or the duty to buy it later. Longs gain when the price rises. You are short if you sold an asset you borrowed and must replace, which is a short sale, or if a contract commits you to sell or deliver it. Shorts gain when the price falls.

LEWIS: Hedgers often take short positions on purpose. A copper mine that will sell next year's output is naturally long copper and loses if the price drops. If it sells copper futures today, a price drop makes the futures gain, offsetting some or all of the loss on the metal, because the two prices move together. So a business that will sell later hedges by selling futures, and one that will buy later, like the coffee roaster, hedges by buying them.

HEART: With options, the buyer is long the option and the seller, the writer, is short it. A call buyer gains when the underlying rises, and the writer loses. A put buyer holds the right to sell, so she gains when the underlying falls, and the writer loses.

LEWIS: Here is the trap. Long the put does not mean you want the price to rise. When a question asks which way someone wants the price to go, ask what happens to them if the underlying climbs.

HEART: In a swap, each side is long one thing and short another, so the label is often arbitrary. By convention, the side that gains when the quoted price or rate rises is usually called the long. And every currency trade is long one currency and short another. An importer that agrees now to buy pesos with dollars next quarter is long pesos and short dollars.

LEWIS: Now short selling. Through your broker, you borrow shares and sell them at the same time. Later you buy them back and return them, which is called covering the short, and the lender can ask for the shares back at any time. Picture a friend lending you a collectible sneaker that is selling for a fortune. You sell it, planning to buy an identical pair after the hype fades and hand that back. If the hype fades, you pocket the difference. If the price keeps climbing, you pay up.

HEART: The lender should not lose anything by lending, so the short seller pays the lender any dividends or interest paid while the security is on loan. Those are payments in lieu of dividends or interest.

LEWIS: And the cash from the sale is not yours to spend. It stays with the broker as collateral for the buyback. The broker earns interest on it and may pass part back to the short seller at the short rebate rate. That rebate usually goes only to institutions, it sits a little below overnight rates, and for a stock that is hard to borrow it can be lower or even negative. The lender of the shares earns the gap between the interest on the collateral and the rebate. The short seller may also have to post extra margin, in cash or short term riskless securities.

HEART: And the risk is lopsided. A stock can only fall to zero, so a short seller's gain is capped at what the shares sold for, but a price has no ceiling, so the potential loss has no limit. That is the mirror image of buying, where the most you can lose is what you paid.

LEWIS: Now leverage. Buying partly with borrowed money creates a leveraged position. Borrowing from your broker to buy securities is buying on margin, and the loan is a margin loan. It carries the call money rate, usually above the government bill rate and cheaper for big accounts with strong collateral, the way a strong borrower gets a better mortgage rate.

HEART: At the start you must put up a minimum amount of your own money, your equity. That minimum is the initial margin requirement, and it can be set by the government, the exchange, the clearinghouse or the broker. Brokers often lend more against a less risky portfolio.

LEWIS: The picture for leverage is a house bought with a mortgage. The whole house rises or falls in value, but only your down payment absorbs the change. A smaller down payment means more house per dollar of your own money, so every price move lands harder on your small slice.

HEART: The leverage ratio measures exactly that. It is the value of the position divided by your equity in it. The smaller your slice, the higher the ratio and the harder each move hits you. That extra risk is called risk from financial leverage, and a higher leverage ratio means more of it. The initial margin requirement caps it. The lower the minimum down payment, the higher the maximum leverage.

LEWIS: The return on a margin transaction compares what you gained with what you put in yourself. The gain is the change in the position's value, plus dividends, minus margin interest and both commissions, the buy and the sell. What you put in is your initial equity plus the purchase commission.

HEART: Here is the trap. It is tempting to take the asset's return and scale it up by the leverage ratio. But interest and commissions eat into every margin trade. A winner earns less than that tidy multiple, and a loser usually loses more.

LEWIS: Now the part that bites. After the purchase, your equity must stay above a minimum share of the position's current value, the maintenance margin requirement. It is commonly about a quarter of the position's value, and brokers can demand more for volatile stocks.

HEART: Here is why a falling price is so dangerous. The loan does not shrink when the price drops. So every dollar the position loses comes straight out of your equity, and your equity shrinks much faster than the price.

LEWIS: When your equity share falls below the maintenance margin, you get a margin call, a demand to bring your equity back up to the maintenance level. You can deposit cash, or other securities that are not already margined. If you do not, the broker must sell the position, often at the worst moment.

HEART: The price that sets off the call is the margin call price, and the direction is what matters. Put more of your own money in at the start, and you borrowed less, so the price has further to fall before the call. Raise the maintenance requirement, and the floor is higher, so the call comes sooner, at a higher price. And for a long position, the trigger sits below what you paid, because a rising price only adds to your equity. An answer above the purchase price is wrong.

LEWIS: Short sellers face margin calls from the other direction. They post initial margin as a share of the value of the shares sold short, and a rising price eats their equity, so a rally can push them below the maintenance level and trigger a call.

HEART: Banks and credit unions run on the same logic. A thin layer of capital under a large balance sheet means a small loss on assets takes a big bite out of capital, one reason regulators set minimum capital levels.

LEWIS: The takeaway. Longs win when prices rise and shorts win when they fall. Borrowing magnifies gains and losses, interest and commissions shave the result, and a falling price eats your equity until the broker calls.

## Module 39.3, Order Execution and Validity

HEART: Start with a price quote. A dealer's bid is what it will pay to buy from you. Its ask, also called the offer, is what it charges to sell to you. The gap is the bid ask spread, the dealer's pay, and each quote is good for a stated size.

LEWIS: Think of an airport currency booth, buying euros cheap and selling them dear. The spread always favors the booth, as it does any dealer. You buy at its higher price and sell at its lower one. With currencies, whichever way the rate is written, the rate you get is the one that leaves you fewer units of the currency you are acquiring.

HEART: Across all dealers, the market quotation is the highest bid and the lowest ask. More liquid securities have narrower spreads relative to price, so they are cheaper to trade. Traders who post bids and offers make the market, and traders who hit those posted prices take the market.

LEWIS: Every order says buy or sell and how much. Then it can carry three kinds of instructions. Execution instructions say how to trade. Validity instructions say when the order can be filled. Clearing instructions say how the trade settles.

HEART: The main execution instructions are market orders and limit orders. A market order says trade now at the best price available. A limit order sets a ceiling on what you will pay to buy, or a floor on what you will accept to sell. Think of hailing a ride in a downpour. A market order takes the first car at any surge price, and gets you home for sure at an unknown fare. A limit order rides only under your cap, which protects your wallet but may leave you standing in the rain.

LEWIS: That is the tradeoff. A market order buys execution certainty and gives up price certainty. A limit order buys price certainty and gives up execution certainty.

HEART: A market order fits when speed matters more than price, say a fund manager sure that the price has not caught up with fresh news. The cost is a possibly ugly fill, especially when the order is large relative to normal volume, since a big buy pushes the price up as it fills. That giveaway is the price of instant liquidity, and you cannot know in advance how big it will be.

LEWIS: The limit order's cost is the trade that never happens. If nobody will sell at your limit or lower, you get nothing, and if the stock then climbs, you missed the very move you wanted.

HEART: Where you place a limit has its own vocabulary. A buy limit high enough to reach the best ask, or a sell limit low enough to reach the best bid, is marketable, or aggressively priced, since some or all of it can trade on arrival. A limit between the best bid and best ask is making a new market, also called inside the market, and a buy there becomes the new best bid. A buy at the best bid, or a sell at the best ask, makes the market. Orders waiting like this are standing limit orders.

LEWIS: A buy below the best bid, or a sell above the best ask, is behind the market, and it will most likely sit unfilled unless prices drift its way. Well below or well above, and it is far from the market. The trap is that a marketable limit order is still a limit order. It never fills at a worse price than its limit, and if the quote moves away, the rest just waits.

HEART: Other execution instructions deal with size and visibility. An all or nothing order trades only if the whole quantity fills. A minimum trade size stops an order being chopped into tiny fills, which matters when you pay per trade rather than per share. A hidden order's size is known only to the broker or exchange, so a big investor does not tip off the market. A display size order shows part and hides the rest, which is why it is called an iceberg order, and showing a small piece can also test how much interest is out there.

LEWIS: Validity instructions say when an order is alive. The usual choice is a day order, which lapses unfilled when that session ends. A good till cancelled order stays open until it fills or you cancel it. An immediate or cancel order, which you will also hear called fill or kill, must trade the moment it arrives, and whatever cannot trade at once is cancelled. Some platforms keep the two names apart, and there fill or kill is stricter. The whole order fills at once, or none of it does.

HEART: Good on close orders fill only as the day's trading ends, and when they are market orders, they are called market on close orders, which mutual funds favor because they price their shares from closing prices. Good on open orders fill only as trading begins.

LEWIS: Stop orders are validity instructions too, because they do nothing until the market reaches a stop price you set. Often called stop loss orders, they can limit a loss or lock in part of a gain. Say you own a stock that has had a great run and you want to keep most of the gain if it turns. You set a stop sell somewhat below today's price. If the stock trades at or below the stop, your order becomes a market order to sell.

HEART: Here is the trap. The stop price is a trigger, not a promise. Once triggered, it is a market order, so in a fast falling market or after bad news overnight, the fill can land far below your stop.

LEWIS: A stop buy sits above the current price. A short seller sets one to cap losses if the price climbs. And an investor who thinks a stock is undervalued, but wants to see other buyers come around first, can set a stop buy above the current price so she buys only once it starts rising.

HEART: Notice that stops fire in the direction the market is already moving. Stop sells execute into falling prices and stop buys into rising ones. So stops add to momentum, and their fills are often unfavorable. Investors often raise a stop sell as a stock climbs, keeping it a set distance below the latest price, which is usually called a trailing stop.

LEWIS: And the classic confusion. Do not protect a long position with a sell limit priced below the market. A sell limit means sell at this price or better. The current price is already better, so it would most likely fill at once. A stop sell below the market waits. A sell limit below the market fires.

HEART: Clearing instructions say who clears and settles the trade, and they are usually standing instructions set up once rather than attached to each order. A retail investor's broker usually handles it. An institution may use a custodian or another broker, often its prime broker, which handles margin and safekeeping while other brokers handle execution. Every sell order must also say whether it is a long sale, so the broker confirms you can deliver, or a short sale, so the broker confirms it can borrow the security.

LEWIS: Now where securities come from. The primary market is where newly issued securities are sold. A company whose shares already trade can sell more in a seasoned offering, also called a secondary issue. A first sale to the public is an initial public offering, an IPO. The trap is that a secondary issue is still a primary market deal. New shares, money to the issuer, primary market.

HEART: The secondary market is everything after that, investors trading existing shares while the company collects nothing.

LEWIS: New issues almost always go through an investment bank. It spreads information about the company, the issuer discloses things like how it will spend the money, and the bank collects indications of interest, which are not orders. If interest exceeds the shares on offer, the price may be nudged up, and if it falls short, down. That process is book building. In London the bank running it is the book runner, and in Europe, when securities must be sold quickly, the bank runs an accelerated book build.

HEART: The most common route is an underwritten offering, where the bank commits to take the whole issue at a price agreed with the issuer, so if the issue is undersubscribed, the bank keeps the unsold shares. After an IPO, the bank also stands ready to buy and sell the stock for a while, to steady its price. In a best efforts offering, the bank sells what it can as a broker and is not obliged to buy the rest, so the issuer carries that risk.

LEWIS: And the underwriter has a conflict of interest. Working for the issuer, the bank owes it the highest price, but as the firm stuck with whatever does not sell, it prefers one low enough to sell out, and a low price also lets it hand bargain shares to favored clients. So initial public offerings are typically underpriced. Issuers may go along, because an IPO that trades below its offer price makes bad headlines. An oversubscribed IPO expected to trade well above its offer price is a hot issue, and a big first day jump means money was most likely left on the table.

HEART: In a private placement, securities go directly to qualified investors with substantial wealth and knowledge. The issuer discloses less and pays less to issue, but the securities cannot be resold publicly, so buyers pay a lower price. With a shelf registration, a company files its disclosures as usual, then sells the registered securities bit by bit, whenever it needs cash and conditions look good. A dividend reinvestment plan lets shareholders turn dividends into new shares from the company, usually at a slight discount.

LEWIS: In a rights offering, existing shareholders get the right to buy new shares below the market price. They tend to dislike it, because unless they exercise and buy, their stake is diluted, though sometimes the rights can be sold on their own. And governments raise short and long term money through auctions or through investment banks.

HEART: So why does an issuer care about the secondary market, which pays it nothing? Because it gives investors liquidity and price information, and liquidity means you can sell fast without having to knock the price down. A resellable concert ticket is worth more than one you cannot resell, so a strong secondary market makes primary market fundraising easier and lowers the cost of capital.

LEWIS: Next, how trading is organized. First, when. In a call market, trading happens only at set times. All bids and asks are declared and a single price clears the market. It can be very liquid during a session, because everyone is there at once, and illiquid between sessions. Smaller markets trade this way, and major exchanges do it to set opening prices and to restart after a trading halt. In a continuous market, trades happen any time the market is open, priced by auction or by dealer quotes.

HEART: Then how, with three structures. In a quote driven market, also called a dealer, price driven or over the counter market, you trade with dealers, or market makers, who post bids and asks from inventory. Most securities other than stocks trade this way, often electronically.

LEWIS: In an order driven market, like an exchange or an automated trading system, anonymous buyers and sellers trade with each other under two sets of rules. Order matching rules set the pecking order. Price comes first, so the highest bid and lowest ask are served first. At the same price, visible orders beat hidden ones, and earlier orders beat later ones. That rewards aggressive pricing, full display and early arrival, which all add liquidity.

HEART: Trade pricing rules then set the price. Under the uniform pricing rule, everyone trades at one price, the one that lets the most volume trade, which is how call markets work. Under the discriminatory pricing rule, a trade happens at the limit price of whichever order arrived first. Under the derivative pricing rule, used by crossing networks that serve mostly institutions, orders are batched and matched at fixed times at the midpoint of the best bid and ask in the stock's main market. The network's own orders do not set the price.

LEWIS: In a brokered market, a broker hunts down a counterparty for something unique or rarely traded, like a large block of stock, a building or a painting. Dealers do not stock such things, and there are too few trades for an order driven market.

HEART: Markets also differ in what you can see. Pre trade transparency means seeing quotes and orders before trading, and post trade transparency means seeing the prices and sizes of completed trades. Buy side traders value transparency because it helps them judge value and costs. Dealers prefer opaque markets, where knowing more than occasional traders is an edge, and where costs and spreads are bigger.

LEWIS: What makes a financial system work well? Complete markets, meaning savers earn fair returns, creditworthy borrowers get funds, hedgers manage risks, and traders get the currencies, commodities and other assets they need. Operational efficiency, meaning low trading costs, counting commissions, bid ask spreads and price impact. And informational efficiency, meaning prices reflect fundamental information quickly, because traders bid them up and down as news arrives.

HEART: It also needs intermediaries that run venues, supply liquidity, securitize assets, run banks and insurers, give low cost advice, settle trades and keep assets safe. The payoff is huge. Savers fund entrepreneurs, risk gets shared so risky companies can be funded, and strangers can deal with each other.

LEWIS: The result is allocational efficiency, capital going to its most productive uses, which informationally efficient prices make possible. And the pieces reinforce each other. Cheap trading encourages trading on news, so operational efficiency improves informational efficiency, and reporting standards cut the cost of information, which raises what securities are worth.

HEART: Here is the trap. Operational efficiency is about cost. Informational efficiency is about prices. Allocational efficiency is about where the capital ends up, and it is the payoff of the other two.

LEWIS: Finally, regulation. Without it, four problems fester. Fraud and theft, because sophisticated agents can exploit unsophisticated clients, and noisy returns make it hard to tell whether a manager is any good. Insider trading, which drives investors away and drains liquidity. Costly information, which makes prices less efficient and discourages investment. And defaults.

HEART: So regulation aims to protect unsophisticated investors so trust survives, set minimum standards of competence and make performance easier to judge, which is the job of efforts like the CFA Program and the Global Investment Performance Standards, and stop insiders from exploiting others. It requires common financial reporting, like the standards of the International Accounting Standards Board, so information is cheaper to gather. And it requires minimum capital, so firms like insurers and pension funds can honor long term promises and stay careful with risk.

LEWIS: Regulators include governments and industry groups. Most exchanges, clearinghouses and dealer trade groups are self regulatory organizations that regulate their members, sometimes with authority handed down by government. When regulation fails, trading thins out, companies avoid bold bets, inventors go unfunded, and the economy grows more slowly.

HEART: The takeaway. Market and limit orders each trade one kind of certainty for the other, and a stop is a trigger, not a price. New issues are sold in the primary market, liquid secondary markets make them cheaper to sell, and good markets are complete, cheap to trade and quick to reflect information, so capital lands where it does the most good.

## Recall check

LEWIS Q: An airline buys jet fuel futures to lock in what it will pay next winter. Is it an information motivated trader?

HEART: Answer. No, it is a hedger. Here is why. It is not trading because it thinks the market has the fuel price wrong. Its business already carries the risk of a fuel price spike, and the futures position offsets that risk. An information motivated trader expects an extra return from being right about value, while a hedger is reducing a risk it already has.

HEART Q: A company issues ninety day commercial paper and also sells new common shares. Which belongs to the money market and which to the capital market?

LEWIS: Answer. The commercial paper is a money market instrument, and the shares belong to the capital market. Here is why. The dividing line is maturity. Debt maturing in one year or less trades in the money market. Longer debt and all equity, which never matures, trade in capital markets, no matter who issued them.

LEWIS Q: A firm buys corporate bonds from one client on Monday, holds them, and sells them to another client on Friday at a higher price. Is it acting as a broker or a dealer?

HEART: Answer. A dealer. Here is why. A dealer trades from its own inventory and connects buyers and sellers who show up at different times, earning the bid ask spread for providing that liquidity. A broker never owns the bonds. It only finds the other side of the client's trade.

HEART Q: An insurer finds that people who know their homes sit in flood zones are its keenest buyers of flood coverage. What problem is that, and how does it differ from moral hazard?

LEWIS: Answer. Adverse selection. Here is why. Adverse selection is about who buys. The people most likely to have losses are the ones most eager to insure. Moral hazard is about behavior after buying, when people covered against a loss take more risk. One skews the pool before the policy is written, and the other changes behavior after it.

LEWIS Q: An investor buys a put option. Is she long or short the option, and does she want the underlying price to rise or fall?

HEART: Answer. Long the put, and she wants the price to fall. Here is why. Buying any option makes you long that option. A put is the right to sell at a fixed price, and that right becomes more valuable as the asset's price drops. The writer of the put is short the option and does better when the price holds up.

HEART Q: Two investors buy the same stock on margin, one with a much smaller down payment. The price falls. Whose equity falls faster in proportion, and who gets a margin call first?

LEWIS: Answer. The one with the smaller down payment, on both counts. Here is why. That investor has the higher leverage ratio, so the same price drop is a bigger share of a smaller equity slice. The loan does not shrink as the price falls, so the loss comes entirely out of equity, and the thinner slice reaches the maintenance floor sooner.

LEWIS Q: A broker raises the maintenance margin requirement on a volatile stock. Does the margin call price move up or down?

HEART: Answer. Up, so the call comes sooner. Here is why. The maintenance margin is the minimum equity share you must keep. Raise that floor, and your shrinking equity hits it after a smaller price decline, which means at a higher price. A bigger down payment at the start does the opposite, pushing the trigger price lower.

HEART Q: You must buy a stock before an announcement and do not care about a few cents. Market order or limit order, and what are you giving up?

LEWIS: Answer. A market order, and you give up price certainty. Here is why. A market order trades immediately at the best available price, so execution is all but certain. But the fill can be worse than you expect, especially if your order is large relative to normal trading. A limit order would protect the price but might leave you with no shares at all.

LEWIS Q: You enter a buy limit order priced between the best bid and the best ask. What is that called, and what most likely happens?

HEART: Answer. It is making a new market, also called inside the market, and it most likely waits. Here is why. Your bid is higher than any other bid, so it becomes the new best bid, but it is still below what any seller is asking. It fills only if a seller comes down to your price.

HEART Q: An IPO is heavily oversubscribed and jumps on its first day of trading. Was it most likely priced too high or too low, and why would that happen?

LEWIS: Answer. Too low. Here is why. Demand far beyond the shares offered shows investors would have paid more. The underwriter has a conflict of interest, because a low price makes the whole issue easy to sell, cuts its own risk of holding unsold shares, and lets it reward favored clients with bargain shares. The issuer may accept it to avoid an embarrassing first day drop.

## Key takeaways

HEART: First, a few memory hooks.

LEWIS: For the three functions of the financial system, think do, price and place. It lets people do things like save, borrow, hedge and trade, it prices money by balancing saving and borrowing, and it places capital where it is most valuable.

HEART: Money markets hold debt of a year or less, and capital markets hold longer debt plus all equity. Primary means the issuer gets the cash, and a secondary issue is still primary.

LEWIS: For the middlemen, one verb each. Brokers find. Dealers hold. Arbitrageurs bridge. Securitizers bundle. Insurers pool. Clearinghouses guarantee. Custodians guard. Brokers and exchanges connect people at the same moment, dealers across time, and arbitrageurs across venues.

HEART: For positions: long likes up, short likes down. The put buyer is long the put but wants the asset to fall. A short seller's gain is capped because a price stops at zero, and the loss has no ceiling.

LEWIS: For leverage: smaller slice, bigger swing. The loan never shrinks, so a falling price eats your equity. And interest and commissions shave every margin return.

HEART: For margin calls: more money down, more room to fall. Higher maintenance floor, sooner call. For a long position, the trigger sits below what you paid.

LEWIS: For orders: a market order is sure to trade but not sure of the price, and a limit order is sure of the price but not sure to trade. A stop is a trigger, not a price, and stops chase momentum. Execution is how, validity is when, clearing is settling up.

HEART: For order driven markets: price first, then visible, then early. Uniform is one price for all, discriminatory is the waiting order's price, and derivative is borrowed from the main market. For a good system: operational is cost, informational is prices, allocational is the payoff.

LEWIS: Now, how you would say the big ideas to a senior leader.

HEART: On the system: markets move money and risk to whoever can put them to best use. The jargon is the functions of the financial system and allocational efficiency.

LEWIS: On who we trade with: some firms only find us a counterparty, while others trade with us from their own inventory and profit from the gap. The jargon is brokers, dealers, the bid ask spread and the broker dealer conflict of interest.

HEART: On borrowing to invest: it magnifies gains and losses alike, and if prices fall far enough, the lender forces us to add cash or sell at the worst moment. The jargon is the leverage ratio, maintenance margin and the margin call.

LEWIS: On trading: we can guarantee the trade or the price, not both, and a protective sell trigger does not lock in a price. The jargon is market orders, limit orders and stop orders.

HEART: On raising capital: the easier our securities are to resell, the cheaper money is to raise, and a big first day jump means the new issue was probably priced too low. The jargon is secondary market liquidity, the cost of capital and IPO underpricing.

LEWIS: And on regulation: protecting investors, requiring honest reporting and demanding enough capital keep strangers willing to trade with each other. The jargon is the objectives of market regulation, minimum capital requirements and self regulatory organizations.
