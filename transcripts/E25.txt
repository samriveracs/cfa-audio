LEWIS: Reading 44 covers why the industry shapes a company's profits, how industries are classified and sized up, the five forces and outside influences, and the three ways to compete. Reading 45 covers what analysts forecast and how, from revenue to costs, working capital, capital spending and debt, plus the horizon and scenarios.

## Module 44.1, Industry Analysis

HEART: Start with a puzzle. Put a gifted manager in charge of a company making plain cardboard boxes in a crowded field, and an ordinary manager in charge of the only landfill within a day's drive of a big city. Over a decade, the ordinary manager very often earns the better return on capital. The industry did most of the work.

LEWIS: That is the core idea. Each industry carries its own mix of opportunity and risk, so some earn far more than others over the long run. And a company's chance of earning economic profit year after year, a return on its invested capital above what that capital costs, rests mostly on which industry it is in.

HEART: Competition works like gravity. Over time it drags each company's returns toward the industry's typical level, called the industry base rate. Within one industry, the gaps that remain come from business model, size and competitive strategy. So the industry acts as a ceiling, but a company trailing its peers usually has itself to blame.

LEWIS: So the analyst has three tasks. Pin down the industry's base rate of profitability and its drivers, decide where that rate is heading, and place each company above or below the industry's median or average.

HEART: It pays off in two ways. First, better forecasts, built on what drives sales and margins across the whole industry. Respect the big outside forces, because a company's own decisions, alone, go only so far.

LEWIS: Second, finding overlooked investments, such as an industry growing faster than the market believes. And a portfolio angle: an investor sure about the industry but not the winner can hold small positions in several of its companies, keeping the industry risk while spreading out the company risk.

HEART: The analysis runs in five steps. Step one, define the industry. Step two, the industry survey: size, growth, profitability and the trend in market shares. Step three, analyze its structure with a framework such as Porter's five forces, and decide which forces matter most. Step four, examine outside influences with a checklist called PESTLE. Step five, analyze each company's competitive strategy and its advantages.

LEWIS: Defining the industry takes judgment. What makes companies belong together, similar products or the same region? And what about a company working in several industries at once? Ready made classification systems help. Early government systems grouped companies by stage of production, such as manufacturing, distribution or retail, were rarely updated, and fell out of date. Private index and data firms replaced them with global systems that group companies by the products and services they sell, which lets you track trends and valuations and compare companies across countries. A group of similar industries is called a sector.

HEART: Three systems to know. The Global Industry Classification Standard, G I C S, is maintained by S and P Dow Jones Indices and M S C I. The Industry Classification Benchmark, I C B, comes from Footsie Russell and covers public companies. And the Refinitiv Business Classification, T R B C, also covers private companies, not for profit organizations and government bodies.

LEWIS: Each is a ladder of tiers. G I C S has four: sector, industry group, industry and sub industry. I C B also has four: industry, supersector, sector and subsector. T R B C has five: economic sector, business sector, industry group, industry and activity. Notice the trap. The word sector sits at the top of G I C S but on the third rung of I C B.

HEART: Despite the labels, all three start from essentially the same eleven top level groups: energy, basic materials, industrials, consumer discretionary, consumer staples, health care, financials, information technology, telecommunications services, utilities and real estate.

LEWIS: How does a company get its label? By its principal business activity. One line of business, and that line is its group. Several lines, and a line producing more than sixty percent of revenue decides. If none clears sixty percent, a line producing more than half of revenue, profits or assets decides. If nothing clears that bar, the provider falls back on judgment, or calls it a conglomerate.

HEART: Picture a company that makes washing machines and runs a consumer lending arm that finances them. If appliances bring in more than sixty percent of revenue, it is an appliance maker, even if the lending arm earns most of the profit.

LEWIS: Which points to the four limits of classification. First, inappropriate groupings. A group can be too wide or too narrow for your question. A broad machinery group might hold makers of farm tractors and makers of factory robots, which rarely compete, while a video streaming service and a video game publisher, competing for the same evening hours, can land in different groups. The fix is to move up or down a tier, or regroup by hand.

HEART: Second, companies selling many products get one label, so the washing machine company's lending arm disappears from view.

LEWIS: Third, geography. Some services are sold only locally, such as trash collection, so the real market is a city or a country, while a mobile game studio sells almost anywhere from day one. One global label cannot capture that.

HEART: Fourth, grouping changes. Providers redraw their groups as businesses change, which breaks long runs of industry data, so old figures may need restating. Companies arriving and failing also change who sits in each group. And over long stretches, industry data can suffer from survivorship bias. Study an industry's past using only today's companies and you see only the winners, so the industry looks more profitable and less risky than it really was.

LEWIS: Because of these limits, analysts build their own peer group, a hand picked set of companies driven by the same things, such as the same customers, inputs and sensitivity to the economy. Start from the classification system, then narrow it to the companies that truly compete.

HEART: Products are not the only way to group companies, and the other groupings are handy for building indexes and for performance attribution, which explains where a portfolio's return came from.

LEWIS: One is geography: developed, emerging or frontier markets. The label usually follows the head office or the stock listing, even when most sales come from abroad.

HEART: Another is business cycle sensitivity, on a scale from defensive to cyclical. A defensive company sells things whose demand stays fairly steady through booms and recessions, the items people need for daily life, like toothpaste, electricity, prescription medicine and phone service.

LEWIS: A cyclical company rides the business cycle, its earnings soaring in booms and sinking in slumps. It usually has high operating leverage, so a small change in sales becomes a big change in profit. Think of materials and equipment that other producers buy, or big ticket purchases a buyer can postpone, like steel, cranes, new cars and cruises.

HEART: The trap is the word defensive. It describes how steady demand is. It does not tell you whether the shares are cheap, whether they can still fall hard, or whether the company is growing.

LEWIS: A third way is by financial measures, such as size, valuation ratios, profitability or growth rates. Membership in these groups turns over far more often, since a company can go from cheap to expensive in a year but rarely changes its home country or its industry.

HEART: Statistical methods can group companies too. Cluster analysis gathers companies whose returns have tended to move together, whatever they sell, so each cluster moves less in step with the others. And companies can be grouped by environmental, social and governance factors, ESG, such as carbon emissions or board independence, turned into standardized ESG scores.

LEWIS: On to the industry survey, starting with size. Industry size is the total annual sales of the product, not the total sales of all the companies in the industry. If a big industrial group makes jet engines, medical scanners and home backup generators, only its generator sales count toward the generator industry.

HEART: When many sellers are private or unincorporated, like plumbing contractors, analysts gauge size from government economic data or independent research firms.

LEWIS: Growth can be measured each year or as a compound annual growth rate over several years. Analysts broadly sort industries into growth and mature.

HEART: A growth industry still has lots of room to run, often because of new technology, and it tends to grow whatever the economy is doing. Think of home batteries that store power from rooftop solar panels. The hard call is how long the fast growth lasts, and an unproven technology makes that call harder.

LEWIS: A mature industry has little growth left. It grows roughly in line with the economy, or shrinks if a substitute is taking its place, the way landline phone service has. Here the analyst watches for shifts in how fiercely companies compete, and for signs of decline.

HEART: Cyclical industries also have bumpier returns, and since investors never agree on how long or deep the next slump will be, their valuations spread widely.

LEWIS: Put the two ideas together and you get a style box, a two by two grid of cyclical or defensive against growth or mature. A grocery chain is defensive and mature. A maker of mining trucks is cyclical and mature. A company selling treatments for a newly common chronic illness is defensive and growth. A maker of factory robots is cyclical and growth.

HEART: The grid has drawbacks. A deep recession hurts nearly everyone, so defensive and cyclical describe more or less sensitivity, not immunity. And a fast growing company in a cyclical industry can sail through a recession, because more customers keep adopting its product whatever the economy does.

LEWIS: Many analysts also map the industry life cycle: embryonic, growth, shakeout, mature and decline. The embryonic stage brings slow growth, high prices, heavy investment and a high risk of failure. In the growth stage demand rises fast, prices fall, profits improve, and competition is fairly mild, because the market expands fast enough for everyone. The shakeout comes when growth slows but capacity keeps arriving, so competition turns fierce, profits fall and weaker companies fail. Maturity brings slow growth, consolidation, stable prices and high entry barriers such as brand loyalty and efficient costs. Decline brings shrinking demand, falling prices, excess capacity and exits.

HEART: The trap is assuming the fastest growth brings the fiercest fight. Competition is mild in the growth stage and turns fierce in the shakeout. And the stages are a description, not a timetable. New technology or new rules can restart growth or cut a stage short.

LEWIS: Next, profitability. The ideal measure is return on invested capital, ROIC, an after tax return that does not depend on how the company is financed. Rank companies by decile or percentile within the industry and watch how each one moves over time. For private companies that publish no accounts, analysts assume returns like their public peers, infer them from product prices and production costs, or lean on government or third party data. The goal is the trend: rising, falling or steady.

HEART: Then market share, a company's annual revenue divided by the industry's size. Since industry size is a rough figure, shares are rough too, and the trend matters most. A rising share suggests customers like what the company sells. With one trap. Buying a competitor raises share automatically. A pest control company that buys a rival did not win those customers, it bought them. So check share growth net of acquisitions.

LEWIS: Last, concentration. The standard measure is the Herfindahl Hirschman index, H H I. Take each company's market share as a whole number, square it, and add up the squares.

HEART: Squaring gives big companies far more weight than small ones. Four equal companies score lower than one giant with three small rivals. A single company with the whole market scores ten thousand, the maximum. Below fifteen hundred is low concentration, fifteen hundred to twenty five hundred is moderate, and above twenty five hundred is high.

LEWIS: And the direction. Low or falling concentration usually means more intense competition, less pricing power and lower profits. High or rising concentration means the reverse. The exceptions are local industries, where a fragmented national market can still be a near monopoly in each town, and highly differentiated products, where even a crowded field can earn good margins.

HEART: The takeaway. The industry sets the ceiling, and competition pulls companies toward its base rate. Labels follow the main business, with real limits, and the H H I squares the shares so the giants dominate the score.

## Module 44.2, Industry Structure and Competitive Positioning

LEWIS: An industry creates value. How much do its companies keep, and how much leaks to customers, suppliers, newcomers and substitutes? Michael Porter's five forces answer that, by setting how intense competition is and, in the end, long run profitability.

HEART: The headline rule: let some or all of the forces run strong, and economic profit, return on invested capital minus the cost of that capital, will likely be squeezed to about zero. So strong forces do not mean accounting losses. They mean a return that barely covers what the capital costs.

LEWIS: Force one, rivalry among existing competitors. Picture ocean container shipping, with several big lines of broadly similar size. A ship costs a fortune whether it sails full or half empty, every line carries the same steel boxes, and a ship cannot be turned into anything else. When demand slips, each line cuts rates to fill its ships, and everyone's profits sink together.

HEART: That story holds every driver of rivalry. Many companies of similar size, so nobody sets the price for the rest. Slow growth, so the only way to grow is to take share. High fixed costs, so companies cut prices to keep running at full capacity. Products that look alike, so price is all that is left. And high exit barriers, so losing companies stay and keep fighting.

LEWIS: Force two, the threat of new entrants. Barriers to entry, such as huge upfront capital, economies of scale, licenses and patents, keep newcomers out and make it easier to hold premium prices. Nobody builds a second freight railroad beside an existing one. Open a food truck, though, and a good idea gets copied within months.

HEART: Here is the trap that costs points. Entry barriers and exit barriers push in opposite directions. High entry barriers protect profits by keeping rivals out. High exit barriers hurt profits by keeping weak rivals in.

LEWIS: Force three, the threat of substitutes, different products meeting the same need. Video calls substitute for business trips. Streaming at home substitutes for the movie theater. Substitutes cap prices because they make demand more elastic, meaning more sensitive to price. Raise ticket prices too far and families stay home.

HEART: Do not confuse a substitute with a rival's product. Another cinema chain belongs under rivalry. And when everyone sells the same commodity, price is the only weapon and margins stay thin. The more products differ, the less price decides the sale. Patents can shield a product from copies for years.

LEWIS: Force four, the bargaining power of customers, their ability to push for lower prices or better quality. Picture a frozen vegetable maker selling mostly through a handful of national grocery chains. Each chain can hand its freezer space to a rival or to its own store brand, so the chain gets the better terms.

HEART: Force five, the bargaining power of suppliers, their ability to raise prices or limit supply. Suppliers hold the cards when there are only a few of them and what they sell is hard to get. Airlines buy large jets from essentially two makers worldwide, and when the waiting list stretches for years, airlines have little say on price.

LEWIS: Two forces need extra attention, since nearly every company must worry about them: the threat of new entrants and rivalry. A few rules of thumb tie them together. Higher entry barriers reduce competition. Greater concentration reduces competition, while fragmentation, many companies each holding a sliver, raises it. Unused capacity, especially if it lasts, brings fierce price competition. Stable market shares, usually from loyal customers, reduce competition. More price sensitive customers mean more competition. And as an industry matures, growth slows.

HEART: Step four looks outside with PESTLE: political, economic, social, technological, legal and environmental influences. The five forces describe the contest inside the industry. PESTLE scans the world around it. Because these factors change gradually, it needs repeating less often than the forces analysis, and you focus on the few that really bite.

LEWIS: Political influences work through taxes, regulation and spending, and three sectors are most exposed. In energy, governments want low and steady fuel prices, because voters feel every rise at the pump, yet climate goals push away from cheap fossil fuels, and the group of oil exporting countries called OPEC has real sway over prices. In health care, governments are the largest buyers, so politics decides how much care is bought and whether prices are capped or services rationed. In defense, the government is usually the only customer, so a naval shipbuilder's outlook is really a forecast of a defense budget shaped by threats, alliance commitments and rival priorities such as health care.

HEART: Economic influences come at two speeds: cyclical, like the swings in GDP, and structural, like productivity and the size of the workforce. Interest rates move borrowing costs and bank profits, the supply of credit drives spending, and inflation reaches costs, prices, rates and confidence. Cyclical sectors feel these most.

LEWIS: Social influences are changes in how people work, shop and relax, and in what they value, and they matter most to companies selling to individuals. The move to working from home lifted home office furniture and hurt downtown lunch spots. Pressure for greener materials and fair treatment of the workers who make the products belongs here too.

HEART: Technological influences can make old products redundant. Sustaining innovation improves a product without changing what it is, like each new washing machine using a little less water. Disruptive innovation creates a new market, or creates value in a whole new way in an existing one. Smartphone maps nearly wiped out the navigation gadgets people stuck on their dashboards.

LEWIS: Disruption usually comes from newcomers. Incumbents can keep doing well while customers slowly adopt the new thing, but to keep market share over the long run they have to join the disruption, even though it eats into their old business. That is called cannibalization.

HEART: Legal influences are changes in laws and regulations, bringing risks and opportunities. A ban on single use plastic bags hurts the bag makers and helps makers of reusable totes.

LEWIS: Environmental influences, such as climate change and doubts about sustainability, keep gaining weight. Think of a home insurer in wildfire country facing rising losses, forced into much higher premiums or a retreat.

HEART: Now step five, the company's own strategy. Every company has one, whether it means to or not. An intentional strategy is planned, carried out, reviewed and refined in repeated cycles. An unintentional one is haphazard, mostly copying past or industry habits, and it usually gives worse results, though now and then a loosely organized team stumbles onto a big success.

LEWIS: How do you know a strategy works? Looking back, it shows up as consistent, positive economic profit over the long run. Looking forward, ask three questions. Does it respond well to the forces that matter here? Is it neutral to, or helped by, the outside influences? And can the company actually carry it out?

HEART: Porter named three generic strategies, and in his view a company has to choose one.

LEWIS: The first is cost leadership: the lowest costs in the industry, the lowest prices, and enough volume to earn a superior return. It can be played defensively, to protect share, or offensively, to take share, and managers should be rewarded for efficiency. Picture a discount grocery chain with a short list of store brands, plain stores and a culture that questions every expense. Its tools are economies of scale, favorable access to supplies, strict cost control, aggressive pricing, low cost distribution, and economies of scope, meaning savings from sharing resources across related products.

HEART: Why does it hold up? Against new entrants, because matching its scale takes enormous capital. Against powerful customers, because they can only push prices down to the costs of the marginal producer, the least efficient company still needed to meet demand. The cost leader's costs sit below that, so it keeps a margin. And against rivals, because they cannot match its prices.

LEWIS: It suits capital intensive industries with price conscious customers who do not notice product differences, and little innovation. The risks are cost inflation, sloppy cost discipline, a technology shift that hands the cost edge to someone else, and customers who start wanting premium versions.

HEART: The second is differentiation: products that stand out in type, quality or delivery. The key condition is that the extra cost of being different must be less than the price premium buyers will pay, and that premium has to last. Picture a maker of premium outdoor jackets that offers free repairs for life. It invests in brand, advertising and service and protects designs with trademarks and patents. Other differentiators tie services, software and hardware into one package that is hard to copy. Either way, it takes strong market research, production and creative advertising.

LEWIS: Loyalty to a distinctive product deters entrants and substitutes, and customers may be unable or unwilling to shop around. Against suppliers, the differentiator can often pass a cost rise on, or has the margin to absorb it. It fits markets where price is not the main concern, customers value being different, and steady innovation varies the features. The risks: imitation, buyers growing sophisticated enough not to need the extra service, a premium that grows too large, and exclusivity itself capping market share.

HEART: The third is focus, aiming at a niche, borrowing from cost leadership, differentiation or both. Picture a lender that serves only doctors, with mortgages built for young physicians who carry big student loans but have strong future earnings. It wins by staying near its customers and knowing their needs better than anyone.

LEWIS: Niche loyalty defends against entrants and substitutes, and customers who feel understood shop around less. It suits a group, product or region that is hard or uneconomical for bigger companies to serve. The risks: a larger rival undercuts it on price, the niche's needs drift toward the wider market's, or buyers outgrow the special treatment.

HEART: The trap. The company with the lowest prices is not automatically the cost leader. Cutting prices without lower costs just shrinks margins.

LEWIS: The takeaway. Strong forces push economic profit toward zero, and PESTLE scans the slower moving world outside. And a company must pick cost leadership, differentiation or focus, then prove it with lasting economic profit.

## Module 45.1, Forecasting in Company Analysis

LEWIS: Episode 21 built a full forecast line by line, with the biases, the horizon and the terminal value. This module is the toolkit: what to forecast, which approach fits which company, and how to handle each major line.

HEART: Forecasts feed valuation and investment recommendations. Those published for outsiders highlight revenue and earnings per share, EPS, while a company's internal forecasts go much deeper and run many years ahead.

LEWIS: What you forecast is called the forecast object, and there are four kinds. The first is a financial statement line with clear drivers. A hotel chain's room revenue is rooms, times the share filled each night, times the average nightly rate. The advantages are explanatory power and accuracy. The drawback is that a line can have many drivers that are hard to forecast together.

HEART: The second is a line without clear drivers, forecast directly from a management figure or by adjusting last year's amount. The third is a summary measure, such as EPS or free cash flow, forecast as one number. It is quick and works best when the measure does not swing much, but it is less transparent, because readers cannot see what drives it.

LEWIS: The fourth is an ad hoc object, an event the statements do not yet show. It might be a contingent liability, such as a pending lawsuit over a faulty product, or a potential gain or loss, such as an expected insurance settlement after a warehouse fire.

HEART: Two principles. Build on information that is easy to get and arrives regularly, so a breakdown by product line or division helps, and consolidated totals alone make the job harder. And keep the model lean. Extra detail costs time to build and maintain and may add no accuracy, so cut any step that barely changes the answer.

LEWIS: Next, the four approaches, used alone or, more often, combined. The first is historical results: assume the past continues. It works best for noncyclical or mature businesses, like a water utility, and for small items that hardly matter.

HEART: It goes wrong for cyclical companies, because comparing one year with the next may set two different stages of the cycle side by side, so the forecast should span a whole cycle. And it goes wrong for a company changing its strategy or operations, because the past describes a business that no longer exists.

LEWIS: The second is historical base rate convergence. Assume something like the growth rate or a margin moves toward the industry average or median over time, or toward GDP growth where that fits, using a base rate measured over a long, representative stretch. It suits established industries with many public competitors and few structural shocks, and young companies growing into something like their older rivals.

HEART: It fails in three places. New or fast changing industries have no reliable base rate. In cyclical industries, a smooth path toward an average understates how much results swing. And for a company that dominates its industry, the base rate is mostly the company itself, so the method quietly turns back into historical results.

LEWIS: The third is management guidance, the revenue and earnings targets managers share, often starting late in the current year for the year ahead and updated each quarter. Managers know things outsiders do not, but guidance usually comes as a range built on their own assumptions about GDP, pricing and costs, so ask whether those assumptions make sense today.

HEART: And here is the trap. Do not simply take the middle of the range. Managers sometimes set revenue ranges low and expense ranges high on purpose, so the results that follow look like a beat. Picture a hiking guide who always says the climb takes four hours, knowing most groups finish in three. Everyone feels fast at the top.

LEWIS: Guidance is most useful when management has a proven history, which you check with variance analysis of past budgets against actual results. It helps less for cyclical companies, since managers forecast the economy no better than analysts. But managers are better at forecasting what they control, such as their own expenses and investment in plant and equipment.

HEART: The fourth is the analyst's discretionary forecast, the catch all: surveys, models, probability distributions. It is the right tool for cyclical companies, companies with few or no peers, companies giving no guidance, and companies in a major transition. Picture the first company offering satellite internet to remote farms: no history, no peers and no base rate.

LEWIS: How far ahead? The forecast horizon depends on the investor's or portfolio manager's time horizon, on cyclicality, and on company specific factors. For a cyclical industry, it should at least reach the middle of a business cycle. For a company restructuring its operations, it should run long enough for the benefits to show up in the numbers.

HEART: Beyond the horizon, as Episode 21 covered, a terminal value stands in for every later year. The logic is that the company should look settled by then, at a normal, mid cycle level. That is why reaching mid cycle matters. End the horizon at a peak and you bake the peak into the terminal value.

LEWIS: Now revenue, the line almost everything depends on. A top down forecast starts with a big picture variable, usually expected nominal GDP growth or growth in the product's market. One version links sales to nominal GDP directly. Another version takes real GDP growth for the units sold and an inflation forecast for prices. Nominal, not real, because revenue in dollars includes rising prices.

HEART: Analysts often expect a company to grow faster or slower than GDP, depending on its life cycle stage and cyclicality. Listen to the wording. If a company should grow a tenth faster than nominal GDP, you scale GDP growth up by a tenth of itself. You do not add ten percentage points.

LEWIS: The other top down version is market growth and market share: forecast the industry's total sales, then multiply by the company's expected share. The two multiply, so a bigger slice of a bigger pie grows faster than either change suggests. And when a company holds a small slice, one extra share point is a big jump in its slice, so simply adding share points to market growth understates growth when share is rising.

HEART: A bottom up forecast starts inside the company or its reportable segments, including forecasts built from its own past growth or planned product launches. There are four common drivers. One, average selling price and volume, each forecast and then multiplied. Two, revenue by product line, region or segment, added up, if the company discloses that detail. Three, capacity based measures: forecast existing locations, then add newly opened ones, the way a gym chain grows members at its existing clubs and adds each new club on top.

LEWIS: Four, return or yield based measures: forecast balance sheet items and the return they earn, like an equipment leasing company's fleet on lease and its lease rates. A bank or credit union forecasts loan balances and loan yields for interest income, with deposits alongside, because deposits fund the loans and drive the interest it pays.

HEART: A hybrid approach blends both directions, and its main value is catching inconsistencies. If a retailer's store opening plan implies sales growth far out of line with what the economy and the market can support, recheck the model.

LEWIS: Nonrecurring items should not sit inside a forecast object. Analyze them on their own. Some are flagged by management, such as a gain on selling a building or a lump sum for ending a contract, and may sit on their own line so you can strip them out, if you trust management's judgment. The trap: charges called nonrecurring that show up year after year are not really nonrecurring, so build them into the forecast.

HEART: Others nobody labels, and they take judgment. After a hurricane, roofing contractors see a surge of work. A new normal, or a spike that fades once repairs are done? Treat a temporary surge as permanent and you overstate the forecast for years.

LEWIS: Whatever the approach, account for the big risk factors: competition, the business cycle, inflation or deflation, and technological change, judging which ones matter for this company. Scenario analysis is the natural tool.

HEART: Now costs. Cost of goods sold is closely tied to revenue, so it is usually forecast as a share of revenue, or equivalently, forecast the gross margin and cost of goods sold is the rest. Market share gives clues. A company bleeding share to cheaper alternatives will likely see its gross margin squeezed. One winning share with a new product nobody can match yet has room to widen it.

LEWIS: Then the subtle point from the bakery in Episode 21. If input costs rise and the company adds exactly the same dollar amount to its price, gross profit dollars hold steady, but the gross margin falls, because the same amount was added to both cost and revenue.

HEART: Cost of goods sold is usually a big share of costs, so a small miss there is a big miss in profit. For the near term, dig into the quantity and price of key inputs, like a trucking company's diesel bill. Many companies hedge those costs with forwards or other derivatives, so find out how much is hedged, or at least how the company has hedged before and how far ahead.

LEWIS: And the trap: a hedge cuts both ways. If diesel spikes, the hedged trucker's margin is protected. If diesel collapses, its margin cannot benefit, because it still pays the locked in price. As a final reasonableness check, compare your gross margin forecast with competitors' margins, and ask whether different business models explain any gap.

HEART: Selling, general and administrative expenses, S G and A, are less sensitive to volume. Their fixed part, such as research and development, the head office and executive salaries, is generally larger than the variable part, so it can be forecast with a steady growth rate that allows for inflation. Selling and distribution costs are the exception, moving with volume, because selling more takes more salespeople and more delivery trucks.

LEWIS: One practical limit. Companies rarely break out cost of goods sold or S G and A by segment. Usually the most you get is each segment's operating margin, so segment forecasts work at that level.

HEART: Working capital has three pieces, each forecast with a days measure. Receivables equal days of sales outstanding times a single day's revenue. Inventory equals days of inventory on hand times a single day's cost of goods sold. Payables equal days of payables outstanding times a single day's cost of goods sold. A day's figure is the annual amount divided by three hundred sixty five.

LEWIS: Notice the pairing. Receivables ride on revenue, because customers owe what you charged them. Inventory and payables ride on cost of goods sold, measured at what the goods cost you. And the direction. An auto parts wholesaler that gives repair shops an extra month to pay may win sales, but its days of sales outstanding rise, receivables grow, and more cash is tied up.

HEART: For capital investment, the cash flow statement shows assets bought and sold, and the income statement shows depreciation and amortization. Split spending in two. Maintenance spending keeps existing capacity running, and the usual starting point is historical depreciation adjusted upward for inflation, because depreciation reflects what the assets cost years ago, while replacing them costs today's prices. Depreciation and amortization themselves come from the net book value of property, plant and equipment and the assets' useful life.

LEWIS: Growth spending adds capacity, so forecasting it means understanding management's plans for the business and for revenue growth. A forecast that promises new stores without the spending to build them does not hang together.

HEART: Capital structure forecasts usually start from leverage ratios, such as debt to assets or debt to equity. Watch for borrowing needed to fund planned capital spending, and for any target capital structure management discloses, or debt covenants, the ratios its lenders require.

LEWIS: Last, scenario analysis. One number for net income hides how wrong it could be. So build several complete sets of assumptions about the economy, competition or technology, run each through the model, and see how far net income moves. What you hand over is a range. Picture a ski resort. A snowy winter, a normal one and a warm, dry one each move visitors, ticket prices and staffing together, so each scenario tells one consistent story.

HEART: It is the same idea a bank or credit union applies in capital planning, running a baseline and a severe downturn through the whole balance sheet to see whether capital holds up.

LEWIS: The pitfalls in one place. Carrying a cyclical peak forward. Converging a dominant company toward a base rate that is really itself. Trusting the midpoint of guidance. Calling recurring charges one time. Adding share points to market growth, or using real GDP alone for dollar revenue. Forgetting a hedge blocks good surprises too. Growth without the capital spending to deliver it. And a model so detailed it is only harder to maintain.

HEART: The takeaway. Match the object and the approach to the company, build revenue from the top down, the bottom up or both, keep one time items apart, and show a range, not a single figure.

## Recall check

LEWIS Q: A company has three business lines. One brings in about two thirds of revenue but only a small share of profit. Which line decides its industry label?

HEART: Answer. The line bringing in about two thirds of revenue. Here is why. The providers apply a revenue test first, and a line producing more than sixty percent of revenue sets the label. Only if no line clears that bar do they look for more than half of revenue, profits or assets, and only then fall back on judgment or the conglomerate label.

HEART Q: Two industries each have four companies. In one the shares are equal, in the other one company dominates. Which has the higher Herfindahl Hirschman index, and what does a reading above twenty five hundred mean?

LEWIS: Answer. The industry with the dominant company, and above twenty five hundred means high concentration. Here is why. The index squares each share before adding, which gives one large share far more weight than several small ones. So with the same number of companies, uneven shares score higher.

LEWIS Q: A company's market share jumped right after it bought a rival. Does that show customers prefer its products?

HEART: Answer. Not by itself, because you need the share gain net of the acquisition. Here is why. Buying a competitor adds that competitor's sales, so share rises automatically. The real test is whether the company gains share from the business it already had.

HEART Q: An industry runs huge, specialized plants that cannot be put to any other purpose, and shutting one down triggers big severance costs. Does that make rivalry more or less intense?

LEWIS: Answer. More intense. Here is why. Those are high exit barriers. When leaving is expensive, money losing companies stay and keep producing, so unused capacity lingers and prices stay under pressure.

LEWIS Q: A new kind of product comes from a startup rather than the established companies, and it creates value in a whole new way. What kind of innovation is it, and what must the incumbents eventually do?

HEART: Answer. Disruptive innovation, and the incumbents eventually have to join it. Here is why. Disruption creates a new market or a new way of delivering value, and it usually comes from newcomers. Incumbents can keep earning money while customers slowly adopt it, but to hold market share over the long run they must adopt it themselves, even though it cannibalizes their existing business.

HEART Q: Powerful customers keep pushing prices down. Why does a cost leader still earn a margin?

LEWIS: Answer. Because customers can only push prices down to the costs of the marginal producer. Here is why. The marginal producer is the least efficient company still needed to meet demand. If the price fell below its costs, it would leave and supply would fall short, so prices settle around its costs. The cost leader's costs sit below that level, and the gap is its margin.

LEWIS Q: Under what condition does a differentiation strategy pay off?

HEART: Answer. When the extra cost of being different is less than the price premium customers will pay, and that premium lasts. Here is why. Differentiation costs money in brand building, service, quality and innovation. If buyers will not pay more than that extra cost, the strategy destroys value. And if rivals can imitate the product quickly, the premium erodes and the advantage with it.

HEART Q: Why is base rate convergence a poor approach for a company that dominates its industry?

LEWIS: Answer. Because the base rate is mostly the company itself, so the method collapses into using its own history. Here is why. When one company makes up most of an industry's sales and profits, the industry's figures are largely that company's figures. Converging toward them adds almost no outside information, so you have in effect assumed the past continues.

LEWIS Q: Management guides revenue growth to a range. Why might the midpoint be a poor read of what management really expects?

HEART: Answer. Because managers sometimes shade revenue guidance low and expense guidance high. Here is why. A cautious range makes it easier to report results that beat expectations, so the midpoint may sit below what management truly expects for revenue. The analyst should test the assumptions behind the range and check how past guidance compared with actual results.

HEART Q: An analyst expects a company to grow a quarter faster than nominal GDP. Does she add twenty five percentage points to GDP growth, or scale GDP growth up by a quarter of itself?

LEWIS: Answer. Scale it up by a quarter of itself. Here is why. Faster here is relative to GDP growth, so the company's growth is GDP growth times one and a quarter. Adding twenty five points would assume a huge growth rate with no link to the economy.

## Key takeaways

HEART: First, a few memory hooks.

LEWIS: The five steps of industry analysis: define, survey, structure, surroundings, strategy. Define the industry, run the survey of size, growth, profits and shares, map its structure with the five forces, scan the surroundings with PESTLE, then judge each company's strategy.

HEART: For labels: sixty on revenue first, then more than half of revenue, profits or assets, then judgment or conglomerate. For the ladders: sector sits at the top of G I C S but on the third rung of I C B, and T R B C is the one with five tiers that also reaches private companies and governments.

LEWIS: For concentration: square the shares and add. Under fifteen hundred is loose, fifteen hundred to twenty five hundred is moderate, above twenty five hundred is tight. Squaring makes the giants count.

HEART: For the five forces: two threats, two powers and a rivalry. The threats come from new entrants and substitutes, the powers belong to customers and suppliers, and rivalry sits in the middle. Strong forces drive economic profit toward zero.

LEWIS: For barriers: entry walls keep rivals out, exit walls keep losers in. The first helps profits, the second hurts them.

HEART: For PESTLE: political, economic, social, technological, legal, environmental. It looks outward, changes slowly, and needs refreshing less often than the forces. Sustaining innovation makes a product better, disruptive innovation makes something different, and disruption usually comes from newcomers.

LEWIS: For strategy: cheapest, different or narrow. Cost leadership, differentiation or focus, and pick one. A differentiator's premium must beat its extra cost, and it must last.

HEART: For forecast objects: drivers, lines, summaries and ad hoc events not yet in the statements. For approaches: history for the steady, base rates for the crowded, guidance for a reliable management, and judgment for the cyclical, the one of a kind and the transforming.

LEWIS: For working capital: receivables ride on revenue, inventory and payables ride on cost of goods sold. For capital spending: maintenance starts at depreciation plus inflation, and growth follows management's plans. And hedges cut both ways.

HEART: Now, how you would say the big ideas to a senior leader.

LEWIS: On industry: where a company competes sets the ceiling on what it can earn, and competition pulls everyone toward the industry's normal level of profit over time. The jargon is the industry base rate of profitability and economic profit.

HEART: On competition: profits leak to customers, suppliers, newcomers and substitutes, and they leak fastest in crowded industries with idle capacity and no easy way out. The jargon is Porter's five forces, barriers to entry and barriers to exit.

LEWIS: On strategy: a company wins by being the cheapest, by being different enough that customers pay more, or by owning a niche, and the proof is a return above the cost of capital, year after year. The jargon is cost leadership, differentiation and focus.

HEART: On forecasting: build revenue from both the market and the company's own plans, and if the two disagree, find out why before trusting either one. The jargon is top down, bottom up and hybrid forecasting.

LEWIS: And on uncertainty: never hand over one number. Show a base case, a good case and a bad case, each telling a consistent story. The jargon is scenario analysis.
