## The big picture

Every valuation question on the exam comes down to one comparison. You estimate what a share is worth, its intrinsic value, and you compare that estimate with the market price. If your value is above the price, the share looks undervalued. If your value is below the price, it looks overvalued. If the two are close, it looks fairly valued.

Two cautions apply. Your estimate is only as good as your model and inputs, and a large gap may mean the market knows something you have missed. So the more confident you are in the model and the inputs, the more willing you should be to act on a gap.

Analysts use three families of models. Present value models, also called discounted cash flow models, value a share as the present value of the cash it will deliver, either dividends or free cash flow to equity. Multiplier models, also called market multiple models, compare the share price, or the value of the whole firm, with a measure such as earnings, book value, sales or cash flow. Asset based models value equity as the fair value of the assets minus the fair value of the liabilities.

Module 46.1 covers what shareholders receive: cash dividends, buybacks, and the stock dividends and splits that look like payouts but are not. Module 46.2 values dividends with discount models. Module 46.3 turns to multiples and links them back to the Gordon model, and the episode closes with asset based valuation. Expect formula questions with round numbers, and questions that ask which model or multiple best fits a given company.

## Module 46.1, Dividends, Splits, and Repurchases

Start with what a dividend really is. A cash dividend moves value from the company to its owners. It does not create value. The moment the cash leaves, the company is worth less by roughly that amount, and the shareholder holds the cash instead.

Cash dividends come in three forms. Regular dividends are paid on a set schedule, quarterly in the United States and often twice a year or once a year elsewhere. Companies work hard to keep them steady or rising, because a cut is read as bad news. Special dividends, also called extra or irregular dividends, are one time payments, often after an unusually strong year, and they are common at cyclical companies. A liquidating dividend is a return of capital rather than a share of profits. It is paid when a company goes out of business, sells part of its business and passes on the proceeds, or pays out more than its accumulated retained earnings.

Know the balance sheet effect of a cash dividend. Cash falls, so total assets fall. Retained earnings fall, so shareholders' equity falls by the same amount. Less cash means weaker liquidity ratios, such as the cash ratio and the current ratio. Lower assets and lower equity, with debt unchanged, mean higher leverage ratios, such as debt to assets and debt to equity.

Now the actions that change the number of shares but not the value of the company. A stock dividend pays shareholders in new shares instead of cash. With a 10 percent stock dividend, you get one new share for every 10 you own. No cash leaves the company, so its total value does not change. Every holder gets the same percentage increase, so everyone keeps the same fraction of the company. The same pie is simply cut into more slices.

Walk through it. You own 100 shares at 55 dollars, worth 5,500 dollars. After a 10 percent stock dividend you own 110 shares. The company's total value is unchanged, so the price falls to 50 dollars, and 110 shares at 50 dollars is still 5,500 dollars. In the accounts, a stock dividend moves an amount from retained earnings to contributed capital. Total equity does not change.

A stock split has the same economics. In a 2 for 1 split, each share becomes 2 shares, and the price should halve. In a 3 for 2 split, every 2 shares become 3, so the price falls to two thirds, and a 60 dollar share becomes a 40 dollar share. A 2 for 1 split is economically the same as a 100 percent stock dividend. Companies usually split after a large price rise, to bring the price back into a range that looks more affordable. Price per share and earnings per share fall in the same proportion, so the price to earnings ratio does not change.

A reverse split does the opposite. In a 1 for 10 reverse split, every 10 shares become 1, so a 2 dollar share should trade at about 20 dollars. Companies whose prices have fallen a long way use reverse splits, for example to meet an exchange's minimum price.

Here is the trap. A split or a stock dividend does not make shareholders richer or change their share of ownership. Total market value, proportional ownership and total equity all stay the same. Only the share count and the per share numbers change.

Now share repurchases, also called buybacks. The company uses cash to buy back its own shares, usually in the open market. Repurchased shares are either canceled or held as treasury shares. Treasury shares are not counted as outstanding, they receive no dividends and they carry no votes. On the balance sheet, a buyback has the same effect as a cash dividend of the same size. Cash falls and equity falls.

The key idea is that a buyback at the market price and a cash dividend of the same total amount leave shareholders equally well off, ignoring taxes and signals. Take a company with 10 million shares at 50 dollars, so it is worth 500 million dollars, and it has 50 million dollars of cash to return. Option one is a 5 dollar dividend per share. The company is then worth 450 million dollars, so the share falls to 45 dollars. Each holder has 45 dollars of stock plus 5 dollars of cash, 50 dollars in total.

Option two is to buy back 1 million shares at 50 dollars each. The company is again worth 450 million dollars, but now there are only 9 million shares, so the price stays at 50 dollars. A holder who did not sell still has 50 dollars per share. The wealth is the same either way.

Four reasons can favor a buyback. First, signaling, because management may believe the shares are undervalued. Second, flexibility, because a buyback is not a commitment, while cutting a regular dividend is painful. Third, taxes, in places where capital gains are taxed less than dividends, or where the tax is deferred until the shares are sold. Fourth, offsetting dilution from shares issued when employees exercise stock options. A buyback can also raise earnings per share simply because there are fewer shares, but a higher EPS on its own does not make shareholders richer.

Last, the dividend payment chronology. There are four dates, in this order. The declaration date is when the board announces the dividend, its amount, the record date and the payment date. The ex dividend date is the first day the share trades without the right to the coming dividend. The holder of record date is the day the company checks its books to see who will be paid. The payment date is when the cash is sent.

The ex dividend date is the one that matters to an investor. Buy before it and you receive the dividend. Buy on or after it and the seller keeps it. That is why the price tends to drop by about the amount of the dividend on the ex date.

The gap between the ex date and the record date comes from settlement, because a trade must settle by the record date for the buyer to appear on the books. When trades take two business days to settle, the ex date is one business day before the record date. With one day settlement, now the standard in the United States, the two dates fall on the same day. If a question gives you a settlement period, work back from the record date.

The rule to keep: stock dividends and splits change the number of slices, not the size of the pie. And the ex dividend date decides who gets paid.

## Module 46.2, Dividend Discount Models

First principles. A share is worth the present value of the cash it will pay its owner. If you hold it forever, the only cash you ever receive is dividends. If you plan to sell, you receive dividends plus a sale price, but the buyer will pay only for the dividends that come after. Either way, the value of a share is the present value of all its expected future dividends, discounted at the required return on equity. That is the dividend discount model, or DDM.

The discount rate is the return investors require for bearing the risk of the share. A common way to estimate it is CAPM, which gives the risk free rate plus beta times the equity risk premium.

Start with one period. The value today equals next year's expected dividend plus the expected price at the end of the year, all divided by one plus the required return. Say you expect a 2 dollar dividend and a 42 dollar price in one year, and you require 10 percent. 2 plus 42 is 44, and 44 divided by 1.10 is 40 dollars. With more periods, discount each dividend and the final sale price for their own number of years.

Now the workhorse, the Gordon growth model, also called the constant growth model. If dividends grow at a constant rate forever, the infinite sum collapses into one short formula. The value today equals next year's dividend, divided by the required return minus the growth rate. Next year's dividend equals the dividend just paid times one plus the growth rate.

Here is an example. A company just paid 2 dollars. Dividends grow at 5 percent a year, and you require 10 percent. Next year's dividend is 2 times 1.05, which is 2.10. The required return minus growth is 0.05. And 2.10 divided by 0.05 is 42 dollars.

Three traps live here. The first is using the dividend just paid. Dividing 2 dollars by 0.05 gives 40 dollars, a classic wrong answer. Read the tense carefully. A dividend just paid, or the most recent dividend, must be grown one year. A dividend expected next year is used as it is.

The second trap is the model's conditions. The required return must be greater than the growth rate, and growth must be constant forever. If growth is at or above the required return, the formula breaks down, so a fast grower needs a multistage model. The Gordon model fits mature companies with stable dividend policies, growing at or below the long run growth rate of the economy. Utilities are the classic case.

The third trap is sensitivity. Keep the same company, but lower the required return to 9 percent. The denominator falls from 0.05 to 0.04, and the value rises from 42 dollars to 52.50 dollars. A one percentage point change in the required return moved the value by 25 percent. Equity behaves like a very long duration asset, for the same reason that long term fixed rate loans on a bank balance sheet swing in value when rates move.

You can rearrange the model. The required return equals the dividend yield, which is next year's dividend divided by the price, plus the growth rate. And the growth rate the market implies equals the required return minus the dividend yield. At a 42 dollar price, the dividend yield is 2.10 divided by 42, which is 5 percent. Add 5 percent growth and you get back the 10 percent required return.

Where does growth come from? The sustainable growth rate equals the retention rate times return on equity, or ROE. The retention rate, often called b, is the share of earnings kept in the business, which is one minus the dividend payout ratio. Say ROE is 15 percent and the company pays out 40 percent of its earnings. It retains 60 percent, and 0.60 times 15 percent is 9 percent growth. Profits plowed back into the business at the company's ROE are what make dividends grow.

Preferred stock is the easiest case. A preferred share with no maturity, no call feature and no conversion right pays a fixed dividend forever. That is the Gordon model with zero growth. The value equals the preferred dividend divided by the required return on the preferred.

A preferred share with a 100 dollar par value and a 5 percent dividend rate pays 5 dollars a year. If investors require 8 percent, the value is 5 divided by 0.08, which is 62.50 dollars. When the required return is above the dividend rate, the preferred is worth less than par, just as with a bond. A preferred share with a maturity date is valued like a bond.

Now multistage models. Many companies grow quickly for a few years and then settle down. So you split the future into two pieces. First, discount each dividend in the high growth period one at a time. Second, at the end of the high growth period, use the Gordon model to find a terminal value, which is the value of all dividends after that point. Discount the terminal value back to today, and add the pieces.

Here is an example built to keep the arithmetic clean. A company just paid 1 dollar. Dividends will grow 10 percent a year for two years, then 5 percent forever. The required return is 10 percent. The year one dividend is 1.10, and the year two dividend is 1.21. Discounted at 10 percent, each is worth exactly 1 dollar today, so the high growth period is worth 2 dollars.

Now the terminal value at the end of year two. It uses the year three dividend, which is 1.21 times 1.05, about 1.27. Divide by the required return minus the long term growth rate, which is 0.05, and the value at the end of year two is 25.41 dollars. Discount it back two years, which means dividing by 1.21, and you get 21 dollars. Add the 2 dollars from the high growth years. The share is worth 23 dollars.

Two traps sit in that calculation. The terminal value at the end of year n uses the dividend for year n plus 1. And it is a value as of year n, so you discount it n years, not n plus 1. In this example, discounting the terminal value three years gives a total of about 21.09 dollars instead of 23. Building the terminal value from the year two dividend instead gives 22 dollars.

When does a dividend discount model fit? It works best when the company pays dividends, when its dividend policy has a clear and consistent link to its earnings, and when you are valuing a minority stake, which gives you no say over the payout. If a company pays no dividend, or pays far less than it could afford, or if you are valuing a controlling stake, use free cash flow to equity, or FCFE, instead. FCFE is the cash left for common shareholders after operating needs, capital spending and debt flows. It equals cash flow from operations, minus fixed capital investment, plus net borrowing, which is new borrowing minus debt repaid. The value of equity is the present value of expected FCFE, discounted at the required return on equity, because these are cash flows to equity holders alone.

The rule to keep: the Gordon model value equals next year's dividend divided by the required return minus the growth rate. It works only when the required return is above growth, and next year's dividend is the one just paid, times one plus the growth rate.

## Module 46.3, Relative Valuation Measures

Multiples answer a different question. Instead of asking what the cash flows are worth, they ask what the market pays for similar assets. A price multiple divides the share price by a per share measure, such as earnings, book value, sales or cash flow. An enterprise value multiple divides the value of the whole firm by a measure such as EBITDA.

There are two ways to use a multiple. The first is the method of comparables. You compare a company's multiple with a benchmark, such as the average or median of its peers, its industry, or its own history. The logic is the law of one price: similar assets should sell at similar prices. The second is the method based on fundamentals. You ask what multiple the company deserves, given its expected cash flows, and that leads straight back to the Gordon model.

Divide both sides of the Gordon model by next year's earnings. The justified leading price to earnings ratio equals the dividend payout ratio divided by the required return minus the growth rate. Leading means it is based on next year's expected earnings. A trailing ratio uses the last 12 months of earnings, so its justified value is the leading value times one plus the growth rate.

Here is an example. The payout ratio is 40 percent, the required return is 10 percent, and growth is 6 percent. 0.40 divided by 0.04 gives a justified leading price to earnings ratio of 10. The justified trailing ratio is 10 times 1.06, which is 10.6. If the stock trades at 14 times next year's earnings, it looks overvalued on fundamentals.

Now the directions. The justified ratio rises when expected growth rises, and falls when the required return rises. The payout ratio is the trap. In the formula, a higher payout raises the multiple only if growth stays the same. But paying out more means retaining less, and since growth equals retention times ROE, growth falls. So the net effect of a higher payout is ambiguous unless the question tells you to hold growth constant.

Next comes the comparables trap. Relative value is not absolute value. A stock trading at 12 times earnings while its peers trade at 18 is cheap relative to its peers. But if the whole industry is overpriced, the cheap stock may still be overvalued in absolute terms. Also check whether a low multiple is simply explained by weaker growth or higher risk.

Now each price multiple, with its strengths and its weaknesses. Price to earnings is the most widely used, because earnings drive value. Its weaknesses are that it is meaningless when earnings are zero or negative, that earnings can include one time items that will not recur, and that management's accounting choices can shift earnings. Analysts often strip out the items that will not recur, and for cyclical firms they use normalized earnings, an estimate of earnings at the middle of the business cycle.

Price to book compares the price with book value per share. Book value per share is common shareholders' equity divided by shares outstanding, and common equity is total assets minus total liabilities minus preferred stock. Book value is usually positive even when earnings are negative, and it is more stable than earnings. So price to book works for loss making firms, and for companies whose assets are mostly liquid financial assets. That is why price to book is a standard yardstick for banks, where book value is a meaningful measure of what shareholders own.

Its weaknesses are that it ignores intangible assets that never reach the balance sheet, such as human capital, and that book values can drift from market values because of inflation, technology change and accounting choices. Analysts often adjust book value, for example by subtracting goodwill to get tangible book value, or by restating assets at fair value.

Price to sales uses revenue, which is positive even for struggling companies and is harder to manipulate than earnings. Sales are also more stable than earnings, so price to sales suits mature companies, cyclical companies and companies with losses. Its weaknesses are that sales say nothing about costs or profit, so two firms with the same sales but very different margins look alike. Revenue recognition choices can also distort it.

Price to cash flow uses cash flow, which is harder for management to manipulate than earnings and is more stable. It needs a definition of cash flow. The simplest is earnings plus noncash charges, such as depreciation and amortization. Others are cash flow from operations, FCFE and EBITDA, though EBITDA fits enterprise value better. Its weaknesses are that the simple definition ignores working capital and capital spending, that FCFE can be volatile or negative, and that management can still shift operating cash flow, for example by delaying payments to suppliers.

Now enterprise value. Enterprise value is the value of the whole firm to all of its capital providers. It equals the market value of common equity, plus the market value of preferred stock, plus the market value of debt, minus cash and short term investments. You subtract the cash because a buyer of the whole company would get that cash along with it.

Here is an example. Equity is worth 800 million dollars, debt is worth 300 million, and cash is 100 million, with no preferred stock. Enterprise value is 800 plus 300 minus 100, which is 1,000 million dollars, or 1 billion. If EBITDA is 125 million dollars, enterprise value to EBITDA is 8 times. The trap is adding the cash instead of subtracting it, which gives 1.2 billion and a multiple of 9.6.

Why pair enterprise value with EBITDA? EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, so it is measured before anything is paid to lenders. It is a flow to all capital providers, which matches enterprise value. That makes enterprise value to EBITDA useful for comparing companies with very different leverage, and for capital intensive companies with heavy depreciation. EBITDA is also usually positive when net income is negative. The weaknesses are that EBITDA ignores the capital spending and working capital a business needs, so it can overstate the cash truly available, and that the market value of debt can be hard to find.

Here is the matching rule, a favorite trap. Earnings after interest belong to shareholders, so they pair with price. EBITDA and EBIT come before interest, so they belong to all capital providers and pair with enterprise value. Price to EBITDA, or enterprise value to net income, is a mismatch. Price to sales is the common exception, used widely even though sales come before interest.

Last, asset based valuation. Common equity value equals the fair value of the company's assets minus the fair value of its liabilities and any preferred stock. It works best when the assets are mostly tangible and easy to value at market prices, as with financial firms, natural resource companies and firms being liquidated. It works poorly when much of the value is intangible, such as a software platform or a brand, because those assets are hard to value and often missing from the balance sheet. It also struggles during high inflation, when recorded figures drift far from current values.

Comparing the three families, present value models rest on sound theory but are very sensitive to their inputs. Multiples are simple and tied to market prices, but they give only relative value and fail when the denominator is negative. Asset based models suit firms with tangible assets but miss intangible value.

The rule to keep: the justified leading price to earnings ratio equals the payout ratio divided by the required return minus growth. And enterprise value equals equity plus preferred stock plus debt, minus cash.

## Recall check

Q: You own 2 percent of a company's shares before it pays a 10 percent stock dividend. What fraction of the company do you own afterward, and what happens to the total value of your holding?

Answer: You still own 2 percent, and the total value of your holding is unchanged. Every holder receives the same percentage of new shares, and no cash leaves the company. The share price falls so that the larger number of shares has the same total value.

Q: A stock goes ex dividend on a Wednesday, and an investor buys the shares that Wednesday morning. Does she receive the upcoming dividend?

Answer: No. The ex dividend date is the first day the share trades without the right to the coming dividend, so the seller keeps it. To receive the dividend, she needed to buy on Tuesday or earlier.

Q: A company just paid a dividend of 4 dollars. Dividends are expected to grow at 5 percent forever, and the required return is 10 percent. What is the share worth?

Answer: 84 dollars. Next year's dividend is 4 times 1.05, which is 4.20, and 4.20 divided by 0.05 is 84. Dividing the 4 dollars already paid by 0.05 gives 80, the classic wrong answer.

Q: A profitable company has never paid a dividend, and an investor is considering a controlling stake. Which approach is most likely appropriate: a dividend discount model, the Gordon growth model, or a free cash flow to equity model?

Answer: A free cash flow to equity model. With no dividends, a dividend model has nothing to discount, and the Gordon model is itself a dividend model. A controlling owner can also set the payout, so the cash the company could pay, its FCFE, is the better basis for value.

Q: You are comparing two firms in the same industry, one with no debt and one with heavy debt. Which multiple most likely gives the fairer comparison: price to earnings, or enterprise value to EBITDA?

Answer: Enterprise value to EBITDA. EBITDA is measured before interest, and enterprise value includes both debt and equity, so the multiple is not distorted by how each firm is financed. Earnings come after interest, so leverage affects price to earnings directly.

Q: A company pays out 60 percent of its earnings, its required return is 10 percent, and its dividends are expected to grow at 7 percent. What is its justified leading price to earnings ratio?

Answer: 20. Divide the payout ratio, 0.60, by the required return minus growth, which is 0.03. The trailing version would be 20 times 1.07, or 21.4.

## Recap

Cash dividends and buybacks both lower cash and equity and raise leverage ratios, and at the market price they leave shareholders equally wealthy, before taxes and signals. Stock dividends, splits and reverse splits change the share count and the price per share, but not total value, ownership fractions or total equity. The dates run declaration, ex dividend, holder of record, payment, and a buyer on or after the ex dividend date does not receive the dividend.

The Gordon model value equals next year's dividend divided by the required return minus growth, it needs the required return above growth, and it is very sensitive to that gap. Sustainable growth equals the retention rate times ROE. In a multistage model, the terminal value at year n uses the year n plus 1 dividend and is discounted n years. The justified leading price to earnings ratio equals the payout ratio divided by the required return minus growth. Enterprise value equals equity plus preferred plus debt, minus cash, and it pairs with EBITDA, while price pairs with earnings.
