LEWIS: Reading 47 covers what a bond is, its basic features, and the indenture and covenants that protect lenders. Reading 48 covers how bond cash flows are shaped, the options that can be built into a bond, and the legal, regulatory and tax rules that differ across markets, and Reading 49 covers how the bond market is divided, how bond indexes work, and how new bonds are sold and existing ones traded.

## Module 47.1, Fixed-Income Instrument Features

HEART: Start with the difference between a loan and a bond. A loan is a private agreement between a borrower and a lender, and it is not built to be traded. A member's car loan on a credit union's books is a loan. A bond is a standardized, tradable security. Many investors each hold a slice of the same debt, and any of them can sell that slice to someone else. A Treasury note in that credit union's investment portfolio is a bond.

LEWIS: Either way, the investor is lending money. The amount lent and promised back is the principal, also called the par value or face value. In return the issuer promises interest, usually as a regular coupon stated as a percentage of par. Companies typically borrow this way to pay for long lived investments, such as a new factory, and these loans and bonds usually sit among the company's long term liabilities.

HEART: Every bond can be described by six questions. Who owes the money? When is it due? How much? What does it pay along the way? Where does it stand in line? And what if something happens? Those map to the issuer, the maturity, the par value, the coupon rate and frequency, the seniority, and the contingency provisions.

LEWIS: Start with who. The biggest issuers are national governments, called sovereigns, and companies. Then come local governments, such as states and cities. Supranational organizations, owned by several governments together, such as the World Bank. Quasi government entities, which a government sponsors but does not run directly. The Federal Home Loan Banks are a United States example. They make secured loans, called advances, to member banks and credit unions, and they fund those advances by selling bonds and short term discount notes that carry no explicit government guarantee. And special purpose entities, companies set up only to buy a pool of financial assets, such as auto loans, and issue bonds paid from those assets. Those bonds are asset backed securities.

HEART: Next, when. The maturity date is the date of the final payment, and once the bond is out in the market, the time left until that date is called the tenor. Maturity also sorts bonds into two markets. A bond issued with an original maturity of a year or less is a money market security. Anything issued for longer is a capital market security. And bonds with no maturity date at all are perpetual bonds.

LEWIS: Here is the trap. The label comes from the original maturity, not the time left. A ten year note issued nine years ago has a tenor of about a year, but it is still a capital market security.

HEART: Third, how much. The par value is the principal the issuer will repay. Usually it comes back in one piece at maturity, but some debt repays it gradually, the way a mortgage does.

LEWIS: Fourth, what it pays along the way. The coupon rate is the annual interest as a percentage of par. The frequency is how often it is paid: once a year, twice a year, quarterly or monthly. A bond that pays twice a year sends half of the annual coupon every six months.

HEART: Not every coupon is fixed. A floating rate note, or floater, pays a variable market rate, called the market reference rate, plus a fixed margin. That margin is quoted in basis points, and a basis point is one hundredth of one percent.

LEWIS: And some bonds pay no coupon at all. A zero coupon bond, also called a pure discount bond, is sold below par and pays par at maturity. All the interest arrives at the end, as the gap between what you paid and what you get back. A Treasury bill works exactly this way.

HEART: Fifth, where it stands in line. If an issuer goes bankrupt or is liquidated, lenders are paid before shareholders, so debt is senior to equity. But debt is not all equal. Senior debt is paid before junior debt, also called subordinated debt. Junior bondholders face a bigger chance of losing money, so they demand a higher yield.

LEWIS: Sixth, what if. Contingency provisions are rights to take some action if a future event happens. Most are embedded options: the issuer's right to call the bond back early, the investor's right to sell it back, or the right to convert it into shares. They get a full treatment in the next module.

HEART: Now yield. Given a bond's price and the cash flows you expect from it, the yield is the return you would earn by buying it. For a bond with a fixed coupon, price and yield move in opposite directions. The promised payments never change, so the price is the only lever left. Pay less for the same stream of payments and your return goes up.

LEWIS: Hold on to that seesaw, because every later fixed income episode leans on it. For a credit union holding fixed rate bonds, rising market yields mean falling market values.

HEART: Plot one issuer's yields against maturity and you get a yield curve. When longer maturities yield more, the curve slopes upward, and that is called a normal yield curve, because it is the most common shape. Lenders want extra return for the extra uncertainty of tying money up longer. When shorter maturities yield more, the curve slopes downward, and that less common shape is an inverted yield curve.

LEWIS: Government bonds are usually the lowest credit risk in their market, because a government can tax to pay its debts. So the government curve serves as the benchmark. The extra yield a riskier borrower, such as a company, pays over a government bond of the same maturity is called the spread. A wider spread means investors see more risk.

HEART: Now the contract behind the bond. The indenture is the legal agreement between the issuer and the bondholders. It sets out what the issuer owes and what it may not do, and it governs every dealing between the two sides. It names the exact legal entity that is borrowing, the source of the money for repayment, any assets pledged as collateral, any credit enhancements, and the covenants.

LEWIS: The source of repayment depends on who is borrowing. A national government pays its lenders out of tax revenue, and if it borrows in its own currency, it can in some cases print the money. That is why its bonds are usually treated as the lowest credit risk in their region. A local government repays from local taxes or from the revenue of something it runs, such as a water utility or an airport.

HEART: A company repays from its operating cash flow. A secured bond goes further. If the company defaults, its holders can go after particular assets set aside for them, called collateral, through a legal claim known as a lien or a pledge. An unsecured bond has no assets set aside. Its holders rely on the company's cash flow and a general claim on everything it owns. Compare a car loan, where the lender can take the car, with a credit card balance, where the lender has only your promise. Security protects the investor, so a secured bond can usually be sold at a lower yield than an unsecured bond from the same issuer.

LEWIS: And for an asset backed security, the money comes from the pool of financial assets held by the special purpose entity, such as the monthly payments on those auto loans.

HEART: Now covenants. Bondholders do not get to vote the way shareholders do. Their protection is written into the indenture as covenants, legally binding promises by the issuer. Affirmative covenants say what the issuer must do. Negative covenants say what the issuer must not do.

LEWIS: Affirmative covenants are requirements. Deliver financial reports on time. Spend the money raised on what the indenture says it is for. And give bondholders the right to be repaid at a premium to par if the issuer is taken over in a merger or acquisition.

HEART: Two more affirmative clauses are classic exam material. A cross default clause says that if the issuer defaults on any other debt, it is also in default on this bond, so bondholders need not wait while the issuer misses payments elsewhere. A pari passu clause, Latin for on equal footing, says this bond ranks equally with the issuer's other senior debt.

LEWIS: Negative covenants are restrictions. The issuer may not sell key assets, or sell them and lease them back. It may not pledge the same collateral to several debt issues at once. It may not issue new debt that ranks ahead of these bonds, a promise called a negative pledge clause. And it may face limits on extra borrowing, share buybacks and dividends.

HEART: Those last limits often come as an incurrence test. The action is allowed only if a financial ratio stays inside a threshold. For example, the company may borrow more only if its earnings would still cover its interest bill a set number of times over. The test only bites when the company tries to take the restricted action.

LEWIS: Negative covenants stop shareholders from shifting risk onto lenders after the money is lent, for example by paying out a huge dividend or piling on new debt. But covenants that are too tight can stop a company from seizing an opportunity or adapting to change. Stronger protection usually lets the issuer borrow at a lower yield, at the cost of flexibility.

HEART: Here is the trap. Do not sort covenants by whether they protect bondholders, because they all do. Sort them by the verb. A cross default clause protects bondholders, but it bans nothing, so it sits with the affirmative covenants. A negative pledge also protects bondholders, but it forbids an action, so it is negative.

LEWIS: The takeaway. A bond is a tradable loan defined by its issuer, maturity, par value, coupon, seniority and contingency provisions. The indenture is its contract, and covenants are either things the issuer must do or things it must not do.

## Module 48.1, Fixed-Income Cash Flows and Types

HEART: This module asks how the money comes back to the investor, and who can change those payments along the way. Start with principal. The standard shape is the bullet. The bond pays only interest along the way, and then the whole principal comes back in a single payment at maturity, together with the last coupon.

LEWIS: The opposite shape is the amortizing bond. Each payment covers that period's interest plus a slice of principal. If it is fully amortizing, the balance reaches zero with the last payment. Car loans and home mortgages work this way, with the same payment every period. Each payment carries a little less interest and a little more principal than the one before, because the balance that interest is charged on keeps shrinking. On a thirty year mortgage at typical rates, the early payments are mostly interest.

HEART: In between sits the partially amortizing bond. It pays down some principal along the way and leaves the rest for a final lump sum, called a balloon payment. Many commercial real estate loans at banks look like this. The payment is set as if the loan ran for many years, but the loan comes due much sooner, with a balloon for whatever is still owed.

LEWIS: Here is the trap. The balloon is only the principal left unpaid at the end. A bullet's final payment includes the full principal, so a balloon is smaller.

HEART: Why does the shape matter? Credit risk. With a bullet, the full amount is owed until the last day. With amortization, the amount at risk falls with every payment. The flip side is reinvestment risk. Principal that comes back early has to be put to work again at whatever rates are on offer, which hurts if rates have fallen. On the funding side, the same logic means a bullet borrowing leaves the whole balance to be refinanced on a single date.

LEWIS: A sinking fund is a cousin of amortization. The indenture requires the issuer to retire a portion of the issue on a schedule over its life, and the trustee picks which bonds get redeemed at random. That is good for credit, because less principal is left to repay at maturity. But it carries reinvestment risk. If rates have fallen, the holders whose bonds are picked must reinvest at lower yields.

HEART: Securitized bonds follow a schedule called a waterfall. An asset backed or mortgage backed deal is split into classes of different seniority, called tranches. A common rule is that junior tranches receive no principal until the senior tranches are fully repaid, while interest still flows to every tranche. Picture a tower of champagne glasses. Principal fills the top glass first, and only then spills down to the next level.

LEWIS: Now coupons. The floating rate note pays the market reference rate plus a fixed margin, which reflects the issuer's credit risk and is often called the credit spread. Most floaters pay quarterly and are keyed to a ninety day reference rate.

HEART: When market rates rise, the coupon rises with them, and when rates fall, the issuer's cost falls. A home equity line of credit priced at the prime rate plus a margin is the everyday version. Because the coupon keeps resetting, a floater's price tends to stay near par at each reset, as long as the issuer's credit holds steady, which suits a lender whose deposit costs move with rates.

LEWIS: The risk that does not reset is credit. The margin is fixed at issue, so if the issuer weakens, the old margin is too thin and the price falls below par.

HEART: Some floaters add limits. A cap is a ceiling on the coupon, which protects the issuer when rates soar. A floor is a minimum coupon, which protects the investor when rates collapse. So a cap makes the floater worth less to investors, and a floor makes it worth more.

LEWIS: A step up coupon bond has a coupon that rises over time on a schedule set at issue. That gives investors some protection against rising rates, because their coupon climbs too.

HEART: Coupons can also change when an event happens. Leveraged loans, which are loans to borrowers of lower credit quality, often carry a margin that rises if the borrower's debt climbs past a set multiple of its earnings. A credit linked coupon bond works off the rating. Its coupon rises if the issuer's credit rating falls, and falls if the rating improves. You may also see this called a credit linked note, but in the market that name usually means a note whose repayment depends on the credit of some other borrower. That compensates the investor, but notice the timing for the issuer. Its borrowing cost rises exactly when it is weakest.

LEWIS: A payment in kind bond lets the issuer skip the cash and pay the coupon by adding it to the amount owed, so the debt grows each period instead. The companies that issue them are usually already carrying a lot of debt and expect to be short of cash, which is exactly why investors demand higher yields.

HEART: A coupon can also step up if the issuer misses a stated environmental target by a set date. You may see these called green bonds. Strictly, though, a green bond is defined by what the money pays for, such as solar farms or cleaner buses, while a bond whose coupon steps up on a missed target is a sustainability linked bond.

LEWIS: Index linked bonds tie their payments to a published index. The most common are inflation linked bonds, sometimes called linkers, tied to an inflation index such as the CPI, to protect the purchasing power of what the investor is promised.

HEART: There are two main designs. In an interest indexed bond, the coupon rate is adjusted for inflation, but the principal stays the same. So the coupons keep pace with prices, but the principal does not.

LEWIS: In a capital indexed bond, the coupon rate stays fixed, but the principal is adjusted up with inflation, or down with deflation. The fixed rate is applied to that adjusted principal, so the coupon payment grows as prices rise. This is the most common design, and U.S. Treasury Inflation Protected Securities, known as TIPS, are built this way.

HEART: The way to hear it: the principal moves with prices, and the coupon rate is a real rate. That is why the coupon rate on TIPS looks low next to an ordinary Treasury. It is a rate on top of inflation, not one that includes it. And if prices fall, TIPS investors still receive at maturity the greater of the adjusted principal or the original par. Deflation can trim the coupons, but it cannot push the final repayment below par.

LEWIS: Two more shapes. The zero coupon bond pays a single amount, par, at maturity. With no coupons to reinvest, zeros suit investors who want to minimize reinvestment risk, and since par is the only payment, a zero has to trade below par to offer any return. A deferred coupon bond pays nothing for a set period after issue, and then regular coupons begin. It suits issuers with weak credit, or issuers building something, like a new mine, that will earn nothing for a few years. A zero is the extreme case of deferral, and both often trade below par.

HEART: Now the contingency provisions, and the most important question in this module. Who owns the option? An embedded option is a right written into the bond contract itself, not a separate security. A bond with no such rights is a straight bond, or an option free bond.

LEWIS: The rule is simple. An option is worth something to whoever holds it. If the issuer holds it, the bond is worth less to investors, so they pay a lower price and demand a higher yield. If the investor holds it, the bond is worth more, so investors pay a higher price and accept a lower yield.

HEART: Start with the callable bond. The issuer has the right, but not the obligation, to buy back all or part of the issue before maturity at a set price, the call price. Most callable bonds come with call protection, a stretch after issue when the bond cannot be called. The first date the issuer may call is the first call date, and a call schedule often sets a call price a little above par that steps down toward par as the bond ages.

LEWIS: Why call? To refinance more cheaply. If the yield the market demands on the issuer's debt has fallen, the issuer can redeem the old bonds at the call price and borrow again at the lower yield. That yield can fall for two reasons. Interest rates in general may have dropped, or the issuer's own credit may have improved.

HEART: For the investor, that is call risk. You never know for sure when the bond will end, and you get your money back exactly when yields are low and reinvesting is least attractive. There is a ceiling too. Once the bond can be called, its price struggles to rise much above the call price, because no buyer wants to pay well over the price at which it could be taken away.

LEWIS: So investors demand a higher yield and pay a lower price than for an identical straight bond. In words, the callable bond is worth the straight bond minus the value of the call option.

HEART: This is daily life in bank and credit union portfolios. Callable agency bonds pay more than bullet agency bonds of the same maturity, and that extra yield is payment for selling the issuer a call. Every fixed rate mortgage a credit union makes carries the same kind of option, because the member can pay it off early when rates fall.

LEWIS: The putable bond is the mirror image. The investor has the right to sell the bond back to the issuer at a set price, usually par. Investors exercise that right when the market price has fallen below the put price, because interest rates have risen or the issuer's credit has weakened.

HEART: So the put is a floor under the investor's price. Since the option belongs to the investor, a putable bond sells at a higher price and a lower yield than a straight bond. It is worth the straight bond plus the value of the put.

LEWIS: Now the convertible bond. The investor has the right to exchange the bond for a set number of the issuer's common shares, which gives the investor a share of the upside if the stock does well.

HEART: Three terms go with it. The conversion price is the amount of par you give up for each share. The conversion ratio is the number of shares you get for each bond, which is par divided by the conversion price. And the conversion value is what those shares are worth in the market now, the conversion ratio times the share price. As the share price rises, the conversion value rises, and the bond trades more like the stock.

LEWIS: The conversion right belongs to the investor, so investors pay a higher price and accept a lower yield than on a straight bond. That lower yield is the issuer's reward, at the cost of diluting existing shareholders if the bonds convert. Valuing a convertible properly is Level Two material.

HEART: A cousin is the warrant. Warrants can be attached to a straight bond when it is issued, each giving the right to buy the company's shares at a fixed price over a set period. They can be detached and traded on an exchange on their own. The difference: with a convertible, you give up the bond to get the shares. With a warrant, you keep the bond and pay cash for the shares.

LEWIS: Last, the contingent convertible bond, often called a CoCo bond. It converts from debt into common equity automatically when a set event happens, and some European banks have issued them. Banks must hold a minimum level of equity capital. If a bank's equity falls below a trigger level, its CoCo bonds convert, and in one stroke the bank has less debt and more equity.

HEART: Here is the trap. The name says convertible, so it is tempting to think the investor holds a valuable option. The investor holds no choice. Conversion happens automatically, when the bank is already in trouble and its shares have already fallen. Some versions skip the shares altogether and simply write the bonds down, so holders can lose everything. The feature works for the issuer, by absorbing losses when it most needs capital, so investors demand a higher yield than on the bank's ordinary bonds.

LEWIS: Now, legal, regulatory and tax considerations. The rules that govern a bond depend on where it is issued and traded, and where its issuer is based. A domestic bond is issued by a company or government in its own country's market and currency. A foreign bond is issued by an issuer from another country into a national market, in that market's currency. A Japanese carmaker selling dollar bonds in the United States, registered with U.S. regulators, is issuing foreign bonds, nicknamed Yankee bonds in that market.

HEART: A Eurobond is issued outside the jurisdiction of any single country, in any currency. The market grew up in the nineteen sixties as a way around United States rules, and it is still more lightly regulated than most domestic markets.

LEWIS: And the name is a trap. Euro does not mean the euro currency or a European issuer, although either can be true. The market simply began in Europe. Eurobonds are named by their currency, so a dollar bond in this market is a Eurodollar bond and a yen bond is a Euroyen bond. An Australian miner selling U.S. dollar bonds outside U.S. jurisdiction is issuing Eurodollar bonds.

HEART: Eurobonds were once mostly bearer bonds, owned by whoever physically held them. Today Eurobonds, like most bonds, are registered bonds, with each owner's name on file.

LEWIS: A global bond trades in the Eurobond market and in at least one domestic market at the same time. Foreign bonds, Eurobonds and global bonds together are called international bonds, to set them apart from domestic bonds.

HEART: Markets differ in their laws, rules and coupon conventions. But when yields differ from one market to another, the currency is usually the biggest reason. A bond is priced off the interest rates of whatever currency it pays in, so a dollar bond tracks dollar rates wherever it was issued, and a yen bond tracks yen rates.

LEWIS: One more structure. Sukuk are bonds built to comply with Islamic law, which restricts the payment of interest and limits what the money raised can fund. So the periodic payments are treated as rent from underlying assets rather than as interest.

HEART: Now taxes. Interest income is usually taxed as ordinary income, at the same rates as wages. The big exception in the United States is municipal bonds, the bonds of state and local governments. Their interest is usually exempt from federal income tax, and often from state income tax in the issuing state. That lets them pay lower yields than taxable bonds of similar risk. An investor that pays no income tax, such as a pension fund or a credit union, gets nothing from the exemption and is left with only the lower yield.

LEWIS: Sell a bond before maturity and you may realize a capital gain or loss. Strictly, that gain or loss is measured against the path the bond's price would follow toward par at a constant yield, which a later episode covers. Capital gains are often taxed at lower rates than ordinary income, and gains on assets held longer than some minimum period can be taxed lower still.

HEART: Zero coupon bonds and other bonds sold at a deep discount when issued are called original issue discount bonds. The climb from the issue price toward par is really interest. So in many tax systems, the investor must report part of that discount as taxable interest every year, even though that part of the return arrives in cash only at maturity. Some systems treat bonds issued at a premium the same way in reverse, letting part of the premium reduce the taxable part of the coupons.

LEWIS: The takeaway. Whoever holds an embedded option gets its value. A call or a CoCo trigger works for the issuer and raises the yield investors demand, while a put or a conversion right works for the investor and lowers it. Across markets, currency drives yields, and tax rules decide how much of the yield the investor keeps.

## Module 49.1, Fixed-Income Issuance and Trading

LEWIS: Now the market as a whole. Bond markets are usually divided three main ways: by the type of issuer, by credit quality, and by maturity. Other splits include currency, the issuer's region, and environmental, social and governance features, or ESG.

HEART: By issuer, the big groups are governments, both sovereign and non sovereign, then companies, then the special purpose entities that issue asset backed securities.

LEWIS: By credit quality, the dividing line runs between investment grade and high yield. Rating agencies such as S and P and Moody's grade the bonds. On the S and P scale, triple A down to triple B minus is investment grade. On the Moody's scale, the same band runs from triple A down to Baa three. Anything from double B plus down on the S and P scale, or Ba one down at Moody's, is high yield, also called speculative grade or junk.

HEART: That line matters far more than one notch normally would. Many institutions may own only investment grade bonds, so when a bond is downgraded across the line, a whole group of holders may be forced to sell at once. Issuers that fall from investment grade to high yield are called fallen angels.

LEWIS: By maturity, money market securities have an original maturity of a year or less, intermediate term securities run from one to ten years, and long term securities run beyond ten years.

HEART: Put credit and maturity together and you get a map. Governments issue across every maturity. Strong borrowers fund short term through commercial paper, through repurchase agreements, called repos, which are short term loans secured by bonds, and through asset backed commercial paper. Further out sit investment grade corporate bonds and asset backed and mortgage backed securities. At the riskier edge sit leveraged loans and high yield bonds, and new high yield bonds are often secured, because a company with shaky cash flows has to offer collateral.

LEWIS: What a company issues depends on its access to the capital markets and on what the money is for. The habit is to match the funding to the need. A strong, well known company might cover a seasonal build in inventory with commercial paper, fund a few years of expansion and its permanent working capital with intermediate notes, and pay for a new plant with long term bonds. For a fee, a syndicate of banks can stand ready with a credit facility for the shorter needs. A riskier company has far fewer doors open, often just secured short term borrowing and leveraged loans.

HEART: Investors choose their spot on the same map, based on how much interest rate risk and credit risk they want, and when they will need the money back. Pension funds and insurance companies buy long term investment grade bonds to match promises that stretch decades ahead, and regulations often bar or limit their high yield holdings.

LEWIS: Companies with spare cash park it short term, in commercial paper, repos and asset backed commercial paper. Central banks buy and sell intermediate term Treasury notes as a monetary policy tool, to add to or drain the reserves of commercial banks.

HEART: Bond funds and ETFs go wherever their mandate points them, most often into intermediate term investment grade bonds other than Treasuries. Managers chasing higher returns go into high yield, alongside hedge funds, and at the very bottom of the credit scale sit distressed debt funds.

LEWIS: And banks hold Treasuries across the whole maturity range, to manage interest rate risk and liquidity risk. That is the core of a bank or credit union investment portfolio: high quality bonds that can be sold or pledged quickly, spread across maturities to help offset the rate risk in loans and deposits.

HEART: Next, bond indexes, which differ from stock indexes in three ways. First, size. A company usually has one class of shares, or a handful at most, but a big borrower can have dozens of bonds outstanding, each with its own coupon and maturity, so bond indexes have far more members. Buying every bond is impractical, so bond index funds rely on sampling, holding a smaller set chosen to behave like the whole index.

LEWIS: Second, turnover. Bonds mature and new ones are issued all the time, so bonds drop out of an index and get replaced far more often than stocks do. Third, sector mix. Governments are usually the biggest borrowers, so broad bond indexes carry heavy weights in sovereign debt, and as borrowing shifts toward longer or shorter maturities, or stronger or weaker credits, the index weights shift with it.

HEART: The broadest bond indexes are called aggregate indexes. A typical one holds investment grade bonds across all sectors and leaves out high yield bonds, unrated bonds and issues too small to qualify. Narrower indexes focus on one region, credit band, sector or maturity range, and some build in ESG screens.

LEWIS: And because most bond indexes weight each bond by the market value of debt outstanding, the more an issuer borrows, the bigger its place in the index. The rule for a benchmark is that it should match the fund's own exposure in sector, credit quality and maturity.

HEART: Now the primary market, where new bonds are sold. The issuer sells new securities to investors and receives fresh capital in return. A new issue can go two routes. In a public offering, the issue is registered with securities regulators, and anyone can buy. In a private placement, the bonds are offered only to a chosen few, usually large institutions such as insurers. Investment banks usually run the sale either way.

LEWIS: Private placements skip public registration, so the terms, including the covenants, can be tailored. The tradeoff is that they are harder to resell, so investors usually want some extra yield.

HEART: An issuer selling its first bond ever is a debut issuer, typically a growing company ready to replace its bank loans with bonds. A debut issue usually needs weeks of roadshows, where the banks introduce the company to investors who do not know it yet, so it takes longer and costs more than a repeat issue.

LEWIS: Then comes the question of who carries the risk of the sale. In an underwritten offering, the banks guarantee the issue price, so if investors will not pay it, the banks absorb the shortfall. In a best efforts offering, the banks guarantee nothing. They place the bonds at the best price they can and charge a commission, so if demand is weak, the issuer feels it.

HEART: Think of selling a house. Underwritten is like a buyer who agrees to take the house at a set price and then resells it, carrying the risk that the market softens. Best efforts is like hiring an agent who promises to try hard and takes a commission.

LEWIS: A shelf registration lets an issuer register a total amount of bonds once, with a master prospectus, and then sell pieces off that shelf whenever it needs money. Speed follows familiarity. A frequent investment grade issuer with an underwritten shelf already on file can agree a deal and allocate the bonds the same day. A small high yield issuer selling on a best efforts basis, with detailed covenants and collateral to negotiate, needs far longer. Governments often sell bonds by public auction, which a later episode covers.

HEART: Compared with stocks, a company sells new shares rarely, but it comes back to the bond market again and again, because bonds mature and need refinancing. That is one big reason far more money is raised each year by issuing bonds than by issuing shares.

LEWIS: Now the secondary market, where investors trade bonds that already exist, and the issuer receives nothing. Some bonds trade on electronic platforms or exchanges, but most trading still happens in the dealer market, also called the over the counter market, or OTC. That is the big contrast with stocks, where exchanges sit at the center of trading.

HEART: A dealer quotes two prices. The bid is the price at which the dealer will buy from you. The ask, also called the offer, is the price at which the dealer will sell to you. The gap is the dealer's spread, and for the investor it is a cost of trading. You sell at the bid and buy at the ask.

LEWIS: That spread depends on liquidity. Recently issued government bonds from developed markets, called on the run issues, trade with tiny spreads, and so do bonds of high quality companies that issue often. Smaller, less liquid or older issues, called seasoned bonds, carry much wider spreads. That is why, in a liquidity squeeze, a bank or credit union reaches first for its most liquid holdings.

HEART: Last, distressed debt, the bonds of issuers that are in bankruptcy or expected to file. Distressed debt investors buy these bonds, often from institutions that are not allowed to hold low rated debt. They aim to profit if the issuer's fortunes turn, if creditors recover more than expected in a liquidation, or if a restructuring adds value. A bond that hardly ever trades can suddenly trade a lot once it becomes distressed.

LEWIS: The takeaway. The market sorts bonds by issuer, credit and maturity, and each investor picks its spot to match its own obligations. New bonds raise money in the primary market, and existing bonds change hands in the secondary market, mostly over the counter through dealers.

## Recall check

HEART Q: A note was issued with a ten year maturity nine years ago. Is it a money market security or a capital market security, and what is its tenor?

LEWIS: Answer. It is a capital market security with a tenor of about one year. Here is why. The money market and capital market labels come from the original maturity, the term when the bond was first issued. Tenor is the time left, and it shrinks every day without changing the label.

LEWIS Q: An indenture says the issuer is in default on this bond if it defaults on any of its other debts. What is that clause called, and is it an affirmative or a negative covenant?

HEART: Answer. It is a cross default clause, and it is an affirmative covenant. Here is why. Every covenant protects lenders, so protection is not the test. Negative covenants forbid actions, such as extra borrowing or pledging assets elsewhere. A cross default clause forbids nothing, so it sits with the affirmative covenants.

HEART Q: A bond has a sinking fund. Name one way it helps bondholders and one way it can hurt them.

LEWIS: Answer. It lowers credit risk, but it adds reinvestment risk. Here is why. Retiring part of the issue on a schedule leaves less principal to repay at maturity, so less rides on the issuer's final payment. But the bonds to be retired are picked at random, so a holder can be repaid early, and if rates have fallen, that holder must reinvest at lower yields.

LEWIS Q: Inflation runs hot for several years. In a capital indexed bond, which part of the bond moves, and what happens to the coupon payments?

HEART: Answer. The principal rises with inflation, and the coupon payments rise with it. Here is why. The coupon rate is fixed and acts as a real rate. Inflation is added to the principal instead, and the fixed rate is applied to that larger principal, so each coupon payment grows and the final repayment keeps its purchasing power.

HEART Q: Compared with an otherwise identical straight bond, does a callable bond offer a higher or a lower yield, and how do their values relate?

LEWIS: Answer. A higher yield, and the callable bond is worth the straight bond minus the value of the call. Here is why. The call belongs to the issuer, who will redeem the bond when yields fall, just when the investor would least like to reinvest. Add an uncertain end date and a ceiling on price gains, and investors demand a higher yield.

LEWIS Q: When is an investor most likely to exercise the put on a putable bond?

HEART: Answer. When the market price has fallen below the put price, usually because interest rates have risen or the issuer's credit has weakened. Here is why. The put lets the investor sell the bond back at a set price, usually par. If the market price is lower, selling to the issuer beats selling in the market, and the money can be reinvested at the new, higher yields.

HEART Q: A bank's contingent convertible bonds convert into shares when its capital falls below a trigger. Does that feature favor the investor or the issuer, and what does it do to the yield?

LEWIS: Answer. It favors the issuer, so the yield is higher. Here is why. The investor has no say in the matter. The bonds turn into shares on their own, just when the bank is struggling and its stock has already been marked down. That turns debt into equity and helps the bank meet its capital requirement, while the investor absorbs the losses, so investors demand a higher yield.

LEWIS Q: A Canadian bank sells U.S. dollar bonds in the United States, registered with U.S. regulators. Is that a domestic bond, a foreign bond or a Eurobond?

HEART: Answer. A foreign bond. Here is why. The issuer comes from one country, and the bonds are sold in another country's national market, in that market's currency and under its rules. A Eurobond would be issued outside the jurisdiction of any single country, and a domestic bond would need a U.S. issuer.

HEART Q: Why do pension funds and insurers favor long term investment grade bonds, and what can happen when a bond they hold is cut to high yield?

LEWIS: Answer. They need long bonds to match long dated promises, and a downgrade below investment grade can force them to sell. Here is why. Their pensions and claims fall due decades from now, so long, high quality bonds match those liabilities. Regulations often bar or limit their high yield holdings, so a fallen angel can be sold by many holders at once.

LEWIS Q: In an underwritten offering, who bears the risk that investors will not pay the expected price? And in a best efforts offering?

HEART: Answer. The banks in an underwritten offering, and the issuer in a best efforts offering. Here is why. Underwriters guarantee the issue price, so if demand is weak they absorb the shortfall. In a best efforts deal the banks only promise to try, placing what they can for a commission, so a weak reception lands on the issuer.

HEART Q: Where does most secondary trading in bonds happen, and which bonds cost the most to trade?

LEWIS: Answer. In the over the counter dealer market, and older, smaller, less liquid issues cost the most. Here is why. Most bonds have no exchange at the center of their trading, so most trades go through dealers who quote a bid and an ask. The gap between the two is the investor's trading cost, tiny for on the run government bonds and much wider for seasoned or thinly traded bonds.

## Key takeaways

HEART: First, a few memory hooks.

LEWIS: For the six features, ask six questions. Who owes it, when is it due, how much, what does it pay along the way, where does it stand in line, and what if? Issuer, maturity, par value, coupon rate and frequency, seniority, and contingency provisions.

HEART: For covenants, listen for the verb. Affirmative is must, negative is must not. Cross default and pari passu sit on the must side, and a negative pledge sits on the must not side.

LEWIS: For principal: a bullet pays it all at the end, amortizing pays it down along the way, and a balloon is whatever is left at the end. Principal back sooner means less credit risk and more reinvestment risk.

HEART: For options, whoever holds the right gets the value. Callable is the straight bond minus the call, so a higher yield. Putable is the straight bond plus the put, so a lower yield. A conversion right lowers the yield too. And a CoCo bond is the convertible the investor does not control, so it pays more.

LEWIS: For timing: issuers call when their borrowing cost falls, because rates dropped or their credit improved. Investors put when the price falls, because rates rose or the credit weakened.

HEART: For inflation linked bonds: interest indexed moves the coupon rate, capital indexed moves the principal, and in a capital indexed bond the coupon rate is a real rate.

LEWIS: For international bonds: Euro means outside, not the euro. A foreign bond is a visitor in someone else's home market. And currency, not location, drives the yield.

HEART: For issuance: underwritten, the banks wear the risk. Best efforts, the issuer does. Shelf registration means register once, sell in pieces. For trading: most bonds trade over the counter, and you sell at the bid and buy at the ask.

LEWIS: Now, how you would say the big ideas to a senior leader.

HEART: On what a bond is: a bond is a loan that can be traded. Its terms tell us who owes us, when we get paid, how much, and who gets paid ahead of us if things go wrong. The jargon is issuer, maturity, par value, coupon and seniority.

LEWIS: On covenants: covenants are the promises that stop a borrower from piling on risk after we have lent. Tighter covenants protect us but cost the borrower flexibility. The jargon is affirmative and negative covenants, the negative pledge and incurrence tests.

HEART: On callable bonds and prepayments: when a borrower can repay early, we get our money back just when rates have fallen, so we should be paid extra yield for giving them that choice. The jargon is call risk, reinvestment risk and an embedded call option.

LEWIS: On floating rate assets: a floating rate bond resets with market rates, so its value stays near par and its income moves with our funding costs. The risk left is the borrower's credit. The jargon is floating rate note, market reference rate and credit spread.

HEART: And on market liquidity: most bonds trade through dealers, not on exchanges, and older or smaller issues cost more to sell in a hurry. The jargon is the over the counter market, the bid ask spread and liquidity risk.
