LEWIS: Reading 50 covers how companies and banks fund themselves, from credit lines and commercial paper to deposits, repurchase agreements and long term bonds, and Reading 51 covers how governments and their agencies borrow, how their auctions work and why the newest issues set the benchmark. Reading 52 covers how one yield turns promised payments into a price, what happens between coupon dates and as maturity nears, and how to price a bond that rarely trades.

## Module 50.1, Fixed-Income Markets for Corporate Issuers

HEART: Picture a garden supply company in early spring. It stocks seed, soil and mowers weeks before customers buy anything, and its retail customers then take a month or two to pay. That gap sits in its short term assets: cash, short term investments, receivables and inventory. It can fund them through a bank, which is loan financing, or by selling short term securities to investors, which is security based financing.

LEWIS: Start with the bank. A line of credit lets the borrower draw money as it needs it, up to a limit. The three kinds form a ladder, from bottom to top: uncommitted, committed and revolving. Each step up is more reliable and more expensive.

HEART: The uncommitted line is the bottom rung. The bank names an amount and usually charges a floating market reference rate plus a fixed spread on whatever is drawn. It is flexible, with typically no fees beyond the interest, and a company that keeps steady cash balances at the bank may get it unsecured. The catch is in the name. The bank can decline to lend if conditions change, and they tend to change exactly when the company needs the money most.

HEART: The committed line, also called a regular line, is the middle rung. The bank promises to lend for a stated period, and the company pays a commitment fee for that promise, on either the full line or the unused part. Regulators make banks hold more capital against committed lines than against uncommitted ones, so banks keep commitments under a year or share large lines with other banks in a syndicate. And the borrower carries renewal risk, because a weakened borrower may find the line not renewed at maturity.

LEWIS: The top rung is the revolving line of credit, or revolver, also called an operating line. It runs longer, sometimes several years, may include medium term loan facilities, and comes with restrictive covenants. Its fees and rates look much like a committed line's.

HEART: Weaker companies usually have to pledge something. A secured loan, also called an asset based loan, is backed by collateral such as equipment, inventory or receivables. A company that assigns its receivables as collateral still owns and collects them, but the lender has a claim on them if the loan goes bad.

LEWIS: Factoring goes further. The company actually sells its receivables to a lender, called the factor, at a discount to their face value, and the factor takes over granting credit and collecting from the customers. The discount works as the factor's interest rate, and here is the twist. Its size depends on the creditworthiness of the company's customers and on collection costs, because the customers now owe the factor. A small supplier selling to large, reliable retailers can factor on good terms even if its own credit is mediocre.

HEART: Now security based financing. Large companies with strong ratings can skip the bank and borrow directly from investors by issuing commercial paper, short term unsecured debt that costs them less than a bank loan. In the United States it is typically a pure discount security, like a Treasury bill. Investors pay less than face value and receive face value at maturity.

LEWIS: Maturities are short, typically under three months. Companies issue it to fund working capital, and as bridge financing, a temporary loan that carries them until permanent financing, such as a long term bond, is in place. There is also a smaller and less liquid international market, called Euro commercial paper.

HEART: The issuer's risk is in the rhythm. Most commercial paper is repaid by selling new paper, which is called rolling it over. If investors stop buying, because markets freeze or the issuer is downgraded, the maturing paper still has to be paid. That is rollover risk. So issuers keep backup lines of credit, also called backup liquidity lines or liquidity enhancement, which a bank will fund to repay maturing paper if needed.

LEWIS: And the trap. A backup line does not make the commercial paper secured. It is a spare tire, not a lock.

HEART: Now financial institutions, where borrowing short is the business itself. The biggest short term source is customer deposits. Demand deposits, meaning checking accounts, offer payment services and immediate access, and typically pay little or no interest. Operational deposits come from larger customers that need the bank's cash management, custody and clearing services.

LEWIS: Savings deposits pay interest, and some lock the money in for a stated term at a stated rate. That kind usually takes the form of a certificate of deposit, which in this short term funding picture matures in under a year. At a credit union these are share accounts and share certificates, and the interest is paid as dividends. A nonnegotiable certificate cannot be sold, so cashing out early costs a penalty. A negotiable certificate can be sold to another investor before it matures, and large negotiable certificates sold to institutions are an important wholesale funding source for banks, trading in domestic bond markets and in the Eurobond market.

HEART: Banks also borrow from each other. Interbank funds are loans between banks for anything from a day to a year, secured or unsecured, priced off a market reference rate, the same kind of rate behind the floating rate notes from the last episode. In a stable banking system that rate carries very little credit risk, so it makes a natural base for pricing riskier loans. The most common form of secured interbank lending is the repurchase agreement, which comes up shortly. In the United States the main market reference rate is the Secured Overnight Financing Rate, or SOFR, and it is measured from overnight repurchase agreements backed by Treasuries.

LEWIS: In most countries, banks must also keep part of their funds as reserves at the central bank, although the United States has set that requirement at zero since twenty twenty. A bank holding more reserves than it needs can lend the excess to a bank that is short, in the central bank funds market, at a rate the central bank steers through its open market operations and other policy tools. And when a bank cannot find funding anywhere else, the central bank is the lender of last resort, through discount window lending, usually at a higher rate and with extra scrutiny and restrictions.

HEART: Financial institutions actually issue more commercial paper than nonfinancial companies do, and they sponsor a special version, asset backed commercial paper. The bank moves a pool of collateral, usually short term loans it has made, into a separate legal entity, a special purpose entity, in return for cash. That entity is designed to sit off the bank's balance sheet, though accounting rules can bring it back on, and it sells commercial paper to investors, who take on the risk and return of the collateral, and the sponsoring bank provides a backup liquidity line.

LEWIS: Now the most important short term tool in this module, the repurchase agreement, or repo. Picture a pawnshop for bonds. You hand over something valuable, get cash today, and agree to buy it back on a set date for a little more than you received. The extra is the interest.

HEART: On paper it is two trades, a sale today and a repurchase on a fixed date at a fixed price, normally a little higher. In substance it is a secured loan. The side that buys the bond hands over cash and holds the bond as collateral, and the gap between the two prices, stated as an annual rate, is the repo rate. Because it is really a loan, the original owner keeps the bond's economic benefits over the term, including the coupons.

LEWIS: A repo for one day is an overnight repo, and anything longer is a term repo. The collateral can be one specific security, or any security of an agreed type, which is a general collateral repo. The legal terms sit in a master repurchase agreement. Because a repo is short, secured and usually backed by high quality government bonds, its rate is customarily below the rate on a bank loan. A credit union holding Treasuries can raise cash overnight by repoing them.

HEART: Now the cushion. The cash lender does not lend the full value of the collateral. The borrower must post collateral worth more than the loan, and that requirement is the initial margin, quoted as the collateral's value relative to the loan, so it sits above one hundred percent. Seen from the other direction, the loan is a discount to the collateral's market value, and that discount, as a share of the collateral's value, is the haircut.

LEWIS: Two views of one cushion. A higher initial margin always means a bigger haircut, and both protect the cash lender. The cushion is also kept up during the trade. The loan balance grows each day at the repo rate. If the collateral's value falls below that growing balance times the initial margin, the lender asks for more collateral, which is called variation margin. If the collateral rises so the loan is overcollateralized, the borrower can ask for some back.

HEART: Who takes part? Financial institutions, especially dealers, sell securities in repos to finance the bonds they hold for trading. Banks and institutional investors, such as mutual funds and pension funds, sit on the other side to earn the repo rate on spare cash. Central banks run repos as a policy tool. Buying securities and lending cash adds money to the system, and selling securities and borrowing cash drains it.

LEWIS: The fourth group is short sellers, such as hedge funds, and their motive is the bond, not the cash. To bet on a falling price, a fund needs the bond itself, so it lends cash in a repo and takes that bond as collateral. It sells the bond at once, buys it back later, ideally cheaper, and returns it when the repo ends, collecting the repo rate on its cash plus any drop in the price.

HEART: The trade seen from the side that buys the security and lends the cash is called a reverse repo, and that is the side a short seller takes when its real aim is to borrow a particular bond. Naming the exact security it needs makes it a special trade.

LEWIS: And that produces a result that sounds backward. If a specific bond is hard to borrow because many short sellers want it, they will accept a lower repo rate on the cash they lend in order to get hold of it. For extremely scarce bonds the repo rate can even turn negative, which means the short seller gets back less cash than it lent.

HEART: Which brings us to what moves the repo rate. Five drivers. It is higher when other short term money market rates are higher, because a repo competes with them. It is lower when the collateral has higher credit quality. It is higher for a longer term, when longer rates sit above shorter ones. It is lower when the collateral is in high demand or short supply. And it is higher if the repo is undercollateralized, or if the collateral stays with the borrower instead of being delivered to the lender.

LEWIS: A repo is safer than most short term lending, but it is still debt, and leaning on it too heavily can push a borrower toward distress. The risks come in five kinds. Default risk, that the cash borrower fails to buy the security back. Collateral risk, about what the collateral would really fetch after a default. Margining risk, about margin being calculated and paid correctly and on time. Legal risk, that the contract cannot be enforced. And netting and settlement risk, about offsetting payments across contracts with the same counterparty and getting the cash and securities to change hands as agreed.

HEART: Many of those risks shrink in a triparty repo, where a third party, usually a custodian bank or a clearinghouse, administers the trade and values and safeguards the collateral. That makes the plumbing cheaper and more reliable, but it does not reduce credit risk. A repo struck directly between two parties is a bilateral repo.

LEWIS: Two repo traps. First, the party that sells the security first is the borrower of cash. Second, the haircut protects the lender of cash, so weaker collateral or a shakier borrower calls for more cushion, never less.

HEART: Now long term funding, and the contrast between investment grade and high yield issuers. When the yield curve slopes up, its normal shape, every borrower pays more to borrow for longer. But the extra cost of going long is usually bigger for high yield issuers, because their larger credit spreads also tend to grow with maturity.

LEWIS: So there is a temptation to borrow short and pay less, and any issuer that does that takes on rollover risk. Shorter is usually cheaper, longer is safer, and the last episode's habit of matching the funding to the need still guides the choice.

HEART: Picture two borrowers, a large regulated electric utility with a strong rating, and a retailer that was bought with borrowed money and carries heavy debt. For the utility's bond investors, imminent default is not the worry. Their main credit concern is a downgrade, a rise in the chance of future default that would knock the price down. For the retailer's bond investors, the concerns are default itself and how much they would lose if it happened, which is called loss given default.

LEWIS: That shows up in the yield. For investment grade bonds, the credit spread is a small part of the total yield, so their yields mostly track benchmark government yields. For high yield bonds, the spread is a much larger share.

HEART: Covenants differ too. Investment grade issuers usually accept only a few, typically limits on placing liens on their core operating assets, or on selling and leasing back those assets, because they generate the cash that repays the debt. High yield issuers face many more: limits tied to debt ratios, on taking on more debt, and on paying cash out to shareholders, such as dividends. They often have to pledge collateral as well.

LEWIS: Then maturity and flexibility. Investment grade issues are fairly standardized and come across many maturities, so the issuer can spread out its repayment dates, which cuts rollover risk, and pick whichever part of the curve looks attractive. High yield issues are less standardized, each with its own covenants and liens, and maturities are usually ten years or less, so these issuers are less able to jump in and refinance when borrowing costs fall.

HEART: But they do plan for one kind of refinancing. If its credit improves, a high yield issuer wants to swap expensive debt for cheaper debt, so it builds in a way out: leveraged loans that can be prepaid, or callable bonds.

LEWIS: And because the retailer's ability to repay is so uncertain, its bonds behave more like its stock. High yield returns are more equity like. That is the trap. Do not assume a high yield bond rallies just because Treasury yields fell. For investment grade, the government rate is the big driver. For high yield, it is the company.

HEART: The takeaway. Companies fund short term needs with credit lines, secured loans, factoring and commercial paper, while banks add deposits, interbank and central bank borrowing, and repos, where the haircut protects the cash lender. For long term funding, high yield issuers pay for their credit risk with wider spreads, more covenants and shorter terms.

## Module 51.1, Fixed-Income Markets for Government Issuers

HEART: A national government, called a sovereign, borrows to pay for public services and to build infrastructure. It can tax to repay, so in its home market it is usually both the strongest credit and the biggest borrower.

LEWIS: One accounting wrinkle. Public sector accounting leans more on cash and less on accruals, so items like depreciation or unfunded future obligations may not show. So an analyst should picture an economic balance sheet. On top of the reported financial assets and liabilities, it counts implied assets, such as taxes the government expects to collect, and implied liabilities, such as pensions and benefits it has promised.

HEART: The big split among sovereigns is developed versus emerging markets. A developed market sovereign has a stable, diversified economy and consistent, transparent fiscal policy, and it borrows in a reserve currency, a major currency that central banks around the world hold as reserves and that is widely used in trade.

LEWIS: An emerging market sovereign usually has a faster growing but less stable and more concentrated economy, so its tax revenue can swing with a single industry or commodity. Think of a country whose budget rises and falls with the price of copper. It often borrows to fund investment in growth.

HEART: Its debt comes in two kinds. Domestic debt is issued in the home currency and held by local investors, and that currency may not be freely convertible, because the market is thin or capital flows are restricted. External debt is owed to foreign creditors, and it can be in the home currency or in a foreign reserve currency.

LEWIS: That raises a subtle point. Say the copper exporter sells dollar bonds to foreign investors. Those investors avoid direct currency risk, because they are paid in dollars. But they still face indirect currency risk. The government has to earn or obtain enough dollars to pay them, and if copper prices collapse, the dollars may not be there.

HEART: How much a government borrows, and in what form, is set out in its debt management policy, and fiscal policy drives the totals. A government that thinks the economy is below full employment may cut taxes and spend more, which usually means new borrowing. So to forecast issuance, watch the fiscal stance and how much revenues and spending swing with the business cycle and with inflation, plus features such as floating rates or inflation indexing, and any guarantees of non sovereign borrowers.

LEWIS: Then the maturity question. Ricardian equivalence, from the economics readings, says taxpayers know that government debt today means higher taxes later, so they save to cover it. If that held completely, it would not matter whether the government taxed now or borrowed, or at what maturity. But that needs four assumptions. People save more when they expect higher taxes. They have rational expectations. They can borrow and lend with no transaction costs. And they pass their tax savings on to future generations.

HEART: Actual taxpayers do not behave that way, so maturity does matter, and most governments issue across the whole curve on a steady schedule to keep the mix of short and long debt stable. Short debt is tempting. Investors treat government bills almost like money in the bank and accept a lower yield for that safety and liquidity. But lean on bills too hard and a large share of the debt keeps coming due, which is rollover risk, just like a company living on commercial paper.

LEWIS: A deep market in longer government bonds helps everyone else. It is the yardstick for other borrowers' credit spreads, the hedge that asset managers, banks and credit unions reach for to manage interest rate risk, the standard collateral in repos, and what central banks trade to carry out monetary policy.

HEART: Below the national government sit non sovereign governments: states, provinces, counties and cities, plus entities created to fund and run local services. General obligation bonds fund general public spending and are backed by the issuer's power to raise local taxes. Revenue bonds fund one specific project and are repaid from the fees that project earns, say a city's water and sewer system or a public parking garage. So the investor's question is what stands behind the promise. If the parking garage sits half empty, revenue bondholders cannot call on the city's tax base.

LEWIS: Next, agency bonds, also called quasi government bonds. These come from entities a national government creates for a specific purpose, such as financing infrastructure or supporting mortgage lending. When an agency's debt is backed by the sovereign, its yield and its rating sit very near the government's own.

HEART: For a credit union, the familiar example is the Federal Home Loan Banks from the last episode, created by Congress to support housing finance. They fund their secured advances to members by selling bonds that carry no explicit government guarantee, which is one reason those bonds yield a little more than Treasuries.

LEWIS: Then supranational bonds, issued by institutions that several sovereign governments set up together to promote economic cooperation, trade or growth, such as the European Investment Bank or the regional development banks. Their bonds typically have high credit quality, and some issues are very liquid.

HEART: Now how government bonds come to market. A company borrows when it needs the money. A sovereign issues on a regular, announced schedule, through public auctions.

LEWIS: There are two kinds of bid. A competitive bid states the yield the bidder wants and the amount, and the competitive bids are what set the price. A noncompetitive bid states only an amount, and the bidder agrees to accept whatever price the auction sets. In return, its allocation is guaranteed.

HEART: The auction runs in a set order. The noncompetitive bids are filled first. Then the competitive bids are ranked from the highest price, which is the lowest yield, downward, and bonds go to the best bids first until the offering is sold. The yield on the last successful competitive bid, the one with the lowest accepted price, is the cutoff yield. Bids asking for a higher yield than that get nothing.

LEWIS: Then the rule that sets what winners pay. In a single price auction, every winner pays the same price, the price that goes with the cutoff yield, whatever yield it actually bid. In a multiple price auction, each successful competitive bidder pays the price it bid.

HEART: Here is the trap. The lowest yield bid is the most aggressive bid, because a lower yield means a higher price. In a single price auction, that aggressive bidder still pays only the cutoff price, the lowest price any winner pays. So bidding aggressively to make sure you win costs very little.

LEWIS: That is the appeal of single price auctions for a government that wants a steady borrowing yield. With one clearing yield for the whole issue, more investors bid with confidence, so the auction is more likely to be fully taken up, the bonds land with a wider group of holders, and funding tends to cost less. Under multiple price rules, an overeager bid costs you directly, so bids bunch tightly together and tend to be large.

HEART: Many sovereigns designate certain financial institutions as primary dealers, with three jobs. They must bid competitively at the auctions. They submit bids on behalf of other investors. And they act as the counterparty when the central bank buys and sells securities to carry out monetary policy.

LEWIS: Once issued, government bonds trade much like corporate bonds, in quote driven over the counter dealer markets. The most recently issued bond at each maturity is called the on the run issue. Trading is most active there, so its price carries the most information, and its yield serves as the benchmark, treated as free of default risk, when the government yield curve is built. Older issues of similar maturity are off the run. When an asset liability team measures a spread against the Treasury curve, that curve is typically built from on the run yields.

HEART: One more force on government yields. Some investors hold government bonds for reasons that have little to do with return. Central banks hold them to run monetary policy. Foreign governments hold them as reserves. And some financial institutions are required by regulation to hold them. These noneconomic buyers add demand, which pushes sovereign yields lower than those of non sovereign issuers.

LEWIS: The takeaway. Sovereigns borrow on their taxing power through scheduled auctions, where competitive bids set the cutoff yield, every winner in a single price auction pays the cutoff price, and the newest issue at each maturity becomes the benchmark. Local governments, agencies and supranationals are each judged by what stands behind their promise.

## Module 52.1, Fixed-Income Bond Valuation: Prices and Yields

HEART: Episode 1 set up two ideas this module builds on. Price and yield sit on a seesaw. And the coupon rate is the rent written into an old lease, while the yield is the return a buyer demands today. Now for why, and what follows from it.

LEWIS: A bond is a list of promised payments, the coupons and then the principal. Its price is the present value of all of them, each discounted back to today at one rate, the yield to maturity, also called the market discount rate. Know the yield and you can find the price. Know the price and you can back out the yield, which is the bond's internal rate of return.

HEART: One rate for every date is a simplification, and the next episode gives each date its own rate. But the single yield is how bonds are quoted, and this module runs on it.

LEWIS: Now why the seesaw tilts. Discounting a payment asks how much you would need to invest today, at the yield, to have that payment waiting on its date. Raise the yield and your money grows faster, so you need less today for every payment. Every payment's present value shrinks, so the price falls. And the further away a payment is, the more it shrinks, because the higher rate works on it for more years. Hold on to that. Rate changes hit distant payments hardest.

HEART: Here is the same thing from the investor's chair. Say you own a bond whose coupon matched the market rate when you bought it. Then yields jump, and new bonds of the same risk pay more. Nobody will pay full price for your smaller coupons, so your price has to drop until a buyer earns the new, higher yield.

LEWIS: That gives the vocabulary. When the coupon rate is below the yield, the coupon is called deficient, and the bond trades at a discount to par. When the coupon rate is above the yield, the coupon is excessive, and the bond trades at a premium. When they match, the bond trades at par. Prices are quoted as a percentage of par, so a price of one hundred means par.

HEART: Many bonds pay twice a year. For those, each coupon is half the annual coupon, there are twice as many periods, and the discount rate per period is half the stated yield. So a semiannual bond's yield is the six month rate times two, a stated annual rate, not an effective annual rate. The effective annual yield is slightly higher, because the first half year's interest earns interest in the second, the compounding point from Episode 1.

LEWIS: There is also a catch in saying the yield to maturity is what you earn. You earn exactly that only under three conditions. You hold the bond to maturity. The issuer makes every payment in full and on time. And you reinvest every coupon at that same yield. Sell early, suffer a default, or reinvest at lower rates, and your realized return will differ.

HEART: Now trading between coupon dates. The settlement date, when cash is exchanged for the bond, usually falls between two coupon payments. Whoever owns the bond on the next coupon date receives the whole coupon, but the seller owned it for part of that period and earned part of that coupon.

LEWIS: Think of buying a rental house in mid month, where the tenant pays at the end of the month. You will collect the whole month's rent, but the seller owned the house for the first half, so at settlement you pay the seller for that half on top of the price.

HEART: For a bond, the seller's share is accrued interest. It equals the coupon payment times the fraction of the coupon period that has passed since the last payment. Counting that fraction needs a day count convention. Under actual over actual, you count the real calendar days, both since the last coupon and in the whole period. Under thirty over three sixty, every month counts as thirty days and a year as three hundred sixty. Government bonds typically follow actual over actual, and corporate bonds thirty over three sixty.

LEWIS: Now two prices. The full price, also called the invoice price or the dirty price, is what the buyer actually pays, and it includes accrued interest. The flat price, also called the clean price, is the full price minus accrued interest, and it is the price dealers quote.

HEART: Why quote the flat price? Because the full price has a sawtooth pattern. Even if the yield never moves, the full price creeps up every day as interest accrues, then drops the moment the coupon is paid. Quoting the flat price strips that out, so a change in the quote mostly reflects the market rather than the calendar.

LEWIS: And the order matters. The flat price is what you are trying to find, so you build the full price first: the bond's value on the last coupon date at today's yield, grown forward at that yield for the part of the period that has passed. Subtract accrued interest, and you have the flat price.

HEART: The trap here is assuming the flat price is simply the bond's value on the last coupon date. It is not. The full price grows by compounding while accrued interest grows in a straight line, so the flat price drifts a little during the period. And keep the inputs straight. Accrued interest comes from the coupon, while the discounting comes from the yield.

LEWIS: Now the relationships among price, coupon, maturity and yield. The first is the seesaw itself, the inverse relationship. When the yield rises, the price falls, and when the yield falls, the price rises.

HEART: Second, the coupon effect. Take two bonds with the same maturity and the same yield, one with a low coupon and one with a high coupon. When yields move, the low coupon bond's price changes by a larger percentage. A low coupon bond gets more of its value from the principal at the very end, and distant payments are the ones rate changes hit hardest. A high coupon bond hands back more of its value early, in coupons that barely react. A zero coupon bond, with everything at the end, is the most sensitive of all for its maturity.

LEWIS: Third, the maturity effect. Other things equal, a longer bond's price is generally more sensitive to a change in yield than a shorter bond's, because its payments sit further out. In the investor's terms, if rates rise, the holder of a thirty year bond is stuck with a below market coupon for thirty years, while the holder of a two year bond is stuck for only two, so the long bond's price has to fall much further. The rare exceptions are very long bonds priced at a deep discount.

HEART: So the most sensitive bond is long and low: long maturity, low coupon. The least sensitive is short with a high coupon. For a credit union holding fixed rate securities, that is the first question when rates rise. Where are our long, low coupon bonds?

LEWIS: Fourth, convexity. For the same size move in yield, the price rises more when the yield falls than it drops when the yield rises, so the percentage gain beats the percentage loss. The intuition comes from discounting itself. As the yield climbs, each further step shaves less off a value that has already shrunk. As the yield falls, each step adds more to a value that keeps growing. Plot price against yield and you get a curve, steep where yields are low and flatter where yields are high. Later readings measure this properly.

HEART: Now time. A bond can trade far from par for most of its life, but on its last day it pays exactly par, so whatever its yield, its price has to finish there. That convergence is called pull to par. And the path the price follows if the yield stays the same the whole time is the constant yield price trajectory.

LEWIS: A premium bond drifts down toward par, a discount bond climbs up toward par, and a par bond, with a coupon equal to its yield, stays at par on each coupon date. The reason is the coupons. A premium is what you pay for a stream of above market coupons. Every coupon paid leaves one fewer still to come, so the premium shrinks. A discount is the price break for a stream of below market coupons, and it shrinks the same way.

HEART: The trap. A premium bond's price falls every year, but that is not a loss of return. The above market coupons make up for it, and an investor who holds to maturity at a constant yield still earns that yield. Likewise, a discount bond's climb toward par is not a windfall. It is part of the yield. And the trajectory assumes the yield holds still. In practice yields move, so the price wanders off the path, and pull to par only promises where the price ends up.

LEWIS: Two connections. The last episode said a capital gain or loss for tax purposes is measured against this constant yield path, and now you know what that path is. And on a bank or credit union's books, the amortized cost of a noncallable bond bought at a premium or a discount follows this same kind of path toward par under the effective interest method, at the yield the bond was bought at.

HEART: Last, matrix pricing. Many bonds rarely trade, or never do, such as a small corporate issue or a thinly traded municipal bond. Matrix pricing works out the yield the market would require for such a bond, and from that, its price.

LEWIS: It is the same idea an appraiser applies to a house that has not sold. You find comparable sales. Here, the comparables are bonds that do trade, with credit quality very near the target bond's, and similar maturities and coupons. Their yields tell you what the market demands for that kind of risk.

HEART: Usually no comparable matches the target's maturity exactly. So you take one that matures sooner and one that matures later, and you interpolate in a straight line. The target's estimated yield sits the same fraction of the way from the shorter bond's yield to the longer bond's yield as its maturity sits between their maturities. Then price the target bond by discounting its own coupons and principal at that estimated yield.

LEWIS: And here is the trap. Interpolate yields, never prices. The comparables have different coupons, so their prices reflect their own coupons, not the target's. Get the yield first, then apply it to the target's own cash flows.

HEART: A variation helps price a brand new issue. Take an existing bond from a similar issuer, and find the benchmark government yield at that bond's maturity, interpolating if needed. The gap between the two is the spread the market demands for that credit. Add that spread to the benchmark yield at the new bond's maturity, and you have the yield the new issue will likely need.

LEWIS: The takeaway. A bond's price is its promised payments discounted at one yield, so price and yield move in opposite directions, long, low coupon bonds move the most, and at a constant yield every bond is pulled to par. Between coupons the buyer pays the flat price plus accrued interest, and a bond that does not trade is priced at a yield taken from comparables.

## Recall check

LEWIS Q: From least to most reliable, what are the three kinds of bank credit line, and what happens to cost as you climb?

HEART: Answer. Uncommitted, then committed, then revolving, and cost rises along with reliability. Here is why. An uncommitted line carries no promise, so the bank can decline to lend when conditions change. A committed line adds a promise for a set period, paid for with a fee. A revolver extends that promise for longer, often years, with restrictive covenants attached.

HEART Q: A company funds its working capital by rolling commercial paper. What risk does its backup line of credit address, and does that line make the paper secured?

LEWIS: Answer. It addresses rollover risk, and no, the paper stays unsecured. Here is why. Commercial paper is usually repaid by selling new paper, and if investors stop buying, the maturing paper still has to be paid. The backup line gives the issuer a second source of cash to repay it, but it gives investors no claim on any asset.

LEWIS Q: In a repo, which party is borrowing cash, and if the lender raises the initial margin, what happens to the haircut?

HEART: Answer. The party that sells the security and agrees to buy it back is borrowing cash, and a higher initial margin means a bigger haircut. Here is why. Initial margin measures the collateral against the loan, and the haircut measures how far the loan falls short of the collateral. They describe the same cushion, so raising one raises the other.

HEART Q: An emerging market government sells bonds in a major reserve currency to foreign investors. What currency risk do those investors still carry?

LEWIS: Answer. Indirect currency risk. Here is why. Being paid in a reserve currency removes the direct risk that the issuer's own currency weakens. But the government has to come up with enough of that reserve currency to pay them. If its export earnings fall, it may not be able to, so the currency problem shows up as credit risk.

LEWIS Q: In a single price auction, a dealer wins with the lowest yield bid. What price does it pay, compared with a winner that bid right at the cutoff yield?

HEART: Answer. The same price, the price that goes with the cutoff yield. Here is why. In a single price auction every winner pays one price, set by the last successful competitive bid. The aggressive dealer bid a lower yield, which means a higher price, but it is charged only the cutoff price. In a multiple price auction, it would pay its own higher price.

HEART Q: Why is an on the run government bond, rather than an older bond of similar maturity, the benchmark for the yield curve?

LEWIS: Answer. Because it is the most actively traded, so its price is the most informative. Here is why. The most recently issued bond at each maturity draws the most trading, so its yield reflects the market's current view most accurately. Older off the run bonds trade less, so their prices say less about where the market is right now.

LEWIS Q: A bond's coupon rate is below its yield to maturity. Is it at a premium or a discount, and if the yield never changes, which way does its price move as it ages?

HEART: Answer. It trades at a discount, and its price rises toward par. Here is why. A deficient coupon means the buyer needs a lower price to earn the market yield, and the discount pays for the below market coupons still to come. Each coupon paid leaves one fewer, so the discount shrinks until the bond reaches par at maturity.

HEART Q: A bond settles between coupon dates. Which price does the buyer pay, and why do dealers quote the other one?

LEWIS: Answer. The buyer pays the full price, and dealers quote the flat price. Here is why. The full price includes accrued interest, the seller's share of the next coupon, so it rises every day and drops on the coupon date even when the yield does not move. The flat price removes that pattern, so the quote moves mainly with the market.

LEWIS Q: Two bonds have the same yield. One has a long maturity and a low coupon, the other a short maturity and a high coupon. Which price changes more, in percentage terms, when yields move?

HEART: Answer. The long maturity, low coupon bond. Here is why. Rate changes hit distant payments hardest, because the new rate works on them for more years. A long, low coupon bond has most of its value far out in the principal, while a short, high coupon bond gets much of its value back soon, in coupons that barely react.

HEART Q: In matrix pricing, what do you interpolate between the comparable bonds, and why not their prices?

LEWIS: Answer. You interpolate yields. Here is why. Each comparable's price reflects its own coupon, and the target bond's coupon is different, so averaging prices would mix in coupon differences. The yield captures what the market demands for that credit and maturity, and then you discount the target's own cash flows at that yield.

## Key takeaways

HEART: First, a few memory hooks.

LEWIS: For credit lines, climb the ladder: uncommitted, committed, revolver. The more the bank promises, the more you pay. For receivables: assigning pledges them, factoring sells them, and in factoring, the customers' credit sets the discount.

HEART: For commercial paper: strong names only, rolled over again and again, with a backup line that is a spare tire, not a lock.

LEWIS: For repos, think of a pawnshop for bonds. Sell it, buy it back for more, and the difference is the interest. The seller of the security borrows the cash, and the haircut protects the cash lender. The scarcer the bond, the lower the repo rate, sometimes below zero.

HEART: For long term corporate funding: investment grade fears the downgrade, high yield fears the default. Investment grade tracks government yields, and high yield tracks the company.

LEWIS: For governments: developed sovereigns borrow in a reserve currency, and emerging ones may owe in someone else's, which turns currency risk into credit risk. General obligation bonds have the tax base behind them, and revenue bonds have one facility's customers.

HEART: For auctions: single price means one price for every winner, set at the cutoff yield. Multiple price means you pay what you bid. The lowest yield bid is the most aggressive. And the newest issue at each maturity, the on the run bond, is the benchmark.

LEWIS: For pricing: excessive coupon, premium. Deficient coupon, discount. Long and low moves the most. Falling yields help more than equal rising yields hurt. Quote clean, pay dirty. And at a constant yield, time pulls every bond to par.

HEART: For matrix pricing: comparables give the yield, and the target's own cash flows give the price. Interpolate yields, never prices.

LEWIS: Now, how you would say the big ideas to a senior leader.

HEART: On short term funding: the cheapest sources are the least dependable when markets get tight, so we pay for committed backup we hope never to touch. The jargon is uncommitted, committed and revolving lines of credit, commercial paper and rollover risk.

LEWIS: On repos: a repo lets us borrow against our high quality bonds, or lend spare cash with bonds as security. The lender advances a bit less than the bonds are worth and asks for more if their value falls. The jargon is repurchase agreement, repo rate, haircut, initial margin and variation margin.

HEART: On government markets: Treasury yields are the yardstick for much of what we price, and the newest issues give the cleanest reading of where rates are. The jargon is sovereign debt, on the run issues and the benchmark yield curve.

LEWIS: On our bond portfolio: when market yields rise, our fixed rate bonds lose value, and the longer, lower coupon ones lose the most. If we hold them, prices return to par by maturity, but the income stays below market until then. The jargon is the inverse price yield relationship, the coupon and maturity effects, and the constant yield price trajectory.

HEART: And on valuing bonds that rarely trade: we price them from similar bonds that do trade, using their yields, not their prices. The jargon is matrix pricing and linear interpolation.
