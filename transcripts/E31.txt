LEWIS: Reading 58 covers convexity, the curvature in the price yield relationship that duration misses, what gives a bond more of it, and how duration and convexity carry over to a whole portfolio. Reading 59 covers risk measures built on shifts in a benchmark curve: effective duration and convexity for bonds with embedded options, key rate durations for changes in the curve's shape, and empirical duration drawn from market history.

## Module 58.1, Yield-Based Bond Convexity and Portfolio Properties

HEART: The last episode left a loose end. Modified duration treats the link between a bond's price and its yield as a straight line, and the real link is a curve. This module measures that curve. The measure is called convexity, and it is the curvature that duration misses.

LEWIS: Picture a ruler held against the outside of a bowl, touching it at a single point. On both sides of that point, the bowl curves up and away from the ruler. The bowl's curve is the true price yield relationship for an ordinary bond with no embedded options. The ruler is duration's straight line, and the point where they touch is today's yield.

HEART: Now put that in bond terms. If yields fall, the bond's real price ends up above the straight line, so the bond gains more than duration predicts. If yields rise, the real price again ends up above the line, so the bond loses less than duration predicts. Duration alone understates the gain and overstates the loss. And the gap between the line and the curve widens the further you move from today's yield, which is why duration works well for small moves and poorly for big ones.

LEWIS: Why does the curve bend at all? You already have the reason from the last episode. A bond's duration gets longer as yields fall and shorter as yields rise. So as yields fall, the bond becomes more sensitive with every step, and its price gains speed up. As yields rise, it becomes less sensitive with every step, and its losses slow down. Convexity measures how quickly that slope changes as the yield moves.

HEART: The formal names help here. Duration captures the first order effect of a yield change, the straight line part. Convexity captures the second order effect, the bend. Put the two together and the prediction improves, and it improves most for large moves in yield.

LEWIS: In words, the improved relationship has two parts. The first is the duration effect from the last episode, negative modified duration times the change in yield. The second is the convexity adjustment, one half times convexity times the change in yield squared. Both parts use annual figures, annual modified duration and annual convexity, with the yield change written as a decimal, and the result is the approximate percentage change in the bond's full price.

HEART: Two things follow from that squared term, and they carry most of the exam. First, squaring a number removes its sign. A rise in yield and a fall in yield of the same size produce exactly the same convexity adjustment. So for a bond with positive convexity, the adjustment is always added, whichever way yields move. It shrinks the loss when yields rise and enlarges the gain when yields fall.

LEWIS: Second, squaring makes the adjustment grow much faster than the move itself. Double the change in yield and the duration effect doubles, but the convexity adjustment quadruples. That is why convexity barely matters for a move of a few basis points and matters a great deal for a shock of a few hundred.

HEART: Here is where that bites in practice. Bank and credit union balance sheet teams routinely report what would happen to the value of their assets under sudden, parallel rate shocks of several hundred basis points. For a portfolio of plain bonds, a duration only figure would overstate the loss when rates jump and understate the gain when they drop. Adding convexity brings both figures much nearer to what a full repricing would show.

LEWIS: The trap here has two sides. One is forgetting the one half, which doubles the adjustment. The other is a sign slip. When yields rise, the duration effect turns negative, but the convexity adjustment for an ordinary bond does not. It stays positive and pulls the loss back toward zero.

HEART: So what does positive convexity buy the holder? Picture two bonds with no options that share the same price, the same yield and the same duration, but one has more convexity. Let yields move sharply in either direction. When yields fall, the more convex bond gains more. When yields rise, it loses less. Either way, it ends up ahead of its twin. All else equal, convexity is valuable to the holder, and it is most valuable when you expect a big move but cannot tell which way.

LEWIS: That deserves one precise caution. Positive convexity does not mean you profit when yields rise. You still lose money. You simply lose less than the straight line said you would. Convexity improves the outcome relative to duration, in both directions. It does not change the direction.

HEART: And because convexity is valuable, it is rarely free. In practice, investors usually pay for extra convexity by accepting a slightly lower yield. That goes a little beyond this module, but it explains why the market does not simply hand out more convex bonds at the same yield.

LEWIS: Now, how is convexity measured? There are two routes, and they mirror the two routes to duration from the last episode.

HEART: The exact route works payment by payment. Every cash flow has its own convexity, and it grows roughly with the square of how far away that payment is. A payment twice as far out carries roughly four times the convexity. The bond's convexity is then the weighted average of its cash flows' convexities, with the same present value weights as Macaulay duration. So the distant payments, above all the principal at the end, dominate convexity even more than they dominate duration.

LEWIS: One unit trap comes with that. If you work in half year periods for a semiannual bond, the result comes out in squared periods. To state it per year, divide by the number of periods per year squared, which means dividing by four for a semiannual bond. Duration converts by the number of periods per year. Convexity converts by its square, because convexity is measured in time squared.

HEART: The practical route is approximate convexity, and it needs no cash flow schedule. You reprice the bond with its yield a little lower, then with its yield a little higher by the same amount. These are the same two prices you would build for approximate modified duration. Then you add the two prices together, compare that sum with twice today's price, and scale the difference by today's price and by the squared change in yield.

LEWIS: The logic is worth having in your head. If the price yield relationship were a straight line, the price after a fall and the price after an equal rise would sit equally far above and below today's price. Their average would be exactly today's price, and the difference you scale would be zero. Curvature lifts both repriced values above that line, so their average lands above today's price. That excess, once scaled, is the convexity.

HEART: Which also gives you a quick way to tell the two approximations apart. Approximate duration works from the gap between the two prices, the down price minus the up price. Approximate convexity works from their sum, set against twice today's price. Both scale by today's price, but duration divides by twice the yield change and convexity by the yield change squared. Gap for slope, sum for bend.

LEWIS: There is one more payoff from that sum. If the two repriced values average above today's price, convexity is positive. If they average below it, convexity is negative. Hold that thought, because some bonds do exactly that.

HEART: Before those bonds, dollars. The last episode built money duration, annual modified duration times the full price of a position. Its partner is money convexity, annual convexity times the full price of the position, stated in currency units and sometimes per hundred of bond value. Together they give the dollar change in the position's full price: negative money duration times the yield change, plus one half money convexity times the squared yield change. Same shape as the percentage version, scaled up to the size of the holding.

LEWIS: That matters because limits are set in dollars. An investment committee does not ask how many percent the bond book might lose. It asks how many dollars. Money duration and money convexity together answer that more accurately than money duration alone, and the difference is largest for exactly the big shocks a committee worries about.

HEART: Next, what gives a bond more convexity. Start with the three features you already know from duration, because the same ones work here. Other things equal, more time to maturity, a smaller coupon and a lower yield each add convexity, and their opposites take it away. Maturity even carries a version of duration's rare exception, for very long coupon bonds priced at a deep discount. It never applies to a zero, whose convexity keeps rising with maturity.

LEWIS: Each one follows from the payment by payment picture. A longer maturity pushes the principal further out, and far payments carry outsized convexity. A lower coupon leaves more of the value in that distant principal. A lower yield discounts far payments less heavily, so they keep more of the weight. Anything that tilts value toward distant payments raises convexity.

HEART: The fourth feature is the new one, and it is often tested on its own. Hold duration fixed, and the bond whose payments are scattered more widely across the calendar has more convexity. The term is dispersion.

LEWIS: Picture two payment schedules with the same value weighted average date, say five years. One pays everything in a single lump at year five. The other pays part of its value very early, around year two, and the rest very late, around year thirty. Same center, very different spread.

HEART: Now recall that a payment's convexity grows roughly with the square of its time. Pushing one payment much later adds a lot of convexity. Pulling another payment earlier removes only a little. So spreading payments out around the same center raises convexity. In portfolio language, a barbell, which holds very short and very long bonds, has more convexity than a bullet with the same duration that concentrates its value near one maturity.

LEWIS: At the other extreme, a zero coupon bond has all of its cash on one date, no spread at all. So for a given duration, a zero coupon bond has the least convexity of any option free bond. Notice that this is a comparison at equal duration. Compare a zero and a coupon bond at the same maturity instead, and the zero has more convexity, because its single payment sits at the far end.

HEART: That pair is a classic trap. Same maturity, the zero has the most convexity. Same duration, the zero has the least. Always check what the question holds equal.

LEWIS: Now the other side of the coin. Every option free bond has positive convexity, so its curve always bends the helpful way. But some bonds have cash flows that respond to rates, and their curve can bend the other way when yields are low. That is negative convexity, and the two everyday cases are callable bonds and mortgages.

HEART: Take a callable bond first. The last episode showed how the call shortens duration, and here is the convexity side. When rates fall well below its coupon, the issuer is likely to call it and refinance more cheaply. Nobody will pay much more than the call price for a bond that can be taken away at that price. So as yields fall, the price stalls instead of speeding up. When yields rise, the call grows less likely, the cap matters less and less, and the bond loses value more and more like an ordinary bond. Gains get capped, losses do not. That is negative convexity.

LEWIS: Mortgages do the same thing through prepayments, the borrower's right to pay off the loan early, which the last episode framed as a call option. When rates fall, homeowners refinance, and the lender, or the holder of a mortgage backed security, gets principal back early at par, just when it would rather keep earning the old, higher rate. So the price gain is limited. That shortening of the expected life is called contraction risk.

HEART: When rates rise, the reverse. Hardly anyone refinances into a higher rate, and fewer people move, so prepayments slow down. The mortgages stay outstanding longer, the expected life stretches out, and the holder is stuck with below market loans for longer. The price falls by more than it would if the cash flows had stayed put. That lengthening is called extension risk.

LEWIS: Put the two together. When rates fall, the holder gains less than duration predicts. When rates rise, the holder loses more. That is the mirror image of positive convexity, and it is why a bank or credit union with a large book of fixed rate mortgages, or a portfolio of mortgage backed securities, carries negative convexity on its balance sheet. When rates rose sharply in twenty twenty two, prepayments slowed to a trickle, and the expected life of many mortgage holdings stretched out just as their values were falling.

HEART: The details of prepayment come in the later reading on mortgage backed securities, and measuring negative convexity properly takes the effective measures in the next module. For now, keep the picture. Positive convexity cushions the holder both ways. Negative convexity works against the holder both ways.

LEWIS: Last part of the module, portfolios. An investment book might hold dozens of bonds. How do you get one duration and one convexity for the whole thing? There are two approaches.

HEART: The first treats the portfolio as if it were one big bond. You combine every cash flow from every holding, date by date. You find the single discount rate that prices that combined stream at the portfolio's value, a rate called the cash flow yield. Then you compute duration and convexity from the combined stream at that rate. This is the theoretically correct approach.

LEWIS: The second is the approach used in practice, because it is far easier. Take each bond's duration and weight it by that bond's share of the portfolio's value, measured at full price, meaning market value including accrued interest, not par amount. Add up the weighted durations and you have portfolio duration. Portfolio convexity works the same way. In dollars it is simpler still, because the money durations of the holdings just add up.

HEART: You already know this kind of number from the funding side. A credit union's cost of funds is a balance weighted average of the rates it pays on regular shares, certificates and borrowings. Portfolio duration is the same kind of blend, weighted by market value instead of by balance. And a blend has the same weakness in both cases. It tells you the overall level, not which piece is doing the moving.

LEWIS: Which brings us to the limitation, and it is the one the exam wants. Blending durations quietly assumes that every holding's yield, short or long, moves by the same number of basis points. That is a parallel shift in the yield curve. When the curve really does shift in parallel, the blended duration tells you the portfolio's percentage change for a one point move. When it does not, the blend can mislead.

HEART: Real curves rarely cooperate. They steepen, with long yields rising relative to short ones. They flatten, the reverse. They twist, with one end rising while the other falls. Picture a portfolio split between short notes and long bonds. If short yields jump while long yields barely move, the single duration number assumes every holding felt the same jump, and it misjudges the result.

LEWIS: Here is the trap. Two portfolios can have exactly the same weighted average duration and respond very differently when the curve changes shape. A single duration number answers one question well, what happens if every rate moves together, and no other. The next module supplies the tool for the rest.

HEART: The takeaway. Convexity is the curvature duration misses, and for an option free bond it is always positive, which helps the holder whichever way rates move. Longer maturity, lower coupon, lower yield and more dispersed cash flows raise it. Portfolio duration and convexity are market value weighted averages that hold only for parallel shifts.

## Module 59.1, Curve-Based and Empirical Fixed-Income Risk Measures

LEWIS: The yield based measures so far rest on two quiet assumptions. The bond's cash flows stay fixed when rates change, and every yield moves by the same amount. This module drops each assumption in turn, and then asks a third question. What if the market simply does not behave the way the formulas assume?

HEART: Start with the first assumption. For a straight bond, one with no embedded options, you know every payment and every date in advance. For a callable bond, a putable bond or a mortgage backed security, you do not, because each one can end early. A putable bond ends early if the investor chooses to sell it back. A callable bond ends early if the issuer chooses to redeem it. A mortgage backed security pays down early when the underlying borrowers choose to prepay.

LEWIS: It helps to see each one as a straight bond with an option attached. Owning a callable bond is like owning a straight bond and having sold the issuer a call option, the right to buy the bond back from you. Owning a putable bond is like owning a straight bond and having bought a put option, the right to sell the bond back. A mortgage backed security works like the callable case, except that the call sits with thousands of homeowners, any of whom can pay off a loan early.

HEART: That uncertainty breaks the bond's own yield as a measuring stick. A callable bond has no single yield you can rely on. It has a yield to maturity and a separate yield to each call date, and only the issuer's later decision settles which one the investor actually earns.

LEWIS: So for these bonds, you measure interest rate risk against a shift in a benchmark yield curve instead, such as the government par curve. That measure is effective duration. The last episode previewed the recipe. Shift the benchmark curve down by a small amount and reprice the bond. Shift it up by the same amount and reprice again. Take the average price change and scale it by today's value and the size of the shift. The result is a straight line approximation of the percentage change in price for a one point parallel shift in the benchmark curve.

HEART: The repricing is the crucial part. You cannot just discount a fixed set of cash flows, because the cash flows depend on rates. You need a pricing model that decides, at each new level of rates, whether the issuer calls, the investor puts or the borrowers prepay, and values the bond accordingly. The curve shift and the model together generate the down price and the up price.

LEWIS: One more thing is held fixed while you shift the curve: the bond's spread over the benchmark, which pays for credit risk and liquidity risk. That is a real difference from modified duration. Modified duration responds to any change in the bond's own yield, whatever caused it. Effective duration isolates one cause, a move in the benchmark curve, with spreads assumed unchanged.

HEART: Think of a boat in a harbor. The benchmark curve is the tide, which lifts or lowers every boat together. The spread is the chop around one particular boat. Effective duration measures how a bond rides the tide, and nothing else. So picture a corporate bond whose issuer is downgraded while government yields sit still. Its own yield rises because its spread widened, and a yield based measure such as modified duration treats that like any other rise in the bond's yield, whatever the cause. A benchmark based measure blames none of the price drop on rates, because the benchmark never moved. That loss belongs to credit spread risk instead.

LEWIS: Effective convexity follows the pattern of approximate convexity. Reprice with the benchmark curve shifted down and up, add the two prices, set that sum against twice today's price, and scale by today's price and the squared size of the shift. If the two repriced values average above today's price, effective convexity is positive. If they average below it, effective convexity is negative.

HEART: Now back to the callable bond, with the right tools. When rates are high relative to its coupon, the call is unlikely, and the bond behaves almost exactly like its straight twin, with positive convexity. As rates fall, the call grows more valuable, and the call price acts as a ceiling on the bond's value. The price yield curve flattens out under that ceiling and bends the other way. At low yields, a callable bond can show negative convexity. Its gain from a fall in yields is smaller than its loss from an equal rise.

LEWIS: And its duration shrinks. Because the price barely responds as rates fall, a callable bond is less rate sensitive at low yields than the same bond without the call. At high yields, where the call is a long shot, the two durations are nearly the same. The trap is assuming a callable bond is always much less sensitive. It is less sensitive when the call is likely, which means when rates are low.

HEART: Bank and credit union portfolios hold plenty of callable agency bonds, which pay a little extra yield to compensate the holder for the call it sold. Picture one that matures in ten years but can be called after one. When rates are well above its coupon, it trades much like a ten year bond. When rates are well below its coupon, it trades much like a one year bond, because everyone expects it back at the first call date.

LEWIS: Which is why a portfolio report shows effective duration for those holdings rather than a duration worked out to the final maturity date. And it is why that effective duration can swing as rates move, even though nothing about the bond itself has changed.

HEART: When convexity turns negative, so does the convexity adjustment, whichever way the curve moves. So the holder gains less than the straight line predicts when rates fall, and loses more than it predicts when rates rise. Keep the sign of convexity attached when you reason about it, and that result falls out on its own.

LEWIS: The putable bond is the mirror image. When rates rise, the put becomes more valuable, because the holder can hand the bond back at the put price. That price acts as a floor. So as yields climb, a putable bond loses less than its straight twin. So the put shortens duration when yields are high, and makes little difference when yields are low, where nobody expects it to be exercised.

HEART: And a putable bond always has positive convexity. The holder bought the option, so the option only ever adds protection. The callable holder sold the option, so it gave away the upside. Bought option, convexity stays positive. Sold option, convexity can turn negative.

LEWIS: Here is a subtle point the exam likes. For an option free bond, you might expect modified duration and effective duration to be identical. They are very near each other, but not always exactly equal. The reason is that par yields are blends of spot rates. When the curve slopes, moving every par yield by the same amount requires the underlying spot rates to move by slightly different amounts. A corporate bond priced at a constant spread over those spot rates therefore sees its own yield move by a slightly different amount than the par curve shift.

HEART: You do not need the mechanics. Just keep the rule. For an option free bond, modified duration and effective duration match exactly only when the yield curve is flat. Otherwise they differ a little.

LEWIS: To turn effective measures into a price change, apply the same two part relationship as before, with the shift in the benchmark curve in place of the change in the bond's yield. Negative effective duration times the curve shift, plus one half effective convexity times the squared curve shift, gives the approximate percentage change in the bond's full price.

HEART: With one caution that is easy to miss. For an option free bond, the smaller the move, the better duration and convexity predict the price. For a bond with an option, that is not guaranteed. Whether the option gets exercised depends on more than government rates. A corporate issuer calls when it can borrow more cheaply, and that depends on its own credit standing as well as on government rates. A homeowner refinances when the savings outweigh the closing costs, and that depends on the size of the remaining loan, not only on rates. So even a small move in the benchmark can cross a threshold where behavior changes, and the effective measures can miss it.

LEWIS: Now the second assumption, that every yield moves by the same amount. Effective duration, like everything before it, is built on a parallel shift of the benchmark curve. To see what a change in the curve's shape does, you need key rate duration, also called partial duration.

HEART: A key rate duration measures how much a bond's or a portfolio's value responds when the benchmark yield at a single maturity moves and every other point on the curve stays put. You pick a set of key maturities, such as two, five, ten and thirty years, and nudge them one at a time.

LEWIS: Picture a house with a thermostat in every room. Each room has its own sensitivity, how much the heating bill rises if you turn that room up one degree. Turn every room up one degree together, and the bill rises by the sum of all the rooms' sensitivities. Turn some rooms up and others down, and the bill changes by each room's sensitivity times its own change, added together.

HEART: Key rates work exactly that way. The key rate durations of a bond or portfolio add up to its effective duration, because a parallel shift is simply every key rate moving together. For a shift that is not parallel, each key rate contributes its own effect, its key rate duration times the change in that one rate, with value falling where rates rise and rising where rates fall. Add those effects and you have the approximate percentage change in value.

LEWIS: Where does a key rate duration come from? For a single cash flow in a portfolio, it is that cash flow's modified duration times its weight in the portfolio's value. So the exposure at a maturity is large when a lot of value sits at that maturity and the maturity is far out.

HEART: The risk key rates reveal has a name, shaping risk, the effect on a portfolio of a change in the shape of the yield curve. Picture two portfolios with the same effective duration. One is a bullet, with nearly all of its value near the five year point. The other is a barbell, part in two year notes and part in thirty year bonds. A parallel shift hits them about equally. Now let only the thirty year yield rise. The bullet barely notices. The barbell takes a loss, because a big share of its key rate exposure sits at thirty years.

LEWIS: Same duration, very different shaping risk. That is exactly what the single duration number from the last module could not show. Balance sheet teams rely on the same idea. When assets are tied to longer rates and funding reprices off shorter ones, the exposure depends on the curve's shape, not only its level, and key rate durations show where along the curve that exposure sits.

HEART: Now the third question. Every measure so far, Macaulay, modified and effective, is an analytical duration. It comes from mathematical analysis, a formula or a pricing model, applied to assumed cash flows and an assumed change in rates. Empirical duration takes the other road. It is estimated from history, with a statistical model of how a bond's price has actually moved when benchmark yields moved.

LEWIS: Think of a car's fuel economy. The figure on the window sticker comes from standardized test conditions. That is analytical. The mileage you actually get on your commute, measured over months of real driving, is empirical. When conditions match the test, the two agree. When they do not, the real world figure is usually the better guide.

HEART: Here is the assumption that makes them part ways. When you compute a corporate bond's duration from a shift in the government curve, you hold its credit spread constant. Put another way, you assume that changes in the benchmark yield and changes in the spread are unrelated. For many bonds, that assumption fails in a predictable direction.

LEWIS: The classic case is a flight to quality. Fear rises, and investors rush into the safest bonds. Government yields fall. At the same moment, worries about default push credit spreads wider. For a government bond, the benchmark drop flows straight into a higher price. A corporate bond feels two forces at once, the falling benchmark lifting its price and the wider spread pulling it down. So it rises by less than the government bond, and if its spread widens by more than the benchmark falls, its price drops.

HEART: So for a portfolio of corporate bonds, empirical duration tends to come out lower than analytical duration. The bonds respond less to benchmark moves than the formulas predict, because the spread tends to move the opposite way and cushion the change. The same offset tends to show up in good times as well, when benchmark yields rise in a strong economy while spreads narrow, though not in every cycle.

LEWIS: And the effect is strongest where credit risk is greatest. For a portfolio of government bonds, there is little spread to move, and analytical duration remains the right tool. For a portfolio of credit sensitive bonds, especially high yield bonds, empirical duration is often the better guide, and it can sit far below the analytical figure.

HEART: The trap. A question asks which bond's empirical duration is most likely to fall well below its analytical duration. Long maturity is the tempting answer. The right answer is the bond with the most credit risk, because the gap comes from spreads moving against benchmark yields, not from maturity.

LEWIS: The takeaway. Bonds with embedded options need effective duration and effective convexity, measured against shifts in a benchmark curve with a model that lets the option respond. Callable bonds can turn negatively convex at low yields, while putable bonds stay positively convex. Key rate durations add up to effective duration and reveal shaping risk. And empirical duration, drawn from market history, tends to run below analytical duration for credit sensitive bonds.

## Recall check

LEWIS Q: You rely on duration alone to predict the price change of an option free bond after a large move in yields. Is the predicted loss from a rise too big or too small, and the predicted gain from a fall?

HEART: Answer. The predicted loss is too big and the predicted gain is too small. Here is why. The true price yield curve of an option free bond is convex, so it sits above duration's straight line on both sides of today's yield. Whichever way yields move, the real price ends up above the line. The gap grows with the size of the move, which is why convexity matters most for big shocks.

HEART Q: The change in yield doubles in size. What happens to the duration effect, and what happens to the convexity adjustment?

LEWIS: Answer. The duration effect doubles and the convexity adjustment quadruples. Here is why. The duration effect moves in step with the yield change, while the convexity adjustment depends on the yield change squared. Squaring a number twice as large gives four times the result. That is why convexity is a rounding error for tiny moves and a major correction for large shocks.

LEWIS Q: Two option free bonds share the same price, yield and duration, but one has more convexity. Which would you rather own, and does it make money when yields rise?

HEART: Answer. The more convex bond, but no, it still loses money when yields rise, just less. Here is why. With more curvature, its price sits further above the straight line on both sides. So it gains more when yields fall and loses less when they rise. Convexity improves the result relative to duration in both directions, without changing the direction of the move.

HEART Q: A five year zero coupon bond and a barbell of two year and thirty year bonds have the same duration. Which has more convexity?

LEWIS: Answer. The barbell. Here is why. A payment's convexity grows roughly with the square of its time, so the distant thirty year payments add far more convexity than the nearby two year payments take away. Spreading cash flows around the same center raises convexity, a property called dispersion. The zero has all its cash on one date, so it has the least convexity possible for its duration.

LEWIS Q: Portfolio duration is computed as a weighted average of the bonds' durations. What are the weights, and what does the result assume about the yield curve?

HEART: Answer. Each bond's full price market value as a share of the portfolio's value, and the result assumes a parallel shift. Here is why. A weighted average can only describe the whole portfolio if every holding feels the same change in yield. When short and long yields move by different amounts, each bond's price responds to its own move, and a single blended number misjudges the total. The theoretically correct alternative works from the combined cash flows, but the weighted average is the one used in practice.

HEART Q: Why is modified duration the wrong measure for a callable bond, and what does effective duration shift and hold constant instead?

LEWIS: Answer. Its cash flows change with rates, so it has no single well defined yield, and effective duration instead shifts a benchmark curve, such as the government par curve, while holding the bond's spread constant. Here is why. Modified duration assumes a fixed payment schedule, which a call can cut short. Effective duration reprices the bond with a model that decides when the call gets exercised at each new level of rates, so it captures how the option changes the price response.

LEWIS Q: Benchmark yields have fallen well below a callable bond's coupon. Is its convexity likely positive or negative, and how does its duration compare with an otherwise identical option free bond?

HEART: Answer. Likely negative, and its duration is shorter. Here is why. With the call likely, nobody pays much above the call price, so the price stalls as yields fall further. It gains less from a fall than it loses from an equal rise, which is negative convexity. And a price that responds less to falling rates means a shorter duration than the straight bond's at low yields.

HEART Q: What is the sign of a putable bond's convexity, and is its duration shorter than an otherwise identical option free bond's at high yields or at low yields?

LEWIS: Answer. Always positive, and its duration is shorter at high yields. Here is why. The holder bought the put, so as yields climb, the right to sell the bond back at the put price puts a floor under its value. The bond falls less than its straight twin, which shortens its duration exactly when rates are high. An option you own only adds protection, so convexity never turns negative.

LEWIS Q: Rates rise. Why can a mortgage backed security lose more value than its duration suggested?

HEART: Answer. Because prepayments slow and its expected life stretches out. Here is why. Homeowners rarely refinance into a higher rate, so principal that was expected back early stays out longer, earning a below market rate. A longer life means a longer duration just as rates are rising, so losses deepen. That is extension risk, one side of the negative convexity that mortgages carry.

HEART Q: For an option free bond, when do modified duration and effective duration match exactly?

LEWIS: Answer. Only when the yield curve is flat. Here is why. Effective duration shifts the government par curve, and par yields are blends of spot rates. On a sloped curve, a parallel shift in par yields means the spot rates move by slightly different amounts. A bond priced at a constant spread over those spot rates sees its own yield move by a slightly different amount, so the two measures drift a little apart.

LEWIS Q: What do a portfolio's key rate durations add up to, and what risk do they reveal that a single duration misses?

HEART: Answer. They add up to the portfolio's effective duration, and they reveal shaping risk. Here is why. A parallel shift is every key rate moving together, so the separate sensitivities sum to the whole. But when the curve steepens, flattens or twists, each maturity moves differently, and only the maturity by maturity breakdown shows where the exposure sits. Two portfolios with the same duration can react very differently to the same twist.

HEART Q: In a flight to quality, is the empirical duration of a high yield bond portfolio likely above, equal to or below its analytical duration?

LEWIS: Answer. Below it. Here is why. Analytical duration assumes the credit spread stays put while benchmark yields move. In a flight to quality, government yields fall while credit spreads widen, so the spread offsets part of the benchmark move. The bonds rise less than the formula predicts, or even fall, and history captures that while the formula does not.

## Key takeaways

HEART: First, a few memory hooks.

LEWIS: Duration is the slope, convexity is the bend. Duration is the first order effect, convexity the second.

HEART: For an option free bond, picture the ruler against the bowl. The straight line sits below the curve on both sides, so duration alone understates gains and overstates losses.

LEWIS: Squared means same sign. The convexity adjustment depends on the squared yield change, so it points the same way for a rise or a fall, and doubling the move quadruples it. Never drop the one half.

HEART: To annualize, divide duration by the periods per year and convexity by that number squared, which is four for a semiannual bond.

LEWIS: Gap for slope, sum for bend. Approximate duration works from the gap between the down price and the up price. Approximate convexity works from their sum against twice today's price.

HEART: For more convexity: long, low, low and spread out. Longer maturity, lower coupon, lower yield and more dispersed cash flows, with the same rare maturity exception as duration. At the same duration, a barbell beats a bullet and a zero has the least. At the same maturity, a zero has the most.

LEWIS: For options: bought option, convexity stays positive. Sold option, convexity can turn negative. Each option shortens duration when it is likely to be exercised, the call at low yields and the put at high yields.

HEART: For mortgages: rates fall, borrowers refinance and the life shrinks. Rates rise, prepayments slow and the life stretches. Contraction then extension, gain less then lose more.

LEWIS: For portfolios: a market value weighted average of durations holds only for a parallel shift.

HEART: For curves: effective duration shifts the benchmark, holds the spread and lets a model decide on the option. Key rates are the rooms, effective duration is the whole house, and the rooms add up to the house.

LEWIS: For empirical duration: sticker versus road. Analytical is the sticker, empirical is what history delivered, and for credit sensitive bonds the road figure tends to run lower.

HEART: Now, how you would say the big ideas to a senior leader.

LEWIS: On convexity: bond prices do not move in a straight line with rates. For plain bonds, the curve works in our favor, softening losses and boosting gains, and it matters most in big moves. The jargon is positive convexity and the convexity adjustment.

HEART: On mortgages and callable bonds: these assets work against us both ways. Our money comes back early when rates fall and stays out longer when rates rise. The jargon is negative convexity, contraction risk and extension risk.

LEWIS: On portfolio risk: one duration number assumes every rate moves together. When short and long rates move differently, we look at our exposure maturity by maturity. The jargon is a parallel shift, key rate duration and shaping risk.

HEART: On bonds with options: for anything with an option, we measure rate risk by shifting the government curve and letting a model decide when the option gets exercised. The jargon is effective duration and effective convexity.

LEWIS: And on credit: in a scare, Treasury yields fall but credit spreads widen, so our credit sensitive holdings gain less than the formulas say, and history is the better guide. The jargon is empirical duration versus analytical duration, and a flight to quality.
