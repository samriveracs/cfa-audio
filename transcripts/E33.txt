LEWIS: Reading 63 covers why lenders turn loans into bonds, who gains from it and the parties to a deal, and Reading 64 covers covered bonds, credit enhancement, asset backed securities built on credit cards and solar loans, and collateralized debt obligations. Reading 65 covers prepayment risk and why it creates negative convexity, the features of home loans, agency and non agency securities, pass throughs, collateralized mortgage obligations and commercial mortgage backed securities.

## Module 63.1, Fixed-Income Securitization

HEART: Picture a credit union that makes thirty year fixed rate mortgages and funds them with member deposits. Every new loan can tie up money for decades. Sooner or later the lender runs short of funding, or of capital, and has to slow down, even though members keep asking for loans. Securitization is the way out. The lender sells a pool of its loans, gets cash today, and lends that cash again.

LEWIS: The process runs in three steps. First, a bank, or a company that extends credit to its customers, builds a pool of loans. That lender is the originator, and the pool is called the collateral. Second, the originator sells the pool to a separate legal entity created just for the deal, called a special purpose entity. Third, the special purpose entity issues bonds whose payments come from the borrowers' payments on those loans. Those bonds are asset backed securities, or ABS. When the loans are mortgages, they are mortgage backed securities, or MBS.

HEART: The buyers are investors such as pension funds and bond funds. And notice what has changed. Before the deal, the bank stood in the middle, taking deposits on one side and lending on the other. After the deal, the borrower's monthly payment flows almost straight through to an investor. In effect, investors now fund the borrowers directly, and the originating lender is no longer the middleman holding the loan.

LEWIS: One vocabulary trap before the benefits. The word issuer gets two jobs here. When a question asks about the benefits of securitization to issuers, it means the originator, the bank or company that made the loans. But anywhere else in a securitization, the issuer means the special purpose entity, which actually issues the bonds.

HEART: Now, who benefits. Start with the originator, which gains in four ways. First, more business. The cash from the sale funds new loans, so the lender can make more loans than its own balance sheet could carry. Second, better profitability. The originator earns fees for making the loans in the first place and for arranging their sale. Third, lower capital requirements. Selling the loans moves their credit risk off the balance sheet, so regulators require less capital to be held against it. Fourth, liquidity. A pile of loans is hard to sell one by one. Securitization turns it into cash.

LEWIS: Investors gain in three ways. The first is risk and return tailored to their needs. One pool of loans can be cut so that a cautious insurer buys a short, top rated piece, while a fund hunting for yield buys a riskier piece of the same pool that pays more. The second is access. Making and collecting thousands of loans takes underwriters and a collections team, and an asset backed security delivers the exposure without any of that. The third is liquidity. A bond trades far more easily than the loans behind it, so investors can react quickly when markets change.

HEART: The economy and financial markets gain too. Liquidity risk goes down, because tradable securities replace loans that could hardly be sold. Market efficiency improves, because more trading pushes prices toward equilibrium, a fair reflection of what investors currently think. And originators can raise money more cheaply than by issuing their own bonds or shares, and grow without piling more debt onto their balance sheets, which keeps their leverage lower.

LEWIS: Then the other side of the ledger. For investors, two risks come with the territory. First, the cash flows are uncertain in both size and timing, because borrowers may repay early or late. Second, the credit risk of the loans passes straight through to the investors. And when that risk piles up across the whole financial system, the damage spreads. The global financial crisis of two thousand seven to two thousand nine showed exactly that.

HEART: Here is the trap. Securitization does not make credit risk disappear. It moves it from the lender's balance sheet to the bondholders.

LEWIS: Now the parties and the roles they play. Picture a furniture chain that lets customers pay for a sofa over three years. Over time it builds up a large book of these installment contracts, and it wants the cash back so it can keep selling on credit.

HEART: The furniture chain is the originator, and in the deal it is called the seller or the depositor, because it sells, or deposits, the loans into the structure. The buyer is the special purpose entity, which you will also hear called a special purpose vehicle, a special purpose company or simply the trust. It buys the loans and issues the bonds, so it is the issuer.

LEWIS: Then someone has to keep collecting from the customers. That is the servicer. It collects monthly principal and interest, sends late notices, and when a borrower stops paying, it repossesses the collateral and sells it. The servicer keeps a fee, and the rest of the cash goes to the bondholders. Often the originator keeps the servicing job, so the furniture chain might be both seller and servicer, but the servicer can be a different firm. A credit union that sells a pool of home loans often keeps the servicing, so its members keep paying the credit union just as before.

HEART: One more party. A trustee is appointed to safeguard the collateral and the cash due to bondholders, and to keep the bondholders informed. Think of the trustee as the referee who holds the money and makes sure everyone follows the deal's rules.

LEWIS: Now the most important structural idea in this reading, bankruptcy remoteness. The special purpose entity is a separate legal entity from the originator, and the loans are sold to it outright. Practitioners call that a true sale. So if the furniture chain later gets into financial trouble, even bankruptcy, its creditors cannot reach the loans. The loans belong to the trust, and the cash from them goes to the bondholders.

HEART: Think of selling your car to a neighbor. Once the title changes hands, your creditors cannot take that car from your neighbor, however badly your own finances go. The special purpose entity is the neighbor.

LEWIS: That cuts both ways, though. Asset backed security holders have no claim on the originator's other assets, only on the collateral sold to the trust. A lender to the chain itself, say a buyer of its corporate bonds, is in the opposite position and rises or falls with the company.

HEART: And the trap. Bankruptcy remote does not mean risk free. Investors trade the originator's credit risk for the credit risk of the borrowers in the pool. If the furniture buyers stop paying, the bondholders lose money even while the furniture chain is thriving.

LEWIS: Last, the paperwork. Beyond the bond indenture and its covenants, two documents matter. The purchase agreement sets the terms on which the special purpose entity buys the collateral from the seller. The prospectus sets out the terms of the securitization, including the fees paid to the servicer and other administrators, and exactly how cash from the collateral is divided among the investors.

HEART: The takeaway. Securitization moves loans from the originator into a bankruptcy remote special purpose entity, which issues asset backed securities paid from the loans' cash flows. The originator gets cash, fees and capital relief, investors get tailored exposure and liquidity, and credit risk moves to the investors rather than disappearing.

## Module 64.1, Asset-Backed Security (ABS) Instrument and Market Features

LEWIS: Start with a cousin of asset backed securities, the covered bond. A covered bond is a senior debt obligation of a bank or other financial institution, backed by a segregated pool of assets called the cover pool. The pool is usually mortgages, and the crucial difference is that no special purpose entity is created. The cover pool is fenced off for the bondholders but stays on the issuing bank's balance sheet. Covered bonds come mainly from European, Asian and Australian banks.

HEART: Because the assets stay put, covered bondholders get dual recourse. If the bank defaults, they have a claim on the cover pool, and they also have a claim on the bank's other assets that have not been pledged to anyone else, called unencumbered assets. An asset backed security holder can look only to the pool.

LEWIS: It works much like a secured advance from a Federal Home Loan Bank. The bank or credit union pledges mortgages as collateral, but the mortgages stay on its books and it still owes the money in full. A covered bond is a way of borrowing, not a sale.

HEART: Several safeguards support it. The mortgages in the cover pool face limits on their loan to value ratios. The pool is usually worth more than the bonds issued against it, which is called overcollateralization. And a third party monitors the pool to make sure those conditions hold.

LEWIS: Then two structural differences. A typical asset backed security's pool is fixed when the deal is issued, and nobody has to replace a loan that goes bad. A cover pool is dynamic. If a loan stops performing or is prepaid, the issuer has to replace it or add more assets, so the pool always supports the promised payments. And covered bonds typically are not split into credit tranches.

HEART: Add it up, and covered bonds generally yield less than comparable asset backed securities. And because prepaid loans are replaced, covered bond investors face much less prepayment risk than investors in a mortgage pass through. The issuing bank gives something up in return. With the cover pool still on its balance sheet, it gets none of the capital relief a true securitization can bring.

LEWIS: Covered bonds also differ in what happens if the issuer misses a payment. A hard bullet covered bond is a firm deadline. Miss a scheduled payment and the bond is in default, and payments to the covered bondholders are accelerated, meaning they come due at once.

HEART: A soft bullet covered bond has a grace period. If a payment is missed, the original maturity date can be pushed back, by as much as a year, which postpones the default and the acceleration that would follow. And a conditional pass through covered bond changes form. If any payments are still owed on the maturity date, it converts into a pass through bond, and from then on investors receive whatever the cover pool brings in, as it comes in.

LEWIS: The trap is to hear a soft bullet as forgiveness. It is a delay, not a write off. Now credit enhancement, the features that protect bondholders against defaults in the pool. They come in two families. Internal credit enhancement is built into the structure of the deal itself. External credit enhancement comes from a third party, such as bond insurance, a bank's letter of credit or a private guarantee, and it is only as strong as the firm behind it.

HEART: Internal enhancement takes three main forms. The first is overcollateralization, which means the collateral is worth more than the face value of the bonds issued against it. Picture a pawnshop that lends you less than your watch is worth. If you never come back, there is room to absorb a loss. In a deal, defaults eat into that extra collateral before any bondholder loses a dollar.

LEWIS: Excess spread works on income rather than principal. The loans in the pool earn more interest than the deal needs to cover its bondholders' coupons and its fees. That surplus builds up as a reserve that can absorb credit losses. Think of a landlord whose rent comes in above the mortgage payment every month. The leftover goes into a repair fund, so when the boiler breaks, the mortgage still gets paid.

HEART: The third form is subordination, and it is the one the exam leans on most. The deal issues several classes of bonds, called tranches, each with a different priority claim on the collateral's cash flows. Senior tranches sit at the top. Subordinated tranches sit beneath them. The lowest tranche has a residual claim on whatever is left once everyone above it is paid, so it is often called the equity tranche.

LEWIS: Losses work from the bottom up. When borrowers default, the most junior tranche absorbs the losses first, up to its full principal. Only once it is wiped out do losses reach the next tranche up. The senior tranche loses nothing until every tranche beneath it is exhausted. So the more subordinated bonds there are underneath, the more protection the senior tranche has.

HEART: That is why the structure is called a waterfall. Picture a tower of champagne glasses. Pour into the top glass, and only when it is full does the overflow spill into the glasses below. In a liquidation, each subordinated tranche receives only the overflow, after the tranches above it have been repaid their principal in full.

LEWIS: A company's balance sheet works the same way. Senior lenders are paid before junior lenders, and shareholders take the first loss.

HEART: The ratings and yields follow. The senior tranche, with the most protection, gets the highest credit rating and offers the lowest yield. The junior tranches offer higher yields to pay for absorbing the first losses. And here is a point the exam likes. Thanks to subordination together with bankruptcy remoteness, a senior tranche can carry a higher credit rating than the company that originated the loans.

LEWIS: One mechanical detail. Each tranche keeps earning its coupon as long as it is outstanding, but the coupon is paid on whatever face value remains. If losses write down part of a tranche's principal, its interest payments shrink in proportion.

HEART: And the trap. Tranching does not reduce the total credit risk in the pool. It decides who bears it first. A pool that loses money loses the same amount however the tranches are stacked.

LEWIS: Now the kinds of asset backed securities. Almost anything that throws off a stream of cash can be securitized: business loans, accounts receivable, auto loans, even music royalties or franchise fees.

HEART: The first question to ask about any collateral is whether it amortizes. An amortizing loan, like a car loan or a mortgage, has a schedule that pays the principal down over its life. A nonamortizing loan has no schedule for repaying principal along the way. On a credit card, the borrower decides when to pay it down.

LEWIS: Auto loan deals show the amortizing pattern. Investors receive interest, scheduled principal and prepayments, which come when a car is sold, traded in or wrecked and paid off by insurance. Car loans are small and short, so refinancing one saves little, and their prepayments respond much less to interest rates than mortgage prepayments do.

HEART: Credit card receivables are the classic nonamortizing collateral. Credit card asset backed securities are backed by the balances cardholders owe to banks, retailers, travel and entertainment companies and other card issuers. The pool's cash flows are finance charges, which is the interest, plus fees such as membership and late fees, plus principal, which cardholders repay whenever they choose. The rates on the cards can be fixed or floating, usually with a cap on how high they can go.

LEWIS: Here is the feature that defines these deals, the lockout period, also called the revolving period. During the lockout, investors receive only the interest and fees from the pool. When cardholders repay principal, that money is not passed to investors. It buys new card receivables, so the pool stays roughly the same size.

HEART: Picture a reservoir with water running out the bottom and fresh water pumped in at the top, so the level holds steady. Because repaid principal is simply reinvested, investors face no prepayment risk during the revolving period. When the lockout ends, the amortization period begins, and principal flows through to the bondholders.

LEWIS: And the trap. These deals usually include an early amortization provision, also called rapid amortization. If the credit quality of the pool weakens, principal starts flowing to investors early to protect them, a circuit breaker that stops the recycling once the pool's performance slips past a set level. So an answer that says investors can never receive principal before the scheduled end of the lockout is wrong.

HEART: The second type is newer, the solar asset backed security, backed by loans to homeowners to install solar panels and cut their energy bills. The loans come from specialist finance companies or from the financing arms of solar companies. Solar deals appeal to investors focused on environmental, social and governance factors, or ESG, because their money helps households switch to renewable energy. The loans can be secured on the solar equipment itself, or on the house as a junior mortgage behind the main one, which gives investors extra security. Internal enhancements like overcollateralization, excess spread and subordination are common.

LEWIS: Credit risk is likely to be low, because the borrowers usually have good credit scores and are saving money on their energy bills. But the asset class is young, and nobody has yet seen how it holds up over a full credit cycle. Many solar deals also have a pre funding period, a set time after the bonds are sold during which the trust can keep buying new solar loans with the money raised, to build a bigger and more diversified pool.

HEART: The last structure in this reading is the collateralized debt obligation, or CDO, a structured security issued by a special purpose entity and backed by a pool of debt obligations. When the collateral is corporate bonds and emerging market debt, it is called a collateralized bond obligation. When the collateral is leveraged bank loans, meaning senior secured loans to companies that already carry heavy debt or have a weak credit record, it is called a collateralized loan obligation.

LEWIS: The defining feature of a CDO is the collateral manager. A typical asset backed security relies on a fixed pool. The CDO's manager actively buys and sells the collateral to generate the cash owed to investors, more like a managed fund than a fixed basket. The tranches look familiar: senior, subordinated and equity. And the deal only works if the equity tranche can earn an attractive return after the debt tranches receive the yields they require. That is leverage, and it cuts both ways. The equity tranche is thin, so a modest rise in defaults can wipe it out.

HEART: The crisis reshaped this market. The complex collateralized debt obligations built on mixed collateral largely disappeared, the rules on collateral quality tightened, and leveraged loans now make up most of the collateral. That makes the collateralized loan obligation the CDO you will meet most often. These come in three main types. In a cash flow structure, investors are paid from the interest and principal the loans generate. In a market value structure, investors are paid by trading the collateral and capturing changes in its market value. In a synthetic structure, the credit exposure comes from credit derivative contracts, and the trust does not own the loans at all.

LEWIS: And to protect investors, the collateral must pass a set of tests written into the deal. Cash flows from the collateral must cover what is owed to investors. Each tranche must keep a minimum level of overcollateralization, and if a limit is breached, cash is redirected to buy more collateral or to pay down the most senior tranches. The pool must stay diversified. And holdings of debt rated triple C must stay under a limit.

HEART: The takeaway. Covered bonds keep the assets on the issuer's balance sheet, with dual recourse and a pool that gets topped up, so they yield less than comparable asset backed securities. Internal credit enhancement means overcollateralization, excess spread and subordination, with losses climbing from the bottom up. Credit card deals revolve before they amortize, and a CDO adds an active manager.

## Module 65.1, Mortgage-Backed Security (MBS) Instrument and Market Features

LEWIS: Mortgages carry a risk that most bonds do not. A prepayment is any principal a borrower repays beyond the scheduled amount, mostly because the owner sells the house or refinances the loan. The price of a mortgage backed security rests on an assumed prepayment speed, and prepayment risk is the risk that the actual speed turns out different from what investors expected when they bought.

HEART: It comes in two directions. Contraction risk is the risk that prepayments come faster than expected, so cash comes back sooner. Extension risk is the risk that they come slower, so investors wait longer for their money. Interest rates are the main driver. When rates fall, homeowners refinance into cheaper loans, prepayments speed up, and the security's average life shrinks. Average life means, roughly, how long on average until each dollar of principal comes back.

LEWIS: That hurts investors in two ways. First, the money comes back just when rates are low, so it can only be reinvested at lower returns. Second, the price does not rise as much as an ordinary bond's would. The market already expects heavy prepayments when rates are low, so the price is held back, much as nobody pays far above the call price for a bond that is likely to be called.

HEART: When rates rise, the reverse happens. Few people refinance into a higher rate, so prepayments slow down and the average life extends. That is bad too. Investors are stuck longer with a below market rate, and each payment now arrives further in the future and is discounted at a higher rate, so the price falls by more than it would if the payments had stayed on schedule.

LEWIS: That should sound familiar from the convexity episode. A mortgage backed security is like a callable bond. Every borrower holds a call option, the right to pay off the loan early at par. And the borrower exercises it when it suits the borrower, not the investor.

HEART: So put the two directions together. When rates fall, the security gains less than its duration predicts. When rates rise, it loses more. Gains get capped, losses do not. That is negative convexity, and for a mortgage backed security it shows up at low yields, where the refinancing option is most valuable and the price rises more and more slowly as yields fall.

LEWIS: A lender holding fixed rate mortgages feels both sides. When rates drop, the best yielding loans refinance away. When rates rise, borrowers hold on to their cheap mortgages, and those assets stay on the books just as funding costs go up.

HEART: The market's answer is not to remove the risk but to reallocate it, with time tranching. A deal issues several classes of bonds that pay off at different times. Neither risk goes away. Each class just takes a different share of both. The tranches that pay off first protect investors against extension risk, and the later ones give relatively more protection against contraction risk.

LEWIS: A residential mortgage is a loan whose collateral is residential real estate, a home. If the borrower defaults, the lender has a legal claim on the property, called a first lien when no other lender ranks ahead of it, and can take possession of it and sell it to recover its losses. That process is foreclosure. Securities backed by these loans are called residential mortgage backed securities.

HEART: A few features of these loans matter to the investor. The first is a prepayment penalty, an extra payment a borrower must make for repaying early, to compensate the lender when rates have fallen. Prepayment penalties are common in Europe and rare in the United States.

LEWIS: The second feature is whether the loan is recourse or nonrecourse. With a nonrecourse loan, the lender can look only to the property. With a recourse loan, if a foreclosure sale falls short of what is owed, the lender can also go after the borrower's other assets for the shortfall.

HEART: That matters most when a house is underwater, worth less than the mortgage on it, also called negative equity. A borrower with a nonrecourse loan has a reason to walk away, accept the damage to the credit score, and stop paying on a debt larger than the house is worth. That is strategic default. Recourse makes it far less tempting, because the debt follows the borrower.

LEWIS: In Europe, most home loans are recourse loans. In the United States, it depends on the state, and nonrecourse treatment is common. Next, two ratios that measure default risk. The loan to value ratio is the share of the property's value that has been lent. A lower ratio means more borrower equity, which makes the loan safer in two ways. The borrower has more to lose, so is less likely to default, and if the lender must sell, the property is more likely to cover the loan.

HEART: The debt to income ratio compares the borrower's monthly debt payments with monthly income before taxes. Lower means the payments take a smaller bite out of each paycheck, so the borrower is a lower default risk. In the United States, loans to borrowers with good credit, a low loan to value ratio and a low debt to income ratio are called prime loans. Loans to borrowers with weaker credit, higher ratios, or a lower priority claim on the property, such as a second mortgage, are called subprime loans.

LEWIS: Now, who stands behind the securities. In the United States, the big distinction is agency versus non agency. Agency residential mortgage backed securities carry a guarantee either from the federal government or from a government sponsored enterprise, a company created by the government.

HEART: And the distinction within that is a favorite trap. A government sponsored enterprise is technically separate from the government. So securities it guarantees carry only the enterprise's own guarantee, not the full faith and credit of the government. Ginnie Mae's guarantee does carry the full faith and credit of the United States. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac are government sponsored enterprises, so their guarantee is their own. Both have operated under federal conservatorship since two thousand eight, with a Treasury support agreement behind them, but that is still not full faith and credit.

LEWIS: To go into an agency pool, a mortgage has to meet minimum underwriting standards, on things like loan size, loan to value ratio and documentation. Loans that meet them are called conforming loans. Because of the guarantee, an agency investor worries far less about default, so the main risk left is prepayment.

HEART: Plenty of credit unions and community banks work this way. They make conforming mortgages, sell them into agency pools, keep the servicing, and buy agency securities for their own portfolios.

LEWIS: Non agency residential mortgage backed securities, sometimes called private label, are issued by private entities such as banks, with no government or enterprise guarantee. They can hold loans that do not conform, from jumbo loans too large for the agencies to riskier ones, including subprime loans. So they lean on credit enhancement, both internal, like tranching, and external, like bond insurance, letters of credit and private guarantees.

HEART: These were at the center of the global financial crisis. Many of their top slices carried investment grade ratings yet rested on subprime loans, and when those borrowers defaulted in large numbers, even the top slices lost heavily. Issuance then collapsed, and under tighter regulation it has stayed a small fraction of its pre crisis peak.

LEWIS: Now the securities themselves. The basic one is the mortgage pass through security. It is a claim on the cash flows from a pool of mortgages, after administrative fees. Each mortgage in the pool is called a securitized mortgage. Every investor owns a proportional share, so every month each one receives the same slice of the pool's interest, scheduled principal and prepayments.

HEART: The loans in a pool have different rates and different maturities, so two averages describe the pool. The weighted average maturity is the average of the loans' remaining terms to final maturity. The weighted average coupon is the average of their mortgage rates. Both are weighted by each loan's current outstanding balance as a share of the pool's total.

LEWIS: The trap is the weights. They are current balances, not original loan amounts, so a loan that has been paid down a long way counts for less.

HEART: Then the pass through rate, which is the coupon investors actually receive, also called the net interest or net coupon. It is lower than the weighted average coupon, because servicing fees and guarantee or insurance fees come out first. Borrowers pay the mortgage rate, and investors receive what is left after the servicer and the guarantor are paid.

LEWIS: Because every pass through investor gets the same slice of every prepayment, no investor can choose which side of prepayment risk to hold. A bank funded with short term deposits mostly fears extension. An insurer with long liabilities mostly fears contraction.

HEART: A collateralized mortgage obligation, or CMO, fills that gap. It is backed by pass throughs and pools of mortgages, and it issues several tranches with different exposures to prepayment risk. Think of one pie, cut into slices for different appetites. The core rule is that a CMO does not change the total prepayment risk of the mortgages. It reapportions it, so whatever one tranche is shielded from, another absorbs. Matching the slices to what investors want widens the market for mortgages, and that may lower funding costs for the lenders who make them.

LEWIS: The simplest structure is sequential pay. Every tranche receives interest on its own outstanding balance, but all the principal, scheduled payments and prepayments alike, goes to the first tranche until it is paid off, then to the second, and so on down the line. Picture someone paying off several credit cards. They pay the interest due on every card, but every extra dollar goes to one card until it is gone, and only then to the next.

HEART: That gives each tranche a different risk profile. The first tranche has the shortest expected life and gets every dollar of principal before anyone else, so it is the best protected against extension risk. But when prepayments surge, it absorbs the surge first, so it carries relatively more contraction risk. The last tranche is the mirror image. Faster prepayments go first to the tranches ahead of it, which gives it relatively more protection against contraction, but if prepayments slow, it waits the longest, so it carries the most extension risk.

LEWIS: The more refined structure pairs a planned amortization class tranche with a support tranche, also called a companion tranche. The planned amortization class tranche follows a set principal schedule, making predictable payments as long as prepayment speeds stay within a set range. The support tranche is the shock absorber. When prepayments run fast, it receives the principal beyond what the planned amortization class is scheduled to get, so it absorbs contraction risk. When prepayments run slow, its principal is cut back so the scheduled payments can still be made, so it absorbs extension risk too.

HEART: So the planned amortization class tranche has less of both risks, and the support tranche has more of both, which is why it offers a higher yield. And the trap. The protection only holds while prepayment speeds stay within the range the support tranche can handle. If prepayments run so fast that the support tranche is paid off entirely, the planned amortization class tranche can no longer stay on its schedule.

LEWIS: Then the specialized pieces. A Z tranche, also called an accrual or accretion bond, receives no cash interest during a set accrual period. Instead, its interest is added to its principal, so its balance grows, and only afterward does it start receiving cash interest and principal. It usually sits at the bottom of the order.

HEART: Some tranches split interest from principal. A principal only tranche, or P O, receives only principal, so it is effectively a zero coupon security bought at a discount. The sooner the principal comes back, the higher its annualized return, so falling rates and faster prepayments help it. An interest only tranche, or I O, receives only the interest on whatever principal is still outstanding. Faster prepayments shrink that principal and cut off future interest, so falling rates hurt it, and slower prepayments help it.

LEWIS: A floating rate tranche pays a coupon tied to a market reference rate, often with a cap and a floor. Its twin, the inverse floater, pays a coupon that rises when rates fall and falls when rates rise. And a residual tranche ranks below every other tranche in the CMO, much like the equity tranche of an asset backed security.

HEART: Last, commercial mortgage backed securities, backed by mortgages on income producing property: apartment buildings, which the industry calls multifamily, warehouses and other industrial property, shopping centers, offices, health care facilities such as senior housing, and hotels. A commercial pool usually holds far fewer loans, sometimes just one big loan on a single landmark building, so the pool is far less diversified and one default can do real damage. A deal can hold loans from one lender, or several loans to one borrower. American deals usually pay fixed coupons. In Europe, where the market is growing, coupons are typically floating.

LEWIS: The bigger difference is who repays the loan. A homeowner repays out of a paycheck. A commercial loan is repaid by a real estate investor, who depends on tenants, mostly businesses, and on those tenants' customers. The regular income from the pool's mortgages is called the weighted average proceeds from the mortgages, and it plays the role the weighted average coupon plays in a residential pool. And because commercial mortgages are usually nonrecourse, the analysis centers on the credit risk of the property. Judge the building, not the borrower.

HEART: Two ratios carry that analysis. The debt service coverage ratio compares the property's net operating income with its debt service, the scheduled principal and interest. Net operating income is the rent, less cash operating expenses and less a noncash replacement reserve for the building wearing out over time. A higher ratio means more protection for the lender. The loan to value ratio compares the current loan with the property's current appraised value, and a higher ratio means more default risk.

LEWIS: Then call protection, meaning protection against early repayment of principal, which comes at two levels. At the loan level, three tools are worth knowing. A prepayment lockout forbids prepaying for a set period, typically two to five years. Prepayment penalty points charge a fee, each point equal to one percent of the principal prepaid. And with defeasance, the borrower does not pay the loan off. It buys a portfolio of government securities big enough to make every remaining scheduled payment, so the lender keeps receiving exactly what it expected, and the borrower can have the lien released and sell the building.

HEART: At the structure level, the pool can be tranched sequentially, like a CMO. Principal, early payoffs included, goes to the highest priority tranches first, so the lower priority tranches waiting behind them get the call protection. Losses travel the other way. Commercial deals are credit tranched as well, with losses absorbed by the lowest priority tranches first.

LEWIS: Finally, the balloon. Commercial mortgages are typically not fully amortizing, so a large chunk of principal is still owed at the end of the term, the balloon payment, and the borrower usually has to refinance to pay it. If refinancing fails, the borrower is in default. That possibility is balloon risk. The lender may extend the loan during a workout period on modified terms, and either way the investor waits longer for principal. That is why balloon risk is a form of extension risk.

HEART: The takeaway. Prepayment risk runs two ways, contraction when rates fall and extension when rates rise, which gives mortgage securities negative convexity. Agency securities carry a guarantee and hold conforming loans, non agency ones rely on credit enhancement, a CMO reshuffles prepayment risk without reducing it, and commercial deals are judged on the property.

## Recall check

LEWIS Q: A lender sells a pool of loans to a special purpose entity and later goes bankrupt. Are the investors in the asset backed securities hurt by the bankruptcy?

HEART: Answer. Not by the bankruptcy itself, but they still bear the borrowers' credit risk. Here is why. The special purpose entity is a separate legal entity that bought the loans outright, so the originator's creditors cannot reach them. But the investors' only claim is on the pool, so if the borrowers default, they lose money whatever happens to the originator.

HEART Q: Compared with an asset backed security backed by similar mortgages, why does a covered bond usually pay a lower yield?

LEWIS: Answer. Because covered bondholders have dual recourse and a pool that is overcollateralized and topped up. Here is why. If the issuer defaults, covered bondholders can claim the cover pool and the bank's unencumbered assets, while asset backed security holders can claim only their pool. The issuer must also replace loans that go bad or prepay. More protection means less credit risk, so investors accept a lower yield.

LEWIS Q: A securitization has senior, subordinated and equity tranches, and the pool suffers losses. Which tranche absorbs them first, and has the total credit risk of the pool gone down?

HEART: Answer. The equity tranche absorbs them first, and no, the total credit risk is unchanged. Here is why. Losses climb from the bottom up, so each junior tranche must be exhausted before the one above it loses anything. That makes the senior tranche safer, but the pool loses exactly the same amount however the tranches are stacked. Tranching moves risk between investors, it does not remove it.

HEART Q: During the lockout period of a credit card asset backed security, what happens when cardholders repay principal, and do investors face prepayment risk then?

LEWIS: Answer. The principal buys new receivables, so investors receive only interest and fees and face no prepayment risk. Here is why. Repaid balances are recycled into new card balances, which keeps the pool about the same size, so nothing is returned to investors early. Principal reaches investors in the amortization period, or sooner if weakening credit triggers the early amortization provision.

LEWIS Q: What most sets a collateralized debt obligation apart from a typical asset backed security?

HEART: Answer. A collateral manager actively buys and sells the collateral. Here is why. A typical asset backed security relies on a fixed pool of loans set when the deal is issued. The CDO's manager trades the pool to generate the cash owed to investors, within tests on coverage, overcollateralization, diversification and triple C holdings.

HEART Q: Mortgage rates fall sharply. Which prepayment risk shows up, and why does a mortgage backed security gain less than an option free bond?

LEWIS: Answer. Contraction risk, and the borrowers' option to prepay caps the price gain. Here is why. Lower rates set off a wave of refinancing, so principal comes back early, just when it can only be reinvested at lower rates. Investors will not pay much extra for high coupons that are likely to vanish, so the price rises less than duration predicts. Gaining less when rates fall and losing more when they rise is negative convexity.

LEWIS Q: An agency mortgage security is guaranteed by a government sponsored enterprise. Does it carry the full faith and credit of the United States government?

HEART: Answer. No, only the enterprise's own guarantee. Here is why. A government sponsored enterprise is a company created by the government but technically separate from it. Only a guarantee from the government itself, such as Ginnie Mae's, carries full faith and credit. Either way, agency investors bear little credit risk, so their main concern is prepayment.

HEART Q: In a sequential pay CMO, which tranche is best protected against extension risk, and which carries the most?

LEWIS: Answer. The first tranche is best protected, and the last tranche carries the most. Here is why. Every dollar of principal goes to the first tranche until it is retired, so it gets its money soonest even when prepayments slow. The last tranche waits for every tranche ahead of it, so slower prepayments push its payoff furthest out. The flip side is that the first tranche carries relatively more contraction risk.

LEWIS Q: Rates rise and prepayments slow. In a CMO with a planned amortization class tranche and a support tranche, whose principal payments get pushed back?

HEART: Answer. The support tranche's. Here is why. When prepayments slow, the support tranche's principal is cut back so the planned amortization class can still be paid on schedule. The support tranche absorbs the extension, just as it absorbs the extra principal when prepayments run fast. That protection holds only while prepayment speeds stay within the planned range.

HEART Q: Rates fall and prepayments surge. Which gains, the principal only tranche or the interest only tranche?

LEWIS: Answer. The principal only tranche gains, and the interest only tranche loses. Here is why. A principal only tranche is bought at a discount and earns its return as principal comes back, so getting that principal sooner raises its annualized return. An interest only tranche is paid interest only on principal still outstanding, so every prepayment cuts off future interest.

LEWIS Q: A commercial borrower cannot refinance the balloon payment at the end of its loan, and the lender agrees to extend it. What kind of risk is that for the investor?

HEART: Answer. Extension risk. Here is why. Commercial mortgages are often only partly amortizing, so a large payment falls due at maturity, and paying it usually depends on refinancing. If refinancing fails and the loan is extended in a workout, the investor waits longer than planned for principal. That is balloon risk, a form of extension risk.

## Key takeaways

HEART: First, a few memory hooks.

LEWIS: For the deal itself: sell, shield, slice. The originator sells the loans, the special purpose entity shields them from the originator's fate, and tranches slice the risk among investors. And issuer has two meanings: benefits to issuers means the originator, but the bonds come from the special purpose entity.

HEART: For the originator: more lending, more fees, less capital, more liquidity. For investors: tailored, accessible and liquid.

LEWIS: For the waterfall: cash pours in from the top, losses rise from the bottom. Tranching moves risk, it never removes it.

HEART: For internal credit enhancement, three cushions. More collateral than bonds is overcollateralization. More interest than coupons is excess spread. Junior layers underneath is subordination. External enhancement is someone else's promise, so it brings someone else's credit risk.

LEWIS: For covered bonds: stays home, two claims, pool refilled. That is on the balance sheet, dual recourse and a dynamic pool. A hard bullet breaks on a missed payment, a soft bullet bends for up to a year, and a conditional pass through pays as the pool pays.

HEART: For credit cards: revolve, then amortize, unless an early amortization trigger trips first. For a CDO: a managed pool, and today mostly collateralized loan obligations built on leveraged loans.

LEWIS: For prepayment: rates down, money back too soon, that is contraction. Rates up, money back too late, that is extension. Gain less, lose more, that is negative convexity.

HEART: For sequential pay: first in line has more protection against extension, last in line has more protection against contraction. For planned amortization class and support: the support tranche is the shock absorber, taking both kinds of hits until the range is breached.

LEWIS: The P O wants speed, the I O wants patience. And a Z tranche grows its balance before it pays.

HEART: For commercial deals: judge the building, not the borrower. Coverage higher is safer, loan to value lower is safer. And balloon risk is extension risk.

LEWIS: Now, how you would say the big ideas to a senior leader.

HEART: On why lenders securitize: selling loans turns them into cash we can lend again, earns fees and can cut the capital we hold, while investors take on the credit risk. The jargon is securitization, the originator, a bankruptcy remote special purpose entity and lower capital reserves.

LEWIS: On structure: slicing a deal decides who takes losses first. It makes the top slices safer, but it never makes the risk go away. The jargon is credit tranching, subordination, the waterfall and internal credit enhancement.

HEART: On our mortgage exposure: home loans pay us back early when rates fall and late when rates rise, so they work against us both ways. The jargon is prepayment risk, contraction risk, extension risk and negative convexity.

LEWIS: On mortgage bonds: we can buy slices of a mortgage pool whose timing risk matches what we can carry. The jargon is collateralized mortgage obligations, with sequential pay, planned amortization class and support tranches.

HEART: And on commercial real estate: we judge the property's income and value, and watch for loans that cannot refinance at maturity. The jargon is commercial mortgage backed securities, the debt service coverage ratio, the loan to value ratio and balloon risk.
