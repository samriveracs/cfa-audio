## The big picture

This episode opens the derivatives topic, and it is about vocabulary and mechanics. Before you can price a derivative, which starts in the next episode, you need to know what each contract promises, who pays whom, when, and why anyone would sign it.

The first module defines a derivative, lists the terms every contract must specify, and contrasts exchanges with the over the counter market. The next two modules walk through the instruments, first forwards and futures, then swaps, options and credit default swaps. The last module covers why derivatives exist, what can go wrong, and how issuers and investors use them.

Expect three kinds of exam questions. First, features, such as which contract settles gains and losses every day. Second, calculations, mostly option payoffs and profits and futures margin. Third, uses, such as which hedge accounting label applies or which risk a hedger faces.

One idea ties the episode together. A forward commitment obligates both sides and has a symmetric payoff. A contingent claim gives one side a right and has an asymmetric payoff.

## Module 66.1, Derivatives Markets

Start with the definition. A derivative is a financial contract whose value derives from something else, called the underlying. The contract creates no new real asset. It is simply an agreement about how cash or goods change hands, depending on what the underlying does.

Why do derivatives exist? Some people carry risks they would rather not hold, and others will take those risks for a price. A wheat farmer fears falling prices before harvest, and a baker fears rising prices. Hedgers use derivatives to reduce an exposure they already have. Speculators take on new exposure in pursuit of profit, often taking the other side of a hedger's trade and adding liquidity.

Ignoring transaction costs, a derivative is zero sum. Whatever one party gains, the other loses, so the contract moves risk rather than creating it.

Every contract spells out its key terms. First, the underlying, stated precisely, such as a specific stock index or grade of oil. Second, the size, called the contract size or notional amount, which says how much of the underlying the contract covers. Third, the price, such as the forward price or an option's exercise price. Fourth, the expiration date. Fifth, the settlement method.

Settlement comes in two forms. With physical delivery, the seller hands over the underlying and the buyer pays the agreed price. With cash settlement, no goods move, and the losing party pays the winning party the net difference. Some underlyings, such as a stock index or an interest rate, cannot practically be delivered, so those contracts settle in cash.

Underlyings fall into six broad classes. Equities, meaning stocks and stock indexes. Fixed income and interest rates, meaning bonds and reference rates such as the Secured Overnight Financing Rate, or SOFR. Currencies, meaning exchange rates. Commodities, which split into hard commodities that are mined or extracted, such as oil and gold, and soft commodities that are grown or raised, such as wheat, coffee and cattle. Then credit, meaning the risk that a borrower defaults, and a catch all group of other underlyings, such as weather.

Derivatives trade in two venues, and the contrast is heavily tested. Exchange traded derivatives are standardized contracts listed on an organized exchange. The exchange, not the traders, sets all the terms. Because every contract is identical, many traders hold the same instrument, which builds liquidity, and you can exit by making the opposite trade.

The key institution is the clearinghouse. Once a trade is matched, the clearinghouse becomes the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer. You face the clearinghouse, not the stranger on the other side, and it protects itself by requiring margin from both sides and settling gains and losses every day. That nearly removes counterparty credit risk, the risk that the party on the other side fails to pay. Exchange trading also brings transparency, because prices and volumes are reported publicly, and close regulatory oversight.

Over the counter derivatives, or OTC derivatives, are private contracts negotiated directly between two parties. Usually one party is a dealer, typically a large bank, that acts as a market maker, taking the other side of customer trades and then managing the risk it picks up. Most OTC contracts use a standard master agreement from the International Swaps and Derivatives Association, or I S D A, which sets out what happens on default and how amounts owed are netted.

The great advantage of OTC contracts is customization. Every term can be tailored to match the exposure being hedged. The costs are more counterparty credit risk, less transparency, and less liquidity, because a custom contract is hard to exit except through the original dealer.

The OTC market has changed since the 2008 global financial crisis. Regulators in major markets now require many standardized OTC contracts, such as plain interest rate swaps, to be cleared through a central counterparty, or CCP. The two parties still negotiate the trade, but the CCP then steps between them and collects margin, much as an exchange clearinghouse does. Trades must also be reported to trade repositories, so regulators can see who owes what.

Here are the traps. First, central clearing reduces counterparty credit risk for the two original parties, but it does not make that risk vanish. It concentrates the risk in the CCP, which must itself be strong. Second, exchange trading removes most counterparty risk but none of the market risk. If prices move against you, you still lose. Third, if a question asks for the main advantage of OTC trading, the answer is customization, not liquidity or transparency.

So the rule to carry from this module is this. Exchange traded contracts are standardized, cleared, transparent and liquid, while OTC contracts are customized and carry counterparty credit risk unless they are centrally cleared.

## Module 67.1, Forwards and Futures

A forward contract is an OTC agreement in which one party agrees to buy, and the other agrees to sell, an underlying on a future date at a price fixed today. The buyer is the long. The seller is the short. The forward price is chosen so that the contract is worth zero to both sides at the start, so normally no money changes hands when it is signed. Everything happens at the end.

Know the payoff cold. The payoff to the long at expiration equals the spot price of the underlying at expiration minus the forward price. The payoff to the short equals the forward price minus the spot price at expiration. Every dollar the underlying ends above the forward price is a dollar gained by the long and lost by the short, and every dollar below it works the other way. That one for one, straight line payoff is what symmetric means.

Take a farmer who agrees today to sell 10,000 bushels of wheat in six months at a forward price of 5 dollars a bushel. If wheat ends at 4 dollars, the farmer, who is short, earns 5 minus 4, or 1 dollar a bushel, which is 10,000 dollars, and the buyer loses exactly that. If wheat ends at 6 dollars, the farmer's payoff is 5 minus 6, a loss of 1 dollar a bushel, or 10,000 dollars, but his crop is worth more. Either way, crop and contract together lock his revenue at 50,000 dollars. That is what a hedge does. It removes the downside and gives up the upside.

Because a forward is a private contract settled only at the end, both parties carry counterparty credit risk. Whichever side the price has moved in favor of is owed money, and is exposed to the other side's default. That risk can build quietly for the whole life of the contract.

A futures contract makes the same basic promise, but it is built to stop that quiet build up of credit risk. Four differences matter. First, futures are standardized by the exchange. Second, they trade on an exchange and are cleared through a clearinghouse, which guarantees performance. Third, they are marked to market daily, meaning each day's gain or loss, measured from the prior settlement price, is paid in cash through the trader's margin account. Fourth, both sides must post margin.

Unlike margin on a stock purchase, futures margin is not a loan. It is a good faith deposit, a performance bond, that shows you can cover daily losses. Initial margin is the deposit required to open a position. Maintenance margin is the minimum balance you must keep. If your balance falls below the maintenance margin, you get a margin call, and you must deposit variation margin that brings the balance all the way back to the initial margin, not just back to maintenance.

Here is an example. You go long one crude oil futures contract on 1,000 barrels at 80 dollars a barrel, with initial margin of 5,000 dollars and maintenance margin of 4,000 dollars. On day one, the settlement price falls 50 cents to 79.50, a 500 dollar loss, so your balance is 4,500 dollars. That is still above 4,000, so there is no call.

On day two, the settlement price falls another dollar, to 78.50. That is a 1,000 dollar loss, and your balance drops to 3,500 dollars, below maintenance. You must deposit 1,500 dollars to restore the account to 5,000. Meanwhile, the short's account is credited 500 dollars on day one and 1,000 dollars on day two.

Here is the trap. The tempting answer is 500 dollars, just enough to get back to maintenance. Maintenance is the trigger. Initial is the target. And if you do not meet the call, your position can be closed out.

Two more points round out futures. Most futures positions are closed before expiration with an offsetting trade, rather than by making or taking delivery. And as expiration approaches, the futures price converges to the spot price, because a contract to buy at the moment of expiration is the same as buying in the spot market.

So the rule to carry from this module is this. The long's payoff on a forward equals the spot price at expiration minus the forward price. On a futures contract, if your margin balance falls below the maintenance margin, you must deposit enough variation margin to bring it back to the initial margin.

## Module 67.2, Swaps and Options

A swap is an OTC agreement to exchange a series of cash flows on set dates. The most common is the plain interest rate swap. One party pays a fixed rate and the other pays a floating rate, both calculated on the same notional principal. The floating rate is tied to a market reference rate, such as SOFR, and resets each period.

Three features matter. First, the notional principal is not exchanged in an interest rate swap. It exists only to calculate the payments. Second, payments due on the same date in the same currency are netted, so only the party that owes more pays the difference. Third, like a forward, a swap is usually worth zero to both sides at the start. You can think of a swap as a bundle of forward contracts, one for each settlement date, all at the same fixed rate.

Here is the netting rule in words. The net payment to the fixed rate payer equals the floating rate minus the fixed rate, times the notional principal, times the fraction of a year the period covers. If the result is positive, the fixed rate payer receives it. If it is negative, the fixed rate payer pays it.

Take a swap with a notional principal of 10 million dollars, a fixed rate of 4 percent and annual payments. In year one the reference rate is 3 percent, so the fixed payer owes 400,000 dollars, the floating payer owes 300,000 dollars, and the fixed payer pays the net 100,000 dollars. In year two the reference rate is 5 percent, so the floating side owes 500,000 dollars, and the fixed payer receives a net 100,000 dollars. The pattern to remember is that the fixed rate payer gains when rates rise, and the fixed rate receiver gains when rates fall.

A company with a floating rate loan that fears rising rates can pay fixed and receive floating on a swap. The floating receipts cover the loan's floating payments, leaving a fixed cost. A bank or credit union holding fixed rate loans, funded by deposits whose rates follow the market, can pay fixed for a similar reason, so that swap receipts rise when its funding costs rise.

In a currency swap, the two parties pay interest in two different currencies, and they usually exchange the principal amounts at the start and exchange them back at the end. Here is the trap. In an interest rate swap the notional never moves. In a currency swap it usually does.

Now options. An option gives its buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an underlying at a fixed price, called the exercise price or strike price, on or before a set expiration date. A call is the right to buy. A put is the right to sell. The buyer, also called the holder or the long, pays a premium up front. The seller, also called the writer or the short, keeps the premium and takes on the obligation to perform if the holder exercises.

There are two styles of exercise. A European option can be exercised only at expiration. An American option can be exercised at any time up to expiration. The names have nothing to do with where the options trade.

Moneyness describes where the underlying price sits relative to the strike. A call is in the money when the underlying price is above the strike, at the money when they are equal, and out of the money when the underlying price is below the strike. A put is the reverse. It is in the money when the underlying price is below the strike.

Now the payoff formulas. The payoff of a call at expiration equals the greater of zero and the underlying price minus the exercise price. The payoff of a put at expiration equals the greater of zero and the exercise price minus the underlying price. The writer's payoff is the negative of the holder's. To get profit, subtract the premium from the holder's payoff, and add the premium to the writer's payoff.

Take a call with a strike of 50 dollars and a premium of 4 dollars. If the stock ends at 60, the payoff is 10 and the holder's profit is 10 minus 4, or 6. The writer loses 6. If the stock ends at 45, the call expires worthless, the holder loses the 4 dollar premium, and the writer keeps it as profit. The breakeven price for a call holder is the strike plus the premium, here 54.

Here is a trap. If the stock ends at 52, should the holder exercise, even though the profit is negative? Yes. The payoff is 2, which cuts the loss from 4 dollars to 2 dollars, because the premium is already spent. At expiration, you exercise whenever the option is in the money.

Now a put with a strike of 50 dollars and a premium of 3 dollars. If the stock ends at 40, the payoff is 50 minus 40, or 10, and the profit is 10 minus 3, or 7. The breakeven price for a put holder is the strike minus the premium, here 47.

Know the extremes. A call holder can lose at most the premium, and the upside has no limit. So a call writer who does not own the stock faces unlimited potential loss. A put holder can also lose at most the premium, but the gain is capped, because the stock cannot fall below zero. The maximum gain is the strike minus the premium, here 47, and that is also the put writer's maximum loss.

One more direction trap. A long call and a short put both profit when the underlying rises, but the call holder risks only the premium, while the put writer can lose much more. Likewise, a long put and a short call both profit when the underlying falls. These formulas give values at expiration. Before expiration, an option usually trades above its exercise value, which a later episode covers.

Next, credit derivatives, mainly the credit default swap, or C D S. The protection buyer makes regular payments, called the premium, to the protection seller. In return, if a specified borrower, called the reference entity, suffers a credit event such as default or bankruptcy, the seller compensates the buyer for the loss. Say protection is bought on 10 million dollars of a company's bonds at a premium of 1 percent a year, which is 100,000 dollars a year. If the company defaults and its bonds are then worth 40 percent of face value, the seller pays the buyer about 6 million dollars, the loss on the bonds.

Think of a C D S as insurance against default, except that the buyer does not have to own the bonds. The protection buyer is short credit risk and benefits if the borrower's credit worsens. The protection seller is long credit risk, much like holding the bond's credit exposure. Despite the word swap in its name, a C D S is a contingent claim, because the seller's large payment happens only if the credit event happens.

That leads to the central contrast between two families. Forward commitments, also called firm commitments, are forwards, futures and swaps. Contingent claims are options and credit derivatives such as the C D S. Three differences separate them.

First, obligation. In a forward commitment, both parties must perform, while in a contingent claim, one party holds a right and the other performs only if that right is exercised or the triggering event occurs. Second, upfront cost. A forward commitment is usually worth zero at the start, and futures margin is a deposit, not a price, while a contingent claim costs its buyer a premium. Third, payoff shape. Forward commitments have symmetric, straight line payoffs, while contingent claims have asymmetric payoffs, with the option holder's loss limited to the premium.

So the rule to carry from this module is this. A call's payoff at expiration is the greater of zero and the underlying price minus the strike. A put's payoff is the greater of zero and the strike minus the underlying price. Profit subtracts the premium for the holder and adds it for the writer.

## Module 68.1, Uses, Benefits, and Risks of Derivatives

Start with the benefits. Four stand out.

First, risk allocation, transfer and management. Derivatives let you separate a risk from the asset that carries it, and move that risk to whoever is most willing to bear it.

Second, information discovery. Futures markets often react to news quickly, and option prices reveal how much volatility the market expects.

Third, operational advantages. Derivatives often have lower transaction costs and more liquidity than the underlying. Shorting is easy, because selling a futures contract is no harder than buying one, while shorting a stock requires borrowing shares. And because you put up only a small amount of cash, derivatives are capital efficient.

Fourth, market efficiency. Because trading is cheap and fast, mispricings between derivatives and their underlyings get traded away quickly, which pulls prices toward fair value.

Now the risks. Six are worth knowing by name.

First, high implicit leverage. A small amount of cash controls a large exposure. In the oil futures example, a 5,000 dollar deposit controlled 80,000 dollars of oil. A 5 dollar drop in price, just over 6 percent, would wipe out the whole deposit. Leverage magnifies gains too, which is why it tempts people to take too much risk.

Second, lack of transparency. OTC contracts are private, and complex products, such as structured notes with embedded derivatives, can hide how much risk the buyer is really taking, which leads to unexpected losses.

Third, basis risk. This is the risk that the hedge and the exposure being hedged do not move together perfectly. An airline that hedges jet fuel with crude oil futures is exposed if the gap between jet fuel and crude oil prices changes. A hedge whose dates do not match the exposure creates the same problem.

Fourth, liquidity risk. This comes from a mismatch in the timing of cash flows. A futures hedge can demand cash today through margin calls, while the position it protects produces cash only later, as when a farmer who is short futures must post margin as prices rise, long before selling the crop. Banks and credit unions that hedge with swaps face the same issue, because a sharp move in rates can trigger collateral calls that must be met from liquid assets.

Fifth, counterparty credit risk, the risk that the other side fails to pay what it owes. This matters most for OTC contracts that are not centrally cleared.

Sixth, destabilization and systemic risk. When large, interconnected firms take excessive risk with derivatives, losses at one firm can spread and threaten the whole financial system. Heavy use of credit derivatives played a role in the 2008 crisis.

Now issuers, meaning companies, governments and other entities that issue securities. They mostly use derivatives to hedge risks from running or financing the business, such as interest rates on their debt, foreign currency revenue and costs, and commodity inputs.

A problem arises. Derivatives are carried at fair value, so their gains and losses could swing reported earnings, even when they offset a hedged item whose changes are recorded differently or later. To fix that mismatch, International Financial Reporting Standards, or IFRS, and U.S. generally accepted accounting principles, or GAAP, allow hedge accounting when a company formally designates a derivative as a hedge. There are three kinds.

A fair value hedge offsets changes in the fair value of an asset or liability. For example, a company issues fixed rate bonds and enters a swap to receive fixed and pay floating, effectively turning its debt into floating rate debt. Gains and losses on the swap, and the offsetting change in the value of the hedged debt, both go through profit or loss, where they largely cancel.

A cash flow hedge offsets the variability of future cash flows, from a floating rate asset or liability, or from a forecast transaction. For example, a company with floating rate debt pays fixed on a swap. The effective portion of the swap's gains and losses goes to other comprehensive income, and is moved into profit or loss when the hedged cash flows affect earnings.

A net investment hedge protects the value of an investment in a foreign operation against currency moves, using a currency derivative or debt issued in the foreign currency. Its gains and losses go to other comprehensive income, alongside the currency translation adjustment for that foreign operation.

Here is the trap, and a way to remember it. A fixed rate item has a changing value, so swapping fixed rate debt to floating is a fair value hedge. A floating rate item has changing cash flows, so swapping floating rate debt to fixed is a cash flow hedge.

Now investors, such as asset managers, pension funds, insurers and hedge funds. Four uses stand out. First, to replicate a cash market position cheaply, such as gaining index exposure with futures instead of buying every stock. Second, to hedge, such as buying puts to protect a portfolio. Third, to modify risk and return, such as using swaps to change a bond portfolio's duration, or writing calls on stocks you own to earn premium while giving up some upside. Fourth, to create exposures that are hard to get in cash markets, such as a position on volatility itself, or on a borrower's credit without owning its bonds.

The contrast is simple. Issuers mainly hedge business risks and care about hedge accounting, while investors use derivatives to add, remove or reshape exposure in pursuit of return.

So the rule to carry from this module is this. Fair value hedge gains and losses go to profit or loss. For cash flow and net investment hedges, the effective portion goes to other comprehensive income instead. Basis risk is an imperfect match between hedge and exposure, and liquidity risk is a mismatch in the timing of cash flows.

## Recall check

Q: Which feature is most likely found in a futures contract but not in a typical forward contract: customized delivery terms, daily settlement of gains and losses, or a price fixed at initiation?

Answer: Daily settlement of gains and losses. Futures are marked to market every day through margin accounts, with a clearinghouse guaranteeing performance. Customized terms belong to forwards, and both contracts fix the price at initiation.

Q: You are long a futures contract with an initial margin of 6,000 dollars and a maintenance margin of 4,500 dollars. After a bad day, your balance is 4,000 dollars. How much must you deposit?

Answer: 2,000 dollars. The balance fell below maintenance, so you must restore it to the initial margin, and 6,000 minus 4,000 is 2,000. Depositing only 500 dollars to reach maintenance is the tempting wrong answer.

Q: You buy a put with an exercise price of 40 dollars for a premium of 2 dollars. At expiration the stock is at 35. What are your payoff and your profit?

Answer: The payoff is 40 minus 35, or 5 dollars. The profit subtracts the 2 dollar premium, so it is 3 dollars. Your breakeven was 38, the strike minus the premium.

Q: A company pays fixed and receives floating on an interest rate swap. If the market reference rate rises well above the swap's fixed rate, does the company gain or lose on the swap?

Answer: It gains. Its floating receipts rise while its fixed payments stay the same, so the net settlement payments flow to it. The fixed rate receiver on the other side loses.

Q: Which of these is a contingent claim: an interest rate swap, a futures contract, or a credit default swap?

Answer: The credit default swap. The protection seller pays only if a credit event occurs, so its payment is contingent, despite the word swap in the name. Interest rate swaps and futures are forward commitments that obligate both sides.

Q: A company with floating rate debt designates a swap to pay fixed and receive floating as a hedge. Which type of hedge is this most likely, and where does the effective portion of the swap's gains and losses go first?

Answer: It is a cash flow hedge, because the swap offsets variability in future interest payments. The effective portion goes to other comprehensive income and is moved to profit or loss when the hedged interest payments affect earnings. A swap that turns fixed rate debt into floating rate debt would be a fair value hedge instead.

## Recap

Exchange traded contracts are standardized, cleared, transparent and liquid, while OTC contracts are customized and carry counterparty credit risk unless centrally cleared. The long's payoff on a forward is the spot price at expiration minus the forward price. Futures are standardized forwards settled daily, and a balance below maintenance margin must be restored to initial margin.

An interest rate swap exchanges fixed for floating on a notional principal that never changes hands, and the fixed rate payer gains when rates rise. A call pays the greater of zero and the underlying price minus the strike, a put pays the greater of zero and the strike minus the underlying price, and the holder's profit subtracts the premium. Forward commitments obligate both sides with symmetric payoffs, while contingent claims, meaning options and credit default swaps, have asymmetric payoffs. The six risks are implicit leverage, lack of transparency, basis risk, liquidity risk, counterparty credit risk and systemic risk. Fair value hedges run through profit or loss, while the effective portion of cash flow and net investment hedges goes to other comprehensive income.
