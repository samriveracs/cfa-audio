## The big picture

The last episode described what derivatives are and who uses them. This episode asks the next question. What should a derivative cost? The answer comes from one idea, called no arbitrage. If two portfolios produce exactly the same cash flows in every possible future, they must cost the same today. If they do not, traders buy the cheap one, sell the expensive one, and collect a riskless profit until the gap closes.

Four modules grow out of that idea. The first builds the toolkit and gives you the core forward pricing formula. The second values a forward at the start, during its life, and at expiration, then extends the idea to interest rates. The third shows that futures are priced almost exactly like forwards, and explains when the two prices differ. The fourth treats an interest rate swap as a bundle of forwards, which tells you how to set its fixed rate and how to value it later.

Exam questions here are short and mechanical, and they are full of direction traps. Does a dividend raise or lower the forward price? Which side gains when interest rates rise? Is the value of a brand new contract zero, or is its price zero?

Keep one distinction in your head for the whole episode. The price of a forward is the fixed number written into the contract. The value is what the contract is worth to one side right now. At the start, the price is chosen so that the value is zero.

## Module 69.1, Arbitrage, Replication, and Carrying Costs

Start with arbitrage. An arbitrage is a set of trades that requires no money of your own, carries no risk, and still produces a profit. The law of one price is the rule that keeps arbitrage from lasting. Two assets or portfolios with identical future cash flows must have the same price today, whatever anyone forecasts for the market.

A derivative's payoff depends on an underlying asset. That means you can often build the same payoff yourself, using the underlying asset plus borrowing or lending at the risk free rate. That is replication. If you can replicate a derivative, its price must equal the cost of the replicating portfolio. If it does not, there is an arbitrage. Pricing a derivative this way is called arbitrage free pricing.

Real markets have limits to arbitrage. Transaction costs, restrictions on short selling and limits on borrowing can let small mispricings survive. On the exam, assume these frictions away unless the question brings them up.

Here is the key replication. Buy one unit of the underlying asset today and, at the same moment, sell a forward contract on it. At expiration you deliver the asset and receive the forward price, a number you knew on day one. Whatever happens to the asset's price, your payoff is fixed.

A position with a known payoff and no risk is a synthetic risk free bond, so it must earn the risk free rate. That single argument prices every forward contract. Rearrange the pieces and buying the asset with borrowed money creates a synthetic long forward, because at expiration you own the asset and owe a fixed amount.

Notice what is missing from that argument. We never asked how risky the asset is, or what return investors expect from it. Because the hedged position has no risk, attitudes toward risk drop out. This is why derivatives can be priced as if every investor were risk neutral, with expected payoffs discounted at the risk free rate.

That leads to a classic trap. The forward price is not a forecast of the future spot price. It is today's spot price carried forward at the risk free rate, adjusted for the costs and benefits of holding the asset.

Here is the formula with no costs or benefits. The forward price equals the spot price times one plus the risk free rate, raised to the power T, where T is the time to expiration in years. Take an asset priced at 100, a risk free rate of 5 percent, and a one year forward. The forward price is 100 times 1.05, which is 105.

Why 105? The buyer pays later instead of today, so her 100 earns 5 dollars of interest in the meantime. The seller, who holds the asset until delivery, gives up that interest, so the seller charges 105 to break even.

Now suppose the one year forward trades at 108 instead of 105. The forward is too expensive, so you sell it and buy the asset. Borrow 100 at 5 percent, buy the asset, and sell the forward at 108. In a year you deliver the asset, collect 108, and repay 105 on the loan. You keep 3 dollars, with no risk and none of your own money. This is called cash and carry arbitrage, because you buy the asset with cash and carry it to expiration.

Suppose instead the forward trades at 102. Now the forward is too cheap, so you buy it and sell the asset. Short sell the asset for 100, invest the 100 at 5 percent, and buy the forward at 102.

In a year your investment has grown to 105. You pay 102 under the forward, receive the asset, and return it to close the short sale. You keep 3 dollars. This is reverse cash and carry arbitrage.

Here is the memory hook. Whatever is overpriced, you sell. Forward too high, sell the forward and buy the asset. Forward too low, buy the forward and sell the asset short.

Holding an asset is rarely free, and it often pays you something. These are the carrying costs and benefits. Benefits include cash income, such as dividends on a stock or coupons on a bond. For physical commodities, there is also the convenience yield. That is the nonmonetary benefit of having the physical good on hand, such as keeping a factory running when supplies are tight.

Costs include storage, insurance and spoilage. Financial assets have essentially no storage cost.

Here is the direction of each effect. Benefits go to whoever holds the asset. The forward buyer does not hold it until delivery, so she misses the dividends and coupons. She should pay less, so benefits lower the forward price. Costs fall on whoever holds the asset, which is the seller. The seller passes those costs on, so costs raise the forward price.

In words, the forward price equals the spot price, minus the present value of the benefits, plus the present value of the costs, with that total multiplied by one plus the risk free rate, raised to the power T. The present values are taken at the risk free rate.

Use the same asset at 100, with 5 percent and one year. Suppose it pays income with a present value of 3. Subtract to get 97, then multiply by 1.05 to get a forward price of 101.85. Now suppose instead it has storage costs with a present value of 2. Add to get 102, then multiply by 1.05 to get 107.10.

Some questions use continuously compounded rates. Then you replace one plus r, raised to the power T, with the number e raised to the power r times T. If costs and benefits are given as continuous rates, add the cost rate to r and subtract the benefit rate in that exponent. On the B A two plus, enter r times T, then press second and the L N key to get e raised to that power. For 5 percent over one year, the display shows about 1.0513, so a 100 dollar asset with no carry has a forward price of about 105.13.

When the forward price sits above the spot price, the market is in contango. When it sits below the spot price, the market is in backwardation. In the cost of carry model, backwardation appears when the benefits of holding the asset, such as a high dividend yield or a high convenience yield, outweigh the risk free rate plus any costs.

A currency forward uses the same logic. Holding a foreign currency earns that currency's interest rate, which works like a carry benefit. The forward exchange rate equals the spot exchange rate times one plus the interest rate of the price currency, divided by one plus the interest rate of the base currency, with that ratio raised to the power T.

Say the spot rate is 1.10 dollars per euro, the dollar rate is 5 percent and the euro rate is 3 percent. The forward rate for one year is 1.10 times 1.05, divided by 1.03, or about 1.12 dollars per euro. The currency with the lower interest rate, here the euro, trades at a forward premium. The trap is flipping the ratio. The interest rate of the currency in which the price is quoted goes on top.

Here is the core rule again. The forward price equals the spot price, minus the present value of benefits, plus the present value of costs, all grown at the risk free rate to expiration. Benefits lower it. Costs raise it. Expected returns do not enter at all.

## Module 70.1, Forward Contract Valuation

This module separates price from value at three points in a forward's life. The price is fixed. The value moves.

At initiation, the forward price is set by the cost of carry formula, so the contract's value to both sides is zero. Neither side pays anything to enter. A new, fairly priced forward has a value of zero even though its price might be 105.

At expiration, the value to the long equals the spot price at expiration minus the forward price. The short gets the negative of that. If the forward price was 105 and the asset ends at 112, the long gains 7 and the short loses 7. A forward is a zero sum game between its two parties.

During the life of the contract, think of the long forward as owning the asset, paid for with a promise to pay the forward price later. Its value is what you have minus the present value of what you owe. So the value of a long forward equals the current spot price minus the present value of the forward price, discounted at the risk free rate for the time remaining. If the asset has carry, subtract the present value of any benefits still to come before expiration, and add the present value of any costs still to come. The long misses those benefits and avoids those costs.

A one year forward was set at 105 on an asset priced at 100, with a 5 percent risk free rate. Six months later, the asset trades at 110. Discount the forward price of 105 for the remaining half year. One plus 5 percent, raised to the power one half, is about 1.0247. On the B A two plus, enter 1.05, press the y to the x key, enter 0.5, and press equals.

Divide 105 by 1.0247 and you get about 102.47. The value to the long is 110 minus 102.47, or about 7.53. The value to the short is about negative 7.53.

There is a second way to see the same number. Value equals the present value of the gap between today's forward price for the same expiration date and the original forward price. Today's forward price for delivery in six months is 110 times 1.0247, or about 112.72. You locked in 105, so you are 7.72 better off at expiration, and the present value of that is again about 7.53. This second view helps when a question gives you a new forward price instead of a spot price.

Three traps show up here. First, subtracting the full forward price instead of its present value. Second, forgetting to remove dividends still to be paid before expiration. Third, flipping the sign for the short.

The second half of this module covers forwards on an interest rate for a period that starts in the future. Pricing them requires implied forward rates, which come from spot rates, the yields on zero coupon bonds of different maturities.

The logic is no arbitrage again. Investing for two years at the two year spot rate must give the same result as investing for one year at the one year spot rate and then rolling over for a second year at a rate you lock in today. In words, one plus the two year spot rate, squared, equals one plus the one year spot rate, times one plus the one year forward rate starting one year from now.

Say the one year spot rate is 2 percent and the two year spot rate is 3 percent. Square 1.03 to get 1.0609, divide by 1.02, and subtract one. The implied one year forward rate, one year from now, is about 4.01 percent. A quick check is to take twice the two year rate and subtract the one year rate, which gives about 4 percent.

The forward rate is a breakeven rate. If the one year rate a year from now turns out above about 4 percent, rolling short would have paid more. Below it, locking in the two year rate would have paid more.

A forward rate agreement, or F R A, is an over the counter forward contract on an interest rate. One side pays a fixed rate, the F R A rate, and the other pays a floating market reference rate, such as SOFR, on a notional principal for a future period. The notional is never exchanged. Only the net interest difference changes hands.

The long position pays fixed and receives floating, so the long gains when rates rise. A company that plans to borrow at a floating rate later can go long an F R A to lock in its cost. A lender who wants to lock in a future investment rate goes short. A three by nine F R A starts in 3 months, ends in 9 months, and covers a 6 month rate.

The payoff equals the reference rate at settlement minus the F R A rate, times the notional, times the fraction of a year the period covers. Take a notional of 1 million dollars, a 6 month period, an F R A rate of 3 percent, and a reference rate that sets at 4 percent. The rate gap is 1 percent, times 1 million, times one half, which is 5,000 dollars owed to the long. That amount relates to the end of the period, but F R As usually settle at the start, so it is discounted at the reference rate for that period. Divide 5,000 by one plus 4 percent times one half, which is 1.02, and the long receives about 4,902 dollars.

The fair F R A rate at initiation is the implied forward rate for that period, which makes the contract's value zero. Later, the value of the long equals the present value of the gap between the current implied forward rate for the same period and the original F R A rate, times the notional and the period fraction.

To close the module, here is the rule to keep. The value of a long forward during its life equals the spot price, minus the present value of remaining benefits, plus the present value of remaining costs, minus the present value of the forward price. At initiation it is zero. At expiration it is the spot price minus the forward price.

## Module 71.1, Futures Valuation

A futures contract is a standardized forward that trades on an exchange, clears through a central counterparty, or CCP, and settles gains and losses every day. The pricing logic is the same cost of carry formula. Two things change, how value behaves and, in some cases, the price itself.

Start with value. At the end of each trading day, every position is marked to market. Gains are credited and losses are debited to margin accounts, and the contract is effectively rewritten at the new settlement price. So right after each daily settlement, a futures contract's value is zero.

During the day, the value to the long equals the current futures price minus the last settlement price, times the contract size. Suppose yesterday's settlement price was 50 and the futures now trades at 51. The long's value is 1 per unit. At today's close that gain is paid into the long's margin account, and the value resets to zero.

A forward, by contrast, builds up gains and losses until expiration. Here is the trap. The value of a futures contract is not its price. The price might be 51 while the value is zero, or 1, depending on whether the day's settlement has happened.

Now the price. If interest rates are constant, or move independently of the futures price, the futures price equals the forward price, ignoring credit risk. The difference appears when futures prices and interest rates are correlated, because daily settlement creates cash flows that must be reinvested or financed.

Suppose futures prices tend to rise when interest rates rise, a positive correlation. Then a long futures holder receives cash just when rates are high and can reinvest it at those high rates. The long pays cash when rates are low, so it can be borrowed cheaply. That makes a long futures position more attractive than a long forward, so buyers will pay more. The futures price is above the forward price.

With negative correlation, the reverse holds, and the futures price is below the forward price. Keep it simple. Positive correlation, futures higher. Negative correlation, futures lower.

Credit risk also differs. A forward depends on the other party paying at the end, while daily settlement through the CCP keeps futures counterparty risk very small.

Interest rate futures have quotes that run backward. The quoted price equals 100 minus the annualized interest rate, in percent. A quote of 97.50 means a rate of 2.50 percent. If the rate rises to 2.75 percent, the price falls to 97.25. So price and rate move in opposite directions, and a long interest rate futures position gains when rates fall.

That is the exact opposite of a long F R A, which gains when rates rise. This is one of the most tested traps in the topic. A borrower worried about rising rates can go long an F R A, or go short interest rate futures.

Interest rate futures also have a fixed value per basis point. The basis point value equals the notional, times one basis point, times the fraction of a year the rate covers. For a 3 month contract on 1 million dollars, that is 1 million, times 0.0001, times one quarter, or 25 dollars per basis point. If rates rise 25 basis points, a long loses 25 times 25, or 625 dollars per contract.

Because that dollar amount per basis point never changes, the futures payoff is linear in the rate. An F R A payoff is discounted at the settlement rate, so it is not quite linear. That difference, together with daily settlement, creates a small gap between the rate implied by a futures price and the matching forward rate. That gap is called the convexity bias.

Here is the module in one line. Futures are priced with the same cost of carry formula as forwards, their value resets to zero at every daily settlement, and a long interest rate futures position gains when rates fall.

## Module 72.1, Swap Valuation

A plain vanilla interest rate swap is an agreement in which one side pays a fixed rate and the other pays a floating rate, on the same notional principal, at regular dates. Only the net payment changes hands.

The key insight is that a swap is a series of forward contracts. Each payment date works like an F R A. The fixed payer pays the swap's fixed rate and receives whatever the floating rate turns out to be. A real series of F R As would each have its own fixed rate, the implied forward rate for that period. A swap uses one fixed rate for every period.

So each embedded forward is off market. When the yield curve slopes upward, forward rates rise with time. The single swap rate sits above the early forward rates and below the later ones. The early embedded forwards therefore have negative value to the fixed payer, and the later ones have positive value. The swap rate is chosen so that these values sum to exactly zero at inception.

So the swap rate is the fixed rate that makes the present value of the fixed payments equal the present value of the expected floating payments. There is a neat shortcut. A floating rate note that resets to market is worth its par value on each reset date. So the swap rate is the coupon rate that would make a fixed rate bond with the same payment dates sell at par.

In words, the periodic swap rate equals one minus the discount factor for the final payment date, divided by the sum of the discount factors for all the payment dates. Each discount factor is one divided by one plus that date's spot rate, with the denominator raised to the number of periods.

Use the same spot rates as before, 2 percent for one year and 3 percent for two years, with annual payments. The one year discount factor is about 0.9804. The two year discount factor is about 0.9426. One minus 0.9426 is 0.0574. The sum of the two discount factors is about 1.923. Divide and the two year swap rate is about 2.99 percent.

The first year's forward rate is the spot rate, 2 percent. The second year's forward rate is about 4.01 percent. The swap rate lands between them. In fact the swap rate is a weighted average of the forward rates, with the discount factors as weights.

After inception, rates move. The fixed payer gains when rates rise, because it is locked into paying the old, lower rate while receiving a floating rate that has gone up. The receiver of fixed gains when rates fall.

On a payment date, the value to the fixed payer equals the current swap rate for the remaining term, minus the original swap rate, times the notional, times the sum of the discount factors for the remaining payment dates. Suppose you pay fixed at 3 percent on 10 million dollars. One year later, two annual payments remain, and the market swap rate for two years is 4 percent, with spot rates of 4 percent for both years. You save 1 percent of 10 million, or 100,000 dollars, in each of the next two years. The discount factors add up to about 1.886, so the swap is worth about 189,000 dollars to you, and about negative 189,000 dollars to the receiver.

Another way to see the direction is through bonds. Paying fixed and receiving floating is like issuing a fixed rate bond and buying a floating rate note. Receiving fixed is like owning a fixed rate bond funded with floating rate borrowing, so the receiver gains when rates fall, just as a bondholder does. A lender holding fixed rate loans funded by short term deposits can pay fixed on a swap, so that its net income from those loans floats with its funding costs.

Other swaps follow the same logic. The terms make the two legs equal in present value at inception, and later value is the difference between the two legs.

The exam traps here are predictable. First, saying the fixed payer gains when rates fall. Second, confusing the swap rate with today's floating rate. Third, assuming each embedded forward is fairly priced just because the whole swap is. Here is the rule to keep. The swap rate equals one minus the final discount factor, divided by the sum of all the discount factors, and it makes the swap's value zero at inception.

## Recall check

Q: A one year forward on a stock with no dividends trades above the spot price grown at the risk free rate. What arbitrage do you run?

Answer: Cash and carry arbitrage. The forward is overpriced, so you sell it. You borrow at the risk free rate, buy the stock, and sell the forward. At expiration you deliver the stock, collect the forward price, repay the loan, and keep the difference.

Q: An asset trades at 100, the risk free rate is 5 percent, and the asset pays income with a present value of 4 over the next year. What is the one year forward price?

Answer: About 100.80. Subtract the present value of the income to get 96, then multiply by 1.05. The income lowers the forward price because the forward buyer does not receive it.

Q: A new forward contract is fairly priced at 105. What is its value to the long at initiation?

Answer: Zero. The forward price is set so that neither side has value at the start. Price is the fixed number in the contract. Value is what the contract is worth to one side, and it changes only as the market moves.

Q: Futures prices on an asset are positively correlated with interest rates. Is the futures price most likely higher than, lower than, or equal to the forward price?

Answer: Higher. With positive correlation, the long futures holder receives daily gains when rates are high and can reinvest them at those rates, and pays losses when rates are low and borrowing is cheap. That makes the futures position more valuable to a buyer than the matching forward.

Q: An interest rate futures contract is quoted at 97. If the annualized rate rises half a percentage point, what is the new quote, and does the long gain or lose?

Answer: The quote starts at 100 minus 3, so the rate rises from 3 percent to 3.5 percent and the new quote is 96.50. The long loses. A long interest rate futures position gains when rates fall, the opposite of a long F R A.

Q: Six months after entering a pay fixed interest rate swap, market swap rates for the remaining term have risen. Which side has gained value?

Answer: The fixed rate payer. It is locked into paying the old, lower fixed rate while receiving floating payments that are now expected to be higher. On a payment date, its value equals the rise in the swap rate, times the notional, times the sum of the remaining discount factors.

## Recap

No arbitrage pricing says that two positions with identical future cash flows must cost the same today, so a derivative's price equals the cost of the portfolio that replicates it. Owning an asset and selling it forward is a synthetic risk free bond, which is why the forward price equals the spot price, minus the present value of benefits, plus the present value of costs, grown at the risk free rate to expiration. Expected returns and risk preferences never enter, and the forward price is not a forecast. When a forward is overpriced, run cash and carry, and when it is underpriced, run reverse cash and carry.

A long forward's value is zero at initiation and equals the spot price minus the forward price at expiration. In between, it equals the spot price minus the present value of the forward price, adjusted for any carry still to come. Implied forward rates come from spot rates by no arbitrage, and a long F R A gains when rates rise. Futures use the same pricing formula, but their value resets to zero at every daily settlement, and with positive correlation between futures prices and rates, the futures price sits above the forward price. A long interest rate futures position gains when rates fall. A swap is a series of off market forwards, its fixed rate equals one minus the final discount factor divided by the sum of the discount factors, and the fixed payer gains when rates rise.
