LEWIS: Readings 76 and 77 cover why investors reach for alternative investments, the three ways to invest in them, the limited partnership with its fees and payout rules, and how to judge performance through the J curve, leverage, smoothed valuations, fee terms and biased indexes. Readings 78 and 79 cover private equity and private debt, then real estate and infrastructure, with their forms, their risk and return drivers and the role each plays in a portfolio.

## Module 76.1, Alternative Investment Structures

HEART: Traditional investing means holding cash, publicly traded stocks and publicly traded bonds, long only, so you profit when they rise. Picture a retirement account with a stock index fund, a bond fund and some cash, all priced every day and sellable with a click. An alternative investment is defined by exclusion. It is anything outside that box: a stake in a private software company, a warehouse, a toll bridge, a pool of business loans. Alternatives are usually actively managed.

LEWIS: So why bother with them?

HEART: Two reasons. Diversification, because alternatives have tended to show relatively low correlation with stocks and bonds. And the chance of higher returns, partly as payment for locking money up in illiquid assets, and partly because some of these markets may be less efficient, leaving bargains for a skilled manager to find. The catch is that correlations that look low on average can jump during economic stress, just when you need the diversification most. It is an umbrella that folds in a storm.

LEWIS: Compared with traditional investments, alternatives share five typical features. Managers need more specialized knowledge. Correlation with traditional assets is relatively low. The holdings are less liquid. Investors need longer time horizons. And the commitments are larger.

HEART: Those features bring three characteristics. Structures that let managers invest directly in companies and properties. Information asymmetry, because the manager knows far more than the investors and transparency is limited, which funds address with incentive fees that line up the manager's interests with the investors'. And performance that is hard to appraise, because the history of returns and volatility is short, patchy and often built from appraisals rather than trades. Add frequent borrowing and higher fees, and you have the family traits.

LEWIS: Now the map, three broad categories. Private capital is private equity and private debt. Private equity buys companies that are not publicly traded, or public ones it plans to take private, often in the mature or declining stage of their industry. Leveraged buyout funds, which borrow heavily to buy established companies, make up most of it, while venture capital funds back young, unproven companies. Private debt funds lend directly to companies, lend to early stage firms, called venture debt, or buy the debt of struggling or bankrupt firms, called distressed debt.

HEART: Real assets start with real estate, meaning residential and commercial property and the debt secured by it, held as whole buildings, single loans, securities backed by pools of properties or mortgages, or partnership stakes. Natural resources cover commodities, farmland and timberland. Infrastructure covers long lasting assets that provide public services, economic ones like seaports and power lines, and social ones like courthouses. And other real assets include collectibles such as art, intangibles such as patents, and digital assets such as cryptocurrencies.

LEWIS: The third category is hedge funds, usually open only to qualified investors. They may borrow, sell short, trade derivatives and hold illiquid assets, and despite the name they do not necessarily hedge. Natural resources, hedge funds and digital assets get their own readings in the next episode.

HEART: Next, the three ways to invest. Picture renovating a house. You can hire a contractor to do everything, hire one but work alongside the crew on a couple of rooms, or be your own contractor. Those are fund investing, co investing and direct investing.

LEWIS: Fund investing pools your money with other investors under a manager who picks and runs the investments to an agreed strategy, so you control neither what is bought nor when it is sold. The manager earns a management fee plus an incentive fee on the gains. Next to a stock or bond fund, an alternative fund asks for more money, locks it up for longer, tells you less about what it holds and how it is doing, and charges a higher management fee. The fund's term sheet sets out the deal: the investment policy, the fees and who may join.

HEART: Co investing means you invest in the fund and also hold the right to invest directly alongside the manager in some of its deals. You cut your overall fees, still lean on the manager's expertise, and train your own team for direct investing later. The manager gains more capital and can spread the fund across more deals. One caution. The manager picks which deals to offer, so watch for adverse selection, being shown the deals it is least keen to fund alone.

LEWIS: Direct investing means buying the assets yourself. Think of a giant pension plan with its own team buying an office tower. There are no outside manager fees and you have full control. But you get less diversification, much larger minimum sizes, and you need the expertise to evaluate deals and do your own due diligence.

HEART: So the rule. From fund investing to co investing to direct investing, fees fall and control rises, while the expertise, capital and due diligence you must supply all rise, and diversification usually falls.

LEWIS: Most of these funds are limited partnerships. The general partner, the GP, is the fund manager. It makes every investment decision and takes on the partnership's liabilities, including its debts. The limited partners, the LPs, are the investors. They own shares in proportion to what they put in, usually have no say in management, and can lose no more than their investment. Partnerships typically cap the number of limited partners, and because they are less regulated than public companies, they are usually open only to accredited investors, those wealthy enough to bear the risk and sophisticated enough to understand it.

HEART: The money arrives over time. A limited partner commits an amount, may pay in only part at first, and supplies the rest through capital calls as the general partner finds deals. This drawdown usually runs three to five years, at the manager's discretion, and committed money not yet called is known as dry powder. A credit union knows that shape from a home equity line. Once the line is approved, the member draws whenever they choose, so cash must be kept ready. A limited partner has made the same kind of promise.

LEWIS: The documents. The limited partnership agreement holds the rules and operating details. Special terms for one investor go in a side letter. One is an excusal right, letting that investor withhold a capital contribution it would otherwise owe, say an endowment that will not fund a certain kind of business. Another is a most favored nation clause, meaning any special terms granted to other limited partners must be offered to this one too. And while most partnership stakes are illiquid, a master limited partnership trades publicly, mostly in natural resources and real estate.

HEART: Now pay. The management fee is typically one to two percent of assets a year, earned regardless of performance. The performance fee, or incentive fee, is a share of profits, often called carried interest in private equity. Hedge funds charge the management fee on assets under management. Private equity charges it on committed capital, not invested capital, because a fee on invested capital would pay the manager for rushing money into deals instead of choosing carefully. Investors pay on dry powder, but they get a selective manager.

LEWIS: The hurdle rate, or preferred return, is the return the fund must earn before any performance fee is paid. It protects investors from paying a share of profits on returns they could have had without a skilled manager. Think of a salesperson with a quota. A hard hurdle pays commission only on the sales above the quota. A soft hurdle says hit the quota and you earn commission on every sale from the first.

HEART: In fund terms, a hard hurdle charges the performance fee only on gains above the hurdle, and a soft hurdle, once cleared, charges it on the entire gain. So hard favors the limited partners and soft favors the general partner. A catch up clause has an effect similar to a soft hurdle. Investors get all the gains up to the hurdle, then the general partner gets the next slice until it has caught up to its full agreed share of everything earned so far, and after that gains split at the normal ratio.

LEWIS: A high water mark means no performance fee on gains that only recover earlier losses. The fee applies only above the highest previous value, measured net of fees at the end of a fee period, so investors never pay twice for the same gains. Think of a runner who earns a bonus only for a new personal best, not for getting back to her old time after an injury. And since investors join at different times, each can have its own high water mark.

HEART: The waterfall sets the order in which cash from sold investments goes to the general partner and the limited partners. A deal by deal, or American, waterfall shares profits as each investment is sold. A whole of fund, or European, waterfall pays the limited partners everything until they have all their initial investment back plus the hurdle, usually once the fund has sold everything, and only then does the general partner share.

LEWIS: So deal by deal favors the general partner, which can collect fees on early winners before investors are made whole, and whole of fund favors the limited partners. Do not judge by which name sounds friendlier. Judge by timing. Whoever is paid first is favored.

HEART: Which is why the clawback exists. If the general partner was paid performance fees on gains that later reversed, the limited partners can recover the excess. It matters most under deal by deal, when winners are sold early and losers later.

LEWIS: The takeaway. Fees fall and control rises as you move toward direct investing. Hard hurdles, high water marks, whole of fund waterfalls and clawbacks protect investors, while soft hurdles, catch ups and deal by deal waterfalls tilt toward the manager.

## Module 77.1, Performance Appraisal and Return Calculations

LEWIS: Alternatives carry risks that an unleveraged, long only portfolio does not, and four stand out. The timing of cash flows over the investment's life. Leverage. Valuing assets without observable prices. And complicated fees, taxes and accounting. Adjusting returns for all four is hard, but ignoring them is naive and misleading.

HEART: Start with timing. A private fund has three phases. In the capital commitment phase, managers find investments and make capital calls, and with money going out and nothing yet paying off, returns tend to be negative. In the capital deployment phase, managers fund the companies and often help run them, and returns usually improve but stay negative, above all for start ups and turnarounds. In the capital distribution phase, the investments start earning income and get sold, and returns turn positive and pick up speed.

LEWIS: Plot the cumulative return and you get the J curve, a dip below zero, then a long climb, often leveling off at the end as the last holdings are sold. Think of planting an orchard. Years of paying for saplings and water come before the first harvest. A young orchard with losses is not a failed orchard.

HEART: Which is the trap. A negative return in a fund's early years is not evidence of a bad manager. It is the J curve. That is also why funds are compared with others of the same vintage year, the year a fund made its first investment.

LEWIS: Because the manager decides when money is called and returned, the most appropriate measure over a fund's life is the internal rate of return, the IRR. It is a money weighted return, and Episode 1 showed why that fits when the manager controls the timing. Its weakness is that the answer leans on assumptions, about what the money called from investors really costs them and about what the money paid back can earn when it is reinvested.

HEART: The simpler measure is the multiple of invested capital, or money multiple. It is the total capital returned plus the value of what the fund still holds, divided by the total capital paid in. It ignores timing, so it is considered somewhat naive. Doubling your money in three years and in fifteen years gives the same multiple. Only the IRR tells them apart.

LEWIS: Next, leverage. Hedge funds especially borrow to magnify gains, through margin financing from prime brokers, the firms that lend to and service hedge funds, or through derivatives. The same borrowing magnifies losses. In words, the fund earns its return on its own money plus the borrowed money, pays interest on the borrowing, and you measure what is left against its own money. So leverage lifts returns only when the assets earn more than the borrowing rate. Funds borrow because some strategies chase pricing anomalies too small to matter without it.

HEART: And the dangers. If the fund's equity drops below a set level, the lender makes a margin call, and the fund may have to sell at bad prices, with large sales pushing prices lower still. Lenders can also cut off new borrowing at the worst moment. Any institution that leans on borrowed funding knows that a lender's appetite shrinks just when you need it most.

LEWIS: Third, valuation. Accounting standards require fair value, and the assumptions fall into a three level hierarchy. Level one assets have readily available quoted prices in active markets, like exchange traded shares. Level two assets have no ready quote but can be valued from observable inputs, the way many derivatives are priced with models built on market rates. Level three assets need unobservable inputs, the valuer's own assumptions, like a private company or an office building that rarely trades.

HEART: A credit union's balance sheet works the same way. Many of its bonds are priced from observable market rates and sit in level two, while any fair value it puts on the loans it holds usually rests on level three assumptions.

LEWIS: Level three values can sit near cost for long stretches and may not reflect what an exit would actually fetch. Imagine checking your home's value once a year with a cautious appraiser who leans on last year's number. The chart looks calm even in a year when buyers vanished. The house was not less risky. You just priced it less often.

HEART: So stale, smoothed values make alternatives look as if they have higher returns, less risk and less correlation with traditional investments than they really do, and the diversification benefit looks bigger on paper than in reality.

LEWIS: Fourth, fees. Terms are negotiable and can depend on how early an investor commits, so investors in the same fund can earn different returns. Withdrawals are restricted too. The worse a fund performs, the more investors want out, and meeting redemptions can force sales at bad prices, much like margin calls. So funds, hedge funds especially, limit early redemptions.

HEART: There are four tools. A lockup period after the initial investment, during which investors cannot redeem, or can redeem only by paying a steep fee. A notice period, usually one to three months, giving the manager time to reduce positions in an orderly way. Redemption fees, which offset the trading costs. And a gate, which lets the manager temporarily restrict redemptions, like stadium turnstiles that slow the exit to prevent a stampede. These terms protect the investors who stay.

LEWIS: Now the fee menu. Two and twenty, a two percent management fee and a twenty percent performance fee, used to be the going rate for a fund, and one and ten for a fund of funds, but competition keeps pushing both lower. Bigger commitments win lower fees. Investors can also trade liquidity against fees, accepting longer lockups and notice periods for a lower fee, or paying more for easier exits. Hurdles, hard or soft, and catch up terms are negotiable too, and one investor's custom terms go in a side letter.

HEART: Founders class shares give the earliest investors in a new fund lower fees or better liquidity for backing a manager before it has proven itself. An either or fee charges, each year, the larger of the management fee or the incentive fee, not both, so investors pay a modest fee in weak years and the manager earns well only in strong ones. Some versions subtract the management fee already paid from a later year's incentive fee. And a fund of funds adds its own management and performance fees on top of the underlying funds' fees, two layers paid in exchange for diversification, access and due diligence.

LEWIS: To get a net return, measure the gross return as usual, then subtract the management fee and the performance fee from the ending value and compare what is left with the starting value. The idea is simple. The fine print decides the answer.

HEART: Four pieces of fine print. Whether the management fee is charged on beginning or ending assets, and in a year the fund grows, ending assets cost more. Whether the performance fee is calculated net of the management fee or independently on the whole gain, and net is the smaller base, so it favors the investor. Whether there is a hurdle, hard or soft. And whether a high water mark applies. None of these is standard, so on a question, apply only the provisions it states.

LEWIS: Last, the indexes. An index of these funds can tell you surprisingly little about the asset class, because no two funds are built alike, and at any moment some are just starting while others are selling their last holdings. That is another reason to compare by vintage year. Survivorship bias arises because failed funds drop out of the database, so the index overstates returns and understates risk. It is especially severe for hedge funds, since a large share of them fail within a few years.

HEART: Backfill bias makes it worse. Reporting is voluntary, so managers choose which funds to add to an index, and they add the successful ones, often with their good past returns attached. Picture a school that publishes grades only for students who graduated, and only after a good semester. Both biases push reported returns up and reported risk down.

LEWIS: The takeaway. The J curve makes young funds look weak. The IRR captures timing, while the money multiple ignores it. Leverage helps only when assets earn more than the borrowing rate. And level three values, survivorship and backfill all flatter the numbers.

## Module 78.1, Private Capital

HEART: Private capital is funding raised outside the public markets, as private equity or private debt. The companies a private equity fund owns are its portfolio companies.

LEWIS: The most common type is the leveraged buyout, or LBO. The fund buys a public company, paying for much of it with debt, and the shares stop trading, so the company goes private. The fund then sharpens or reorganizes the business to lift sales, profit and cash flow, and that cash covers the interest and gradually repays the money borrowed for the purchase. It is like buying a rental house with a big mortgage, fixing it up so it rents for more, and letting the rent pay down the loan while your equity grows.

HEART: Two variations. In a management buyout, an MBO, the existing managers join the purchase. In a management buy in, an MBI, the fund brings in a new team to replace them. A buyout keeps the team. A buy in brings one in.

LEWIS: Venture capital, or VC, finances companies early in their development. VC funds usually take common equity, and sometimes convertible debt or convertible preferred stock, which lines up everyone's interest in a rising company value while giving the VC investors priority if the company is liquidated. The risk is high, the potential return large, and VC investors get closely involved, often on the board or in key management roles.

HEART: The stages follow the company. Picture a home baker with a great cookie recipe. The formative stage has three phases. Pre seed capital, or angel investing, comes at the idea stage, small sums usually from wealthy individuals rather than funds, for a business plan and a test of demand. Seed stage financing pays for product development, marketing and market research, and it is usually where VC funds first invest. Early stage, or start up stage, financing funds operations in the run up to production and sales, the commercial kitchen before the first shipment.

LEWIS: Later stage, or expansion, financing comes once production and sales have begun and pays for growth, product improvement and marketing, and the founders often sell control to the VC investors here. Then mezzanine stage financing prepares the company for an initial public offering, an IPO. The trap. Mezzanine stage describes timing, not the type of security. Mezzanine financing means hybrids of debt and equity, such as convertibles. Mezzanine stage money can take that form, but more often it is equity or short term debt.

HEART: One more equity category. Minority equity investing, also called developmental capital, buys stakes short of control in public or private companies that need money to grow or restructure. For a public company, a common route is a private investment in public equity, a PIPE, where shares are sold privately to institutions, faster and cheaper than a public offering, with fewer disclosures.

LEWIS: Now exits. Funds typically hold a company for about five years. A trade sale sells it, or part of it, to a strategic buyer such as a competitor, through a direct sale or an auction. The buyer often pays a premium because it expects synergies, and the sale is faster and cheaper than an IPO. But management and employees may resist, and the pool of buyers is small.

HEART: A public listing comes three ways. The IPO is the most common. Banks underwrite it, it usually fetches the highest price, and the visibility can lift the value of the shares the fund keeps, but costs and compliance burdens are high and the market may not welcome it. The best candidates are big companies with steady finances and a clear plan, in industries that are still growing. A direct listing floats existing shares without underwriters, which is cheaper but raises no new capital.

LEWIS: And a special purpose acquisition company, a SPAC, or blank check company, raises money solely to buy an unnamed private company within a deadline, or else returns the money. It can be more flexible than an IPO, reduce uncertainty about valuation and reach the sponsors' regular investors. The drawbacks are dilution from the SPAC's shares and warrants, a gap between the SPAC's value and the target's, deal risk, overhang from SPAC holders selling after the announcement, and growing regulatory scrutiny.

HEART: A recapitalization has the company borrow money and pay it out as a dividend. Strictly, that is not an exit, because the fund keeps control. It is the corporate version of a cash out refinance, where the homeowner keeps the house and takes equity out with a bigger loan. A secondary sale sells the company to another private equity firm or investor group. And a failure ends in a write off or liquidation.

LEWIS: On performance, private equity funds have earned more on average than public stocks, with a higher standard deviation that reflects illiquidity and leverage. But the indexes rely on self reporting, so survivorship and backfill bias overstate returns, and infrequent revaluation biases volatility and correlation downward.

HEART: Now private debt, lending directly to private entities, in five types. Direct lending means loans straight to a private company with no intermediary, typically senior and secured, with covenants to protect the lender. Banks know these borrowers well, because these funds chase the same midsize businesses that a bank's commercial lending team serves. A leveraged loan, in this setting, means the private debt fund borrows money to make its loans, leveraging its own portfolio to magnify returns. Bank lenders often mean something else by the phrase, a loan to a company that already carries heavy debt, so keep the two senses apart.

LEWIS: Venture debt lends to start ups that are not yet profitable, often convertible or with warrants, and founders like it because they keep ownership and control. Mezzanine debt is subordinated to senior secured debt and often carries conversion rights or warrants to pay for that junior position. Distressed debt is the debt of mature companies in financial trouble, such as default or bankruptcy, and the fund often helps restructure it. Some distressed investors back good companies with temporary cash problems, expecting recovery, while others take on turnarounds and help run the company.

HEART: Unitranche debt merges secured and unsecured debt into one loan at a blended rate, ranking between senior and subordinated debt. Picture a home buyer who, instead of a first mortgage plus a second loan, takes one loan at a blended rate.

LEWIS: Private debt pays more than traditional bonds, for more risk and less liquidity, and adds diversification through relatively low correlation. The rate is usually set over a reference rate such as SOFR, the Secured Overnight Financing Rate, so it floats like an adjustable rate loan. The risks are illiquidity and default, and it takes specialized knowledge of the debt structure, the borrower's life cycle and the collateral. Senior debt has less risk and steadier yields, while mezzanine offers more upside.

HEART: On diversification, private capital helps, but keep it in proportion. Measured correlations with public markets are below one but clearly positive, and smoothing means the true figure is likely higher. Timing matters too. A fund's vintage year, the year of its first investment, shapes its results. A fund launched into an expansion tends to do best backing early stage companies, while one launched into a contraction tends to do best with distressed companies. Since nobody knows which kind of year is coming, investors should diversify across vintage years, committing a steady amount each year.

LEWIS: And the risk ladder, highest risk and return first. Private equity, then mezzanine debt, unitranche debt, senior direct lending, senior real estate debt, and infrastructure debt at the bottom.

HEART: The takeaway. Buyouts borrow to buy established companies, venture capital backs young ones stage by stage, and a recapitalization is not a true exit. Private debt runs from senior direct lending to mezzanine and distressed debt, mostly at floating rates, with risk rising as the claim gets more junior.

## Module 79.1, Real Estate

LEWIS: Real estate pays through rent and potential appreciation, owned directly or indirectly through partnerships and public securities. The main types are single family residential and commercial, and the four largest commercial categories are office, shopping, industrial, which takes in warehouses and distribution centers, and rental residential, meaning rental houses and apartment buildings.

HEART: Organize the forms as a quadrant built on two questions, private or public, and equity or debt. Private means direct. You buy a property, alone or through a partnership where the general partner manages it and the limited partners supply the money, or you lend to a buyer. Public means owning securities that are claims on property: shares of a real estate investment trust, or REIT, of a real estate company, or of exchange traded funds, plus residential and commercial mortgage backed securities, mortgage REITs and mortgage ETFs.

LEWIS: A credit union sits in more than one box. The mortgages and commercial property loans it makes are private debt. The mortgage backed securities it buys are public debt.

HEART: Equity versus debt is control versus priority. The equity owner decides how much to borrow, how to manage the property and when to sell. The lender holds a mortgage or mortgage backed securities secured by the property, with the higher priority claim in a default. Because the lender is repaid first, the equity is worth the property's value minus the debt, which every homeowner knows as home equity.

LEWIS: Private holdings are larger, because a building cannot be split and is hard to sell, and they need property management expertise. Public vehicles split the ownership instead of the building, so they are liquid, spread money across many properties and come with professional managers.

HEART: Direct ownership offers control over buying, financing, improvements, tenants and timing. It offers diversification, since real estate is less than perfectly correlated with stocks and bonds. And it offers tax benefits, through deductions for depreciation, a noncash expense even while the property may be gaining value, and for mortgage interest. The drawbacks are illiquidity and opaque pricing, complex management, the need for local market knowledge, a large initial investment, and concentration risk.

LEWIS: Indirect routes include partnerships, joint ventures and public securities. REITs are popular for income property because they are exempt from double taxation, so the income is not taxed once inside the company and again for the investor. They trade on exchanges, so unlike a mutual fund they never have to sell properties to meet redemptions. They bring expert managers, report standard measures such as earnings per share, and sometimes report non GAAP measures such as net asset value. Equity REITs own property, mortgage REITs lend or hold mortgage backed securities, and hybrid REITs do both.

HEART: Strategies rank by risk. Core strategies buy high quality, stable properties, like a fully leased, grocery anchored shopping center, and core vehicles are typically open ended with no set end date, so investors can come and go. Riskier strategies usually come in closed end vehicles with a fixed life span. Core plus adds modest development and redevelopment, like refreshing a lobby and filling empty units. Value add redevelops on a bigger scale, like gutting a tired office tower. Opportunistic goes furthest, with large scale repurposing, such as turning a dying shopping center into apartments, buying distressed properties, or betting on a market upturn.

LEWIS: That ranking drives the risk profile. First mortgages and investment grade commercial mortgage backed securities are the least risky and behave like bonds. Core strategies are also bond like, collecting steady rent from many tenants, much like coupons. Core plus, value add and opportunistic strategies are equity like, with value add and opportunistic carrying the most risk.

HEART: REITs offer diversification across many properties plus liquidity. The trap is that REITs move more closely with stocks than direct real estate does, and correlations rise in steep downturns. Even so, real estate improves the risk and return profile of a traditional portfolio.

LEWIS: As background, appraisers value property through three lenses. Sales comparison emphasizes the market, what similar properties recently sold for, adjusted for differences. The income approach emphasizes cash flow. It divides net operating income, rent after vacancies and operating costs but before any mortgage payments, by a market capitalization rate, so a higher cap rate means a lower value, or it discounts expected cash flows. The cost approach emphasizes replacement, the land plus what an equivalent building would cost new, less wear and obsolescence, and it works best for newer buildings. And because appraisals lean on past sales and past values, appraisal based returns lag and look smooth, the level three problem again.

HEART: The takeaway. Sort real estate by private or public and by equity or debt. Senior debt and core behave like bonds, while core plus, value add and opportunistic behave like equity, and REITs add liquidity but move more with stocks.

## Module 79.2, Infrastructure

HEART: Infrastructure means long lasting, capital intensive assets that deliver essential services, in four groups. Transportation, such as highways, airports, seaports and rail lines. Utilities, such as gas distribution, power generation and transmission, and water and waste treatment. Information and communication technology, such as cell towers and cable networks. And social infrastructure, such as schools, health care facilities and prisons.

LEWIS: An investor can build an asset and then sell or lease it to the government or run it, buy an existing asset from a government to lease back or operate, or join a public private partnership.

HEART: Cash comes in three forms. Availability payments reward keeping the asset available, whether traffic is heavy or light, like a state paying an operator a fixed sum to keep a courthouse open and in good repair. Usage based payments depend on demand, like a bridge toll. Take or pay arrangements require a buyer to pay a minimum price for an agreed volume whether or not it takes delivery, the way a utility might commit to pipeline capacity. Availability payments behave like a government's promise to pay. Usage leaves the investor exposed to how many cars or customers show up.

LEWIS: A greenfield investment is an asset still to be built. It typically follows a build, operate, transfer life cycle, with cash going out during construction, cash coming in and growing as the private investor operates it, and then a handover to a government or a third party. A brownfield investment expands or privatizes an existing asset. A sale leaseback, buying an asset from the government and leasing it back, is one brownfield structure, and a city selling its parking garages to an operator is another. A fully operational facility that needs no further development is a secondary stage investment. Green fields are empty. Brown fields are built on.

HEART: Direct ownership is highly illiquid, because these assets are huge and last for decades. More liquid routes include exchange traded funds, mutual funds, private equity funds and master limited partnerships, but listed vehicles are a small slice of the universe and concentrated in a few asset types. Infrastructure debt can be privately placed or publicly traded.

LEWIS: Brownfield assets provide stable cash flows and relatively high current yields, with little growth, and secondary stage brownfield assets are the least risky with the lowest returns. Greenfield assets carry more uncertainty and may yield less in the near term, but they offer more growth. The riskiest are assets whose revenue depends on uncertain future demand.

HEART: Long term contracts and high barriers to entry, since nobody builds a second airport beside the first, make infrastructure equity cash flows stable, with low correlation to public equities. Infrastructure debt is safer still and less affected by the economic cycle. Greenfield assets in developing economies carry extra risk, yet over long periods they have rewarded investors as incomes there climb. The risks to watch are regulation, leverage, cash flows falling short of expectations, construction risk on new builds, and operational risk when a private owner runs the asset.

LEWIS: The natural owners are long term institutions such as pension plans, life insurers and sovereign wealth funds, whose horizons and obligations stretch decades ahead. On valuation, infrastructure is usually priced by discounting the asset's long dated cash flows, so the emphasis falls on the contract terms, the demand forecast and the discount rate, and values are sensitive to interest rates, much like long bonds.

HEART: The takeaway. Greenfield is not yet built, with construction risk and more growth. Brownfield already exists, with steadier, higher current yields, and secondary stage is the safest. Demand based revenue is the riskiest.

## Recall check

LEWIS Q: An investor moves from fund investing to co investing and then to direct investing. What happens to fees, control and the expertise it needs?

HEART: Answer. Fees fall, while control and the expertise required both rise. Here is why. In a fund, the manager does all the work, charges for it and decides every deal. Co investing puts some money into deals alongside the manager at lower overall fees. Direct investing removes the outside manager entirely, so the investor must find, evaluate and check every deal itself, with bigger minimums and usually less diversification.

HEART Q: Which hurdle favors the limited partners, a hard hurdle or a soft hurdle?

LEWIS: Answer. The hard hurdle. Here is why. A hard hurdle charges the performance fee only on gains above the hurdle, so investors keep the hurdle return free of that fee. A soft hurdle, once cleared, charges the fee on the entire gain, which pays the manager more. A catch up clause has a similar effect to a soft hurdle.

LEWIS Q: A fund falls one year and climbs back to its old peak the next. Under a high water mark, is a performance fee due on that recovery?

HEART: Answer. No. Here is why. A high water mark charges performance fees only on value above the highest previous net of fees value. Gains that merely make up for earlier losses earn the manager nothing, so investors never pay twice for the same gains.

HEART Q: Which waterfall favors the general partner, and which provision protects the limited partners under it?

LEWIS: Answer. The deal by deal, or American, waterfall favors the general partner, and the clawback protects the limited partners. Here is why. Deal by deal lets the manager take performance fees on each winner as it is sold, before investors have their capital and the hurdle back. If losers come later, the manager may have been paid on gains the fund as a whole never made, and the clawback lets the limited partners recover that excess.

LEWIS Q: A private equity fund in its third year reports a negative cumulative return. Is that a sign of a poor manager?

HEART: Answer. Not by itself. Here is why. Private funds follow a J curve. Early on the fund is calling capital, paying fees and buying holdings that take years to mature, so returns are negative in the commitment phase and improve only gradually during deployment. Gains arrive mainly in the distribution phase, which is why funds are compared within the same vintage year.

HEART Q: Two funds each returned three times the money invested. Why might their IRRs differ sharply?

LEWIS: Answer. Because the multiple of invested capital ignores timing and the IRR does not. Here is why. The multiple compares total value returned with total capital paid in, so tripling money in four years and in twelve years look identical. The IRR is a money weighted return that counts when each dollar went in and came out, so the faster fund has the far higher IRR.

LEWIS Q: Most of a fund's holdings are valued with level three inputs. How does that likely distort its reported return, volatility and correlation with stocks?

HEART: Answer. Return is likely biased up, while volatility and correlation are biased down. Here is why. Level three values rest on unobservable assumptions and can sit near cost for long periods, so they move far less than true values would. The smooth numbers make the fund look safer and less tied to public markets than it is, and they may not reflect what an exit would fetch.

HEART Q: Do survivorship bias and backfill bias make a hedge fund index look better or worse than reality?

LEWIS: Answer. Better, both of them. Here is why. Survivorship bias drops failed funds from the database, so their losses vanish from the history. Backfill bias lets managers add only their successful funds, often with their strong past returns attached. Either way the index overstates returns and understates risk.

LEWIS Q: A portfolio company borrows money to pay its private equity owner a large dividend. Has the fund exited the investment?

HEART: Answer. No, that is a recapitalization. Here is why. The fund still owns and controls the company. It has pulled cash out by adding debt, like a homeowner doing a cash out refinance. A true exit means selling, through a trade sale, a public listing or a secondary sale, or writing the investment off.

HEART Q: What is the difference between mezzanine stage financing and mezzanine debt?

LEWIS: Answer. Mezzanine stage is about timing, while mezzanine debt is about the type of claim. Here is why. Mezzanine stage financing is venture money that prepares a company for an IPO, and it is more often equity or short term debt. Mezzanine debt is subordinated private debt that ranks behind senior secured lenders and often carries warrants or conversion rights.

LEWIS Q: Which real estate investments behave most like bonds, and which most like equity?

HEART: Answer. First mortgages, investment grade commercial mortgage backed securities and core strategies behave like bonds, while core plus, value add and opportunistic strategies behave like equity. Here is why. Senior debt collects contractual payments backed by the property, and core properties collect steady rent from many tenants. Strategies that develop, redevelop or speculate depend on construction, leasing and the market, so their returns swing like equity.

HEART Q: An investor wants the lowest risk infrastructure exposure. Should it pick a secondary stage brownfield asset or a greenfield asset, and what kind of revenue is riskiest?

LEWIS: Answer. The secondary stage brownfield asset, and revenue tied to uncertain demand is the riskiest. Here is why. A secondary stage asset is built and fully operating, with no construction risk, and it pays stable cash with relatively high current yields but little growth. A greenfield asset trades construction risk and lower near term yields for growth. And revenue from usage, like tolls, swings with traffic, while availability payments do not.

## Key takeaways

HEART: First, a few memory hooks.

LEWIS: For the three ways in, renovate a house. Hire the contractor, work beside the contractor, or be the contractor. The more you do yourself, the lower the fees, the more control, and the more you need to know.

HEART: For the partnership, the general partner has general control and general liability. The limited partners have limited say and limited liability.

LEWIS: For the management fee, hedge funds charge on assets and private equity on commitments, so the manager is never paid for rushing.

HEART: For hurdles, hard means a fee only above the bar. Soft means clear the bar and the fee covers everything. A catch up acts like a soft hurdle. And a high water mark means no fee for getting back to even.

LEWIS: For waterfalls, deal by deal pays the dealmaker early. Whole of fund makes the investors whole first. And the clawback claws back fees on gains that later vanished.

HEART: For the life cycle, commit, deploy, distribute. The J curve dips before it climbs, like an orchard. The IRR knows when. The money multiple only knows how much.

LEWIS: For valuations and indexes, rarely priced looks rarely risky. Level three values, survivorship and backfill all flatter the numbers.

HEART: For venture stages, angel, seed, start up, expansion, mezzanine. Idea, product, launch, growth, listing. Mezzanine stage is a when, mezzanine debt is a what. A recapitalization is a refinance, not an exit.

LEWIS: For the private capital ladder, top to bottom: equity, mezzanine, unitranche, senior direct lending, senior real estate debt, infrastructure debt.

HEART: For real estate, a lease pays like a coupon and a redevelopment behaves like a start up. Senior debt and core are bond like. Core plus, value add and opportunistic are equity like. For infrastructure, green fields are empty and brown fields are built on, secondary stage is the safest, and revenue that depends on demand is the riskiest.

LEWIS: Now, how you would say the big ideas to a senior leader.

HEART: On why alternatives: they can raise returns and spread risk, partly because we are paid for locking money up, but the diversification can shrink in a crisis. The jargon is the illiquidity premium, low correlation, and correlations that rise in stress.

LEWIS: On fees: pay the manager mainly for results above a minimum, only after our own capital comes back, never twice for the same gains, and get money back if early wins are undone. The jargon is the hurdle rate, the high water mark, the whole of fund waterfall and the clawback.

HEART: On reported performance: private holdings look calm because they are priced rarely, and the published averages leave out the failures, so true risk is higher than reported. The jargon is level three fair value, appraisal smoothing, survivorship bias and backfill bias.

LEWIS: On private credit: nonbank funds lend senior, floating rate money to the same midsize businesses that banks serve, earning more in exchange for less liquidity. The jargon is direct lending, private debt and floating rates set over SOFR.

HEART: And on real assets: fully leased buildings and operating utilities pay like bonds, while new construction and anything that depends on demand behaves like equity. The jargon is core versus value add and opportunistic real estate, and brownfield versus greenfield infrastructure.
