LEWIS: Reading 80 covers farmland, timberland and commodities: where their returns come from, how a futures price relates to today's price, and why commodities can diversify a portfolio. Reading 81 covers hedge funds, their strategy families, structures, fees and the biases in their reported returns, and Reading 82 covers how a distributed ledger works, the main kinds of digital assets, and how they behave as investments.

## Module 80.1, Farmland, Timberland, and Commodities

HEART: Natural resources means three things: raw land, land that grows crops or trees, and commodities, the raw materials themselves. You can own them directly, through pooled vehicles such as ETFs, REITs, limited partnerships and limited liability companies, or through derivatives such as commodity futures and swaps.

LEWIS: Raw land is undeveloped. Farmland grows crops or raises livestock. Timberland is forest managed for its wood. None of the three is quick to sell, so all are illiquid, and location does most of the work in setting their value. For farms and forests that means practical things: nearness to highways, rail lines and buyers, reliable water, and good soil.

HEART: Any of the three can be leased out for rent, and any of them can rise in value. Farmland and timberland add a third source of income, their output: the harvest or the timber.

LEWIS: The owners differ. Farmland is mostly held by individuals, think of family farms. Raw land and timberland are more often held by institutions. Timber tracts are far larger than farm parcels, and running a forest takes specialist skill, so investors without it can hire a timberland investment management organization. Financing is thinner than for houses or office buildings, mostly bank loans or private debt, and farmland REITs are a newer way for small investors to buy a slice.

HEART: Now the difference the exam loves. Crops have a harvest window. When wheat is ripe it has to be cut, whatever the price that week. Trees do not. If lumber prices are weak, the owner leaves the trees standing and they keep growing, so the inventory stores itself on the stump. The timber owner chooses when to harvest, based on today's prices and how fast the trees are still growing.

LEWIS: Here is the trap. If a question asks which land investment gives the owner more control over when the output is sold, the answer is timberland.

HEART: Farmers manage price risk another way. A corn grower who plants in spring can sell corn futures, a short position, to lock in a price for part of the expected crop. If corn prices fall by autumn, the gain on the futures offsets the lower price for the actual corn. And because growing plants, trees included, pull carbon from the air, farmland and timberland appeal to investors with an ESG focus on climate change.

LEWIS: So what drives the return? Changes in the price of the land, and changes in the price of what comes off it. For timberland, the land's value depends on expected lumber prices and on how much timber is still standing, since a forest that was just harvested has already sold its most valuable inventory. For farmland, the return depends on land prices, crop prices, and the quality and quantity of the harvest.

HEART: The risks are low liquidity, cash flows that swing with the weather, and heavy losses from natural disasters such as drought, flood, wildfire or disease. A farm lender watches the same two drivers, land values and crop prices, because together they set the value of the collateral and the borrower's ability to repay.

LEWIS: Now commodities, raw materials where one unit is as good as another of the same grade. There are three sectors. Metals, split into base or industrial metals such as copper, and precious metals such as gold. Agricultural products, such as grains, coffee and cattle. And energy, such as crude oil and natural gas. Contracts also spell out a grade, meaning the quality, and a delivery location, because light crude at one pipeline hub is a different product from heavy crude somewhere else.

HEART: Governments shape these markets more than most. Some subsidize food for consumers or support prices for farmers. Many control who may mine or drill, and some run the producers themselves. Climate policy cuts both ways: rules that reduce demand for fossil fuels raise demand for battery metals such as lithium, cobalt and nickel.

LEWIS: You can buy the physical good, a bar of gold or a silo of grain, but physical goods cost money to store, insure and move. So most investors turn to derivatives, mainly futures, along with forwards and options on futures. Futures trade on exchanges, and the clearinghouse guarantees each trade, so you are not relying on the trader on the other side. That is why exchange traded futures are treated as carrying essentially no counterparty risk, unlike a privately negotiated forward.

HEART: There are other routes. Exchange traded products, meaning ETFs and exchange traded notes, suit investors whose mandates only let them buy shares. They can hold the commodity or futures, and track a price or an index. Managed futures funds are run by commodity trading advisers, or C T As, who trade actively, either in one sector or across many. They come as limited partnerships with hedge fund style fees and limits on who may invest, or as publicly traded fund shares with lower minimums and more liquidity. Separately managed accounts suit large investors who want a custom portfolio, and specialized funds focus on one corner, such as oil and gas or precious metals.

LEWIS: Now the pricing idea. Picture a coffee roaster who needs beans a year from now. She can buy them today and warehouse them for a year, or sign a futures contract today and pay on delivery. Buying today ties up cash that could earn interest, and she pays to store and insure the beans. So the futures price should sit above today's price by roughly those costs.

HEART: But owning the beans has a benefit. If a frost wipes out a harvest, the roaster with beans in her warehouse keeps roasting while competitors scramble. That benefit of having the physical good on hand is the convenience yield. It is not paid in cash, and the futures buyer does not get it, so it pulls the futures price down.

LEWIS: In words, the futures price is roughly the spot price grown at the risk free rate, plus storage costs, minus the convenience yield. More compactly, futures equal spot plus the net cost of carry, which is the interest and storage cost of holding the good, less its convenience yield.

HEART: That gives two curve shapes. When convenience yield is small, carrying costs win and futures sit above spot. That is contango. When convenience yield is high enough to outweigh the carrying costs, the net cost of carry turns negative and futures sit below spot. That is backwardation. Plentiful inventories tend to go with contango, and tight supplies with backwardation.

LEWIS: Why does the shape matter? A long only investor in commodity futures earns three pieces. The spot price return, which is the change in the commodity's price. Roll yield, from selling each contract as it nears expiration and buying a later one to keep the exposure. And collateral yield, the interest on the cash or Treasury bills backing the position.

HEART: Roll yield is where the shape bites. A futures price drifts toward spot as expiration nears. In contango the investor keeps buying above spot, and if spot does not move, each contract drifts down, so every roll costs a little. In backwardation the investor keeps buying below spot, each contract drifts up, and every roll adds a little. So contango drags on a long only investor's return, and backwardation helps.

LEWIS: Here is the trap. Roll yield comes from the curve's shape, not from whether prices are rising. An investor in a market stuck in contango can lose money while spot creeps higher. And collateral yield is just interest on the cash, whatever the curve looks like.

HEART: What moves spot prices is supply and demand. On the demand side, it comes down to how much end users need the commodity and how strong the global economy is. On the supply side, it is production, storage costs and the inventories already on hand. In the short run, supply barely responds to price. You cannot open a copper mine in a month, and a farmer plants once a season. With supply that inelastic, a demand swing over the business cycle, or a supply shock such as a hurricane, forces prices to move a long way. That is why commodity prices are volatile.

LEWIS: Agriculture adds weather and plant disease, so a poor harvest sends prices up and a bumper crop pushes them down. Extraction gets more expensive as producers move to deeper wells and more remote deposits. So producers and investors both watch inventories, production forecasts, government policy and expected economic growth.

HEART: Now the history. In recent decades commodities have earned higher returns than global stocks or bonds, with higher volatility. Timberland and farmland have also earned higher average returns, with lower volatility than global stocks and about the same as global bonds. One caution: land values usually come from appraisals that change slowly, so part of that smoothness reflects how the values are measured. And speculators who correctly call short term moves can earn high returns over short periods.

LEWIS: Then diversification. Commodity returns have had low correlation with global stocks and bonds, so a modest allocation can reduce portfolio risk. Commodity prices tend to rise with inflation, which makes them an inflation hedge. When inflation runs high they have tended to beat stocks and bonds, and when it is low they have tended to lag. They are also more sensitive to geopolitical events and weather, such as a war near a shipping lane or a drought in a grain belt.

HEART: The takeaway. Land returns come from the land's value and the price of its output, and timber can wait while crops cannot. Futures equal spot plus the net cost of carry, convenience yield pulls them down, and for a long only investor contango costs while backwardation helps.

## Module 81.1, Hedge Funds

LEWIS: A hedge fund is a private pool of money open only to qualified or accredited investors, meaning institutions and wealthy individuals judged able to bear the risk. The early funds held stocks they liked and shorted stocks they did not, aiming to make money whichever way the market went. Today the label covers many strategies, and some hedge very little.

HEART: They are lightly regulated compared with mutual funds, their managers have broad freedom, and they can go long and short, add leverage and trade derivatives. Their raw material is market inefficiency, meaning prices that are wrong, and price volatility, meaning prices that move enough to trade. Instead of asking whether they beat an index, investors often grade them on total return or on risk adjusted return.

LEWIS: Anyone can buy into a mutual fund, a REIT or an ETF, because they are publicly traded. A hedge fund is sold privately. Compared with private equity, hedge funds hold mostly liquid securities, work on a shorter horizon, and allow periodic redemptions, while private equity ties money up for years. Managers often invest their own money in their funds, and the performance fee usually has a high water mark. It is paid only when the fund's value rises above its highest previous value, so a manager earns nothing for merely recovering a loss.

HEART: Now the four strategy families: equity hedge, event driven, relative value and opportunistic. For each, ask what the bet is and what can go wrong.

LEWIS: Equity hedge funds go long and short stocks and stock derivatives, and when the manager wants less market risk without a view on particular companies, the short can be an index. There are five types. Fundamental long short buys undervalued stocks and shorts a basket or an index, hoping to show its skill when the market corrects. Most stay net long, so a broad sell off still hurts. Fundamental growth buys fast growers and shorts companies with little or no revenue growth. It is also usually net long, and it suffers when growth disappoints. Fundamental value buys cheap shares and shorts expensive ones, so its results ride on value versus growth, and value can trail for years.

HEART: Market neutral holds roughly equal amounts of undervalued longs and overvalued shorts, so the market's direction should not matter, only relative performance. Those relative moves are small, so managers often add leverage, which magnifies the damage when the longs and shorts move the wrong way. Short bias funds are mostly short, with negative market exposure overall. It is a contrarian strategy that hunts for flawed business plans or questionable accounting, and it swims against the current, because markets rise more often than they fall.

LEWIS: The second family, event driven, trades around corporate events such as takeovers and restructurings, in common stock, preferred stock or debt, and it tends to be long biased. Merger arbitrage is the classic. When one company agrees to buy another, the target's shares trade a little below the offer, because the deal might fail. The manager buys the target and shorts the acquirer, betting the deal completes. Despite the name, it is not risk free. Terms can change, regulators can block the deal, or the buyer can walk away, and then the target falls hard. Many small gains with an occasional large loss looks a lot like selling insurance against deals failing.

HEART: Distressed or restructuring funds buy securities of troubled companies when they expect a successful restructuring to raise their value, sometimes shorting overvalued securities as well. The risk is a restructuring that fails or drags on while the securities are hard to sell. Activist funds buy enough shares to push for change, such as a new strategy, new management or more cash returned to shareholders, and they risk a long, costly fight with the board. Special situations funds trade around new issues and buybacks of securities, spinoffs, asset sales and capital payouts.

LEWIS: The third family, relative value, buys one security and shorts a related one, betting that a pricing gap will disappear. Convertible arbitrage trades mispricings between a company's convertible bonds and its stock and options. Specific fixed income works pricing and credit quality gaps in asset backed, mortgage backed and high yield bonds. General fixed income works across many issuers and types, and multistrategy hunts within and across asset classes. The danger is a gap that widens before it narrows. The gaps are small, so these funds often borrow heavily, and a widening gap plus leverage can force selling at the worst moment.

HEART: The fourth family, opportunistic, centers on macro events and commodity trading. Macro funds bet on global economic trends and events, long or short across stocks, bonds, currencies and commodities. Picture a manager who expects one central bank to cut rates while another holds steady. These funds thrive on volatility around major events, so when central banks smooth out shocks there is less to trade. Managed futures funds trade commodity futures, the C T As we met earlier, or financial futures. Commodities tend to correct themselves: high prices choke off demand, and low prices discourage supply.

LEWIS: So in one line: equity hedge trades stocks, event driven trades corporate events, relative value trades price gaps, and opportunistic trades the big picture.

HEART: Now the vehicle itself. Compared with mutual funds and REITs, hedge funds differ in four ways. Less regulation and more flexible mandates. Higher costs, because an incentive fee sits on top of a usually high management fee. Lower liquidity. And less transparency.

LEWIS: The liquidity limits come in three tools. A lockup period is the stretch after you invest when you cannot redeem, or, in a soft lockup, can redeem only by paying a significant fee. A notice period is the time the fund has to meet a redemption request once the lockup is over. A liquidity gate is a partial restriction that limits how much can leave at once without suspending redemptions altogether.

HEART: Why the rules? Selling in a hurry is expensive. Redemption fees offset trading costs, and notice periods and gates give the manager time to shrink positions in an orderly way. Redemptions also pile up after poor performance, when selling hurts most, so the cost lands on the investors who stay. It is the same worry a bank has about a run on deposits.

LEWIS: As for transparency, funds disclose little about strategies and holdings, to protect proprietary methods, which makes it hard for investors and analysts to value what the fund owns.

HEART: Now the investment forms. A commingled fund pools many investors' money, and a separately managed account serves one large investor. Commingled funds often have a master feeder structure. Picture two funnels feeding one tank. An onshore feeder takes money from domestic investors, an offshore feeder in a low tax jurisdiction takes money from investors abroad and tax exempt investors, and both pour into a master fund that does all the investing. It is tax efficient, brings economies of scale, and opens the door to investors worldwide.

LEWIS: A separately managed account is customized to one investor's risk and return goals, but it has drawbacks. The manager has no personal money in it, so interests are less aligned than in a fund where the manager invests alongside you. Someone has to monitor its operations closely, so it suits large or institutional investors with the staff to do that. And lower negotiated fees may come with only the manager's most liquid trades.

HEART: Legally, hedge funds are usually limited partnerships or limited liability companies, with the manager as general partner, paid largely on performance. The terms sit in the fund documents, such as the partnership agreement or the private placement memorandum, and funds are normally set up with no fixed end date.

LEWIS: On fees, briefly, since the last episode walked through the mechanics. The management fee is a share of assets, paid whatever happens. The incentive fee is a share of profits, often subject to a high water mark and sometimes a hurdle rate. The old standard of two percent of assets plus twenty percent of profits is under pressure. Some newer funds charge a smaller management fee with a bigger share of profits, measured against a benchmark rather than on total return, which pays the manager for beating the market rather than riding it. The overall effect is a steady drag on net returns, and an incentive fee gives the manager a share of the upside without a matching share of the downside.

HEART: The indirect route is a fund of funds, which invests in a portfolio of hedge funds. It offers diversification across strategies, expert manager selection, access for smaller investors to funds they could not reach alone, and often shorter lockups and easier exits. The price is a second layer of fees on top of the underlying funds' fees, which can take a big bite out of net returns.

LEWIS: With so little transparency, due diligence matters more here than almost anywhere. Check what the strategy bets on, how hard the holdings are to value, whether the redemption terms match the liquidity of the assets, the full fee load, and whether the manager has real money in the fund.

HEART: Now where returns come from, in three layers. Market beta is return from broad market exposure, which an index fund sells cheaply. Strategy beta is return from the particular exposures the strategy holds, such as a value tilt or the merger spread. Alpha is the extra return from the manager's security selection. Leverage magnifies strategy beta and alpha, and high fees drag on all of it. The trap is paying alpha fees for beta.

LEWIS: Then the measurement problem. Hedge fund indexes rely on voluntary reporting, and funds doing badly are less likely to report, so indexes tend to overstate returns and can distort correlations with traditional assets. Three biases have names.

HEART: Survivorship bias: indexes often admit funds only after a minimum time or size, and failing funds stop reporting, so early failures never show up and the index mainly reflects survivors. Selection bias: index providers may assign funds to categories inconsistently or set different entry rules, so two indexes of the same strategy can describe different groups of funds. Backfill bias: when a fund joins, its earlier returns are added to the index history, and since funds with strong histories are keenest to join, past index returns get pushed up.

LEWIS: Picture marathon results that never list the runners who dropped out, and that let anyone joining partway submit their best training times for the miles they skipped. The field would look faster than it was.

HEART: On diversification, results depend heavily on the period measured, but hedge funds as a group have offered diversification benefits to traditional portfolios. They are more correlated with stocks than with bonds, which fits how many strategies lean net long equities. With proper due diligence, they can earn a place in a traditional portfolio.

LEWIS: The takeaway. Hedge funds trade flexibility for cost, liquidity and transparency. Know the four families by their bets, ask how much of a return is cheap beta versus real alpha, and remember that published track records are flattered by the funds that are missing.

## Module 82.1, Distributed Ledger Technology

HEART: Digital assets can be created, stored and transferred electronically: cryptocurrencies, tokens and digital collectibles. They are secured and validated with distributed ledger technology, often called blockchain technology. Lock in one distinction now. A cryptocurrency runs on a blockchain of its own, while a token is issued on top of a chain that someone else already built.

LEWIS: Picture a running club tracking who has paid dues. The old way, the treasurer keeps the only official list and everyone trusts the treasurer. With a distributed ledger, every member holds an identical copy of the full payment history, and the copies are kept in agreement. In other words, one database that every participant holds in full, with the same complete history of transactions.

HEART: The benefits are accuracy, transparency and security, fast transfers of ownership, and peer to peer dealing without a middleman. The drawbacks are data protection and privacy concerns, since so much is shared, and the heavy computing power needed to verify transactions.

LEWIS: A network has three parts: the ledger, a consensus mechanism and the participants. The consensus mechanism is how everyone agrees on the current state of the ledger, by validating new transactions and then updating it. That makes entries immutable, meaning they cannot be changed afterward, and visible to every participant. Cryptography scrambles the data so unauthorized parties cannot get at it.

HEART: Ledgers can also run smart contracts, computer programs that execute themselves when preset terms are met. Picture flight delay insurance that pays the traveler the moment the airline's data shows a long delay, with no claim form, or an escrow that releases payment as soon as shipping data confirms delivery.

LEWIS: The most common design is the blockchain. Transactions are grouped into blocks and added in order, and each block carries a secure link to the one before it, called a hash. Think of a hash as a digital fingerprint. The same data always gives the same fingerprint, but change one character and it changes completely. So altering an old transaction breaks the link to every later block, and tampering shows. New transactions join the chain only after being validated.

HEART: How do strangers agree on the next block? Through a consensus protocol, the rules for chaining blocks together, designed to prevent manipulation. The two main types are proof of work and proof of stake.

LEWIS: In proof of work, miners run powerful computers to solve a cryptographic puzzle. The winner validates the block, locks it into the chain and is rewarded with cryptocurrency. It burns enormous energy, and that cost is the protection. Rewriting history would require one party to control most of the network, a fifty one percent attack, and the more participants there are, the harder that gets, so a proof of work chain is sturdier with many participants. Proof of work was the original method, and it still secures Bitcoin, the largest network.

HEART: In proof of stake there is no puzzle race. Participants called validators pledge their own coins as collateral, which is called staking. A validator signals that a block is ready, other validators check it, and validators earn a return on their stake, while one that cheats can lose part of it. The honest validators' combined stake is what guards the network, so an attacker would need a majority of the staked coins, and the whole process needs far less energy. Ethereum switched from proof of work to proof of stake in twenty twenty two.

LEWIS: Here is the trap. Proof of work means miners, computing power and energy. Proof of stake means validators and collateral at risk.

HEART: Next, access. A permissionless network is like a public park. Anyone can join, all transactions are visible to all users, and any participant can make transactions and perform any network function. Bitcoin is the standard example. Transactions are confirmed by consensus rather than by a central authority, they cannot be manipulated once they are on the chain, and the parties need not trust each other, only the protocol.

LEWIS: A permissioned network is more like a private club. Users can be restricted from some activities, with access levels ranging from adding transactions down to view only, for a regulator, say. Restricting access makes it more cost effective to run than an open, decentralized network. A group of banks settling trades among themselves fits here.

HEART: Now the asset types, in two families, cryptocurrencies and tokens. A cryptocurrency is digital money issued privately, with no central bank backing, that parties can send almost instantly without an intermediary. Many build in a ceiling on how many units can ever exist, so that scarcity helps them act as a store of value, yet prices still swing wildly.

LEWIS: Bitcoin was the first and is the largest. Altcoins build on the technology Bitcoin introduced but design their blockchains differently. The best known is Ether, the currency of the Ethereum network. Ethereum is programmable, so smart contracts and applications run on it, which means its value rests on more than being a store of value.

HEART: Stablecoins aim for a steady value by linking it to another asset, usually the dollar, and backing the coin with a reserve of assets. Algorithmic or smart stablecoins instead have software create coins as demand rises, relying on code rather than a full reserve, and some have collapsed in runs once confidence broke. Two current points. Whether an ordinary holder can redeem a stablecoin with its issuer at face value depends on the coin and the rules it falls under, and many holders simply sell on an exchange. And regulation is arriving. The European Union now licenses stablecoin issuers, and a United States stablecoin law signed in twenty twenty five, requiring one for one reserves, takes effect by early twenty twenty seven. The asset backed token, pegged to something like gold, is a near relative we meet in the next module.

LEWIS: Meme coins are launched mostly for fun, riding an internet joke. They can surge on social media, letting early buyers sell at a quick profit, and fade just as fast.

HEART: A central bank digital currency, or C B D C, is government money in purely digital form, a banknote without the paper, so unlike private crypto it is a claim on the central bank itself. As of late twenty twenty six, a handful of smaller economies have fully launched one, and Russia began a nationwide rollout of its own in September. Many other central banks, including China's and the euro area's, are piloting or preparing one, and the United States has adopted a policy against issuing one.

LEWIS: Now tokens. Tokenization means tracking an asset's full ownership history on a distributed ledger, which cuts the work of proving ownership. Think of the paperwork it takes to prove who owns a used car. A nonfungible token, or N F T, is a digital asset tied to a certificate of authenticity. Fungible means interchangeable, like one ten dollar bill for another. Each N F T represents a distinct item, such as a digital artwork or a specific concert seat.

HEART: A security token is a digital form of ownership rights in a security, such as a share or a bond, and a blockchain can handle its custody, settlement, bookkeeping and post trade processing on one shared ledger. Initial coin offerings are usually grouped with security tokens, because selling tokens to raise money is often treated as a securities offering, whatever the tokens are called. In an I C O, a young venture sells newly created tokens for cash or cryptocurrency, usually redeemable later for its products or services. It is a cheaper, faster alternative to a regulated initial public offering, but the tokens rarely carry voting rights, oversight has been light, and fraud has been common.

LEWIS: Utility tokens pay for network services, such as transaction fees, and reward holders only through network activity, unlike security tokens, which may pay something like a dividend. Governance tokens, found on permissionless networks, give holders votes on how the network runs, such as a fee change or a software upgrade.

HEART: Here is the trap: match function to name. Voting means governance. Paying for network services means utility. Ownership rights in a security means a security token. One of a kind means nonfungible.

LEWIS: The takeaway. A distributed ledger swaps one trusted bookkeeper for a shared, tamper evident history, kept in agreement by proof of work or proof of stake. Permissionless networks are open to all, permissioned ones restrict access, and each asset type is best remembered by what it does.

## Module 82.2, Digital Asset Characteristics

HEART: Digital assets have moved toward the mainstream. The number of cryptocurrencies has exploded, institutions have taken interest, and exchanges and custodians have built sturdier infrastructure.

LEWIS: Four features set them apart. First, the source of value. A bond pays coupons and a stock is a claim on earnings, but most digital assets have no underlying assets and pay no interest or dividends. With no expected earnings, they have no fundamental value in the usual sense, so a discounted cash flow model has nothing to discount. Prices rest on expected growth in value, driven by scarcity and by future usefulness for transferring value.

HEART: Second, validation. Traditional instruments are tracked on private ledgers kept by central intermediaries, such as a securities depository and the brokers around it. Digital assets typically sit on decentralized ledgers.

LEWIS: Third, the medium of exchange. Traditional assets are priced in fiat currencies, government money such as dollars and euros. Digital assets offer an alternative, mostly for online transactions, but mainstream finance has been slow to accept them, because transaction costs can be high, they are not legal tender in most countries, and several countries restrict or ban them. Central bank digital currencies offer a different route, ordinary government money itself in tokenized form.

HEART: Fourth, regulation. Traditional instruments operate under clear, established rules, while digital asset standards are still being built. In the United States, some coins are treated as commodities, and in twenty twenty six the securities and commodities regulators jointly classed Bitcoin and Ether that way, while other authorities have treated digital currencies as nonfinancial assets. Many trading venues have been lightly regulated or not regulated at all, so rules against manipulation may not clearly apply. As of twenty twenty six this is shifting, with a European Union licensing regime for crypto service providers and a United States stablecoin law, but standards remain uneven from country to country.

LEWIS: Now the ways in. Direct ownership on a blockchain requires a cryptocurrency wallet, which holds the digital keys that give access to the assets. Think of the only key to a safe deposit box, at a bank with no customer service desk. Lose it, and the assets are effectively gone.

HEART: Trading happens on two kinds of exchange. A centralized exchange is run by a company and offers a platform with price and volume information. It is by far the most popular kind, even though a central operator sits awkwardly with Bitcoin's decentralized idea. Trades run on the company's private servers, so a hack can halt trading and leak user information, even wallet keys. Some are regulated as financial exchanges, depending on the jurisdiction, and when large ones have failed, customers have found their assets frozen.

LEWIS: A decentralized exchange has no central authority and runs on many computers, so attacking one machine does not stop it, which makes attacks much harder. The flip side is that with no one in charge, regulations are hard to enforce. On either kind, thin oversight invites fraud and manipulation, such as a pump and dump, where promoters hype a little known coin on social media, then sell to the buyers they attracted.

HEART: Direct investment happens when the transaction is written to the blockchain itself, such as buying coins on an exchange, trading an N F T or joining an initial coin offering, and most crypto exchanges trade around the clock. The risks are a long list: scam offerings, pump and dump schemes, manipulation, theft, phishing for wallet credentials, and lost keys that leave holdings worthless. And many small coins are held mostly by a few large holders, nicknamed whales, who can move the price on their own.

LEWIS: Indirect investment avoids handling keys, through five main routes. Coin trusts sell shares in a trust holding a cryptocurrency. They trade over the counter, much like a closed end fund, so the shares can trade above or below the value of the coins inside. You get more transparency and no keys to guard, but the fees at some trusts are steep. Cryptocurrency futures are listed on regulated exchanges, usually cash settled and leveraged, and they tend to be more volatile and less liquid than traditional futures.

HEART: Exchange traded products aim to track digital asset returns. Some get exposure through futures or cash, and since twenty twenty four the United States has also had spot products that hold Bitcoin and Ether directly. Cryptocurrency stocks, such as public crypto exchanges, payment firms, companies that accept, hold or mine crypto, and firms serving blockchain networks, add the company's own risks and the stock market's moves. And cryptocurrency hedge funds run discretionary long, long short, quantitative and multistrategy approaches, and some mine Bitcoin for extra return.

LEWIS: There is also a route in the opposite direction: digital ownership of assets that are not digital. An asset backed token represents a physical or financial asset, such as a warehouse of copper, a building, or a portfolio of Treasury bills, and is collateralized by it. Fractional ownership can make an expensive, hard to sell asset more liquid, the shared ownership history improves transparency and cuts costs, and regulators generally classify these tokens as securities.

HEART: They are often issued on Ethereum through smart contracts, which leads to decentralized applications that run without a central system. Decentralized finance grew from that, aiming to offer exchange, a store of value, tokenized assets and permanent ownership histories through open source applications. So far, most of those applications focus on speculation.

LEWIS: Now risk and return. Cryptocurrency values depend almost entirely on price appreciation, with limited supply a key driver. Bitcoin's protocol caps it at twenty one million units, which is why some call it digital gold. Bitcoin has produced high returns with very high volatility compared with traditional assets, including several deep crashes, and other cryptocurrencies have been similarly volatile. Fraud, crime, uneven regulation and outright bans in some countries keep many investors wary.

HEART: The diversification case rests on correlation. Historically, cryptocurrency returns have shown low correlation with traditional assets, so a small position could diversify a portfolio. But correlations can rise in extreme market stress, just when diversification matters most, and in recent years crypto has at times moved more closely with stocks, especially in sell offs. Prices respond to forces specific to the asset class: adoption, risk appetite, technological innovation, network effects, speculation and regulation.

LEWIS: Here is the trap. A question may suggest a digital asset is low risk because its ledger is secure and tamper evident. The security of the ledger says nothing about price volatility, custody, fraud or fundamental value. A perfectly secure ledger can track a very risky investment.

HEART: The takeaway. Most digital assets have no cash flows, so their prices run on scarcity, adoption and sentiment. You can hold them directly with your own keys or indirectly through trusts, futures, exchange traded products, stocks and funds, and their low historical correlation comes with extreme volatility and with risks the ledger does not remove.

## Recall check

LEWIS Q: Which land investment lets the owner wait for better prices before selling its output, timberland or farmland?

HEART: Answer. Timberland. Here is why. Crops ripen on a schedule and many spoil, so the farmer must harvest within a short window whatever the price. Trees keep growing while they stand, so the timber owner can delay the harvest when prices are weak.

HEART Q: A commodity is plentiful and has almost no convenience yield. Is its futures curve more likely in contango or backwardation, and how does that affect a long only investor?

LEWIS: Answer. Contango, which lowers the long only investor's return. Here is why. With little convenience yield, the costs of carrying the good dominate, so futures sit above spot. The investor keeps buying contracts above spot that drift down toward it as they expire, so each roll costs a little.

LEWIS Q: A long futures investor rolls contracts in a market that stays in backwardation, and the spot price never moves. Does the roll add to or subtract from the return?

HEART: Answer. It adds. Here is why. In backwardation each new contract is bought below spot, and as expiration nears its price drifts up toward the unchanged spot price. Roll yield depends on the shape of the curve, not on the direction of the spot price.

HEART Q: In merger arbitrage, what does the manager buy and short, and why is the strategy not risk free?

LEWIS: Answer. The manager buys the target and shorts the acquirer, and the deal can fall apart or its terms can change. Here is why. The target trades below the offer because the deal might fail. If the deal completes, the manager earns that small gap, but if it collapses the target can drop sharply, so the payoff is many small gains and an occasional large loss.

LEWIS Q: Why do hedge funds impose lockups, notice periods and liquidity gates?

HEART: Answer. To protect the fund and its remaining investors from forced, costly selling. Here is why. Many positions cannot be sold quickly without losing value, and redemptions bunch up after poor results. These tools give the manager time to sell in an orderly way and cap how much can leave at once, so those who stay do not pay for those who leave.

HEART Q: A large institution is choosing between a commingled hedge fund and a separately managed account with the same manager. What are the account's drawbacks?

LEWIS: Answer. Weaker alignment, more oversight work, and possibly a thinner version of the strategy. Here is why. The manager has no personal money in the account, unlike a fund where the manager invests alongside clients. The account also needs more operational oversight, and the lower negotiated fee may come with access to only the manager's most liquid trades.

LEWIS Q: A hedge fund's return came mostly from the stock market rising. Which source of return was that, and why does it matter for fees?

HEART: Answer. Market beta, which means the investor paid hedge fund fees for something cheap. Here is why. Market beta is exposure to the broad market, which an index fund delivers for very little. Only alpha, the value the manager adds through security selection, justifies premium fees.

HEART Q: A hedge fund joins an index and its earlier returns are added to the index history. What is that called, and which way does it push reported returns?

LEWIS: Answer. Backfill bias, and it pushes reported returns up. Here is why. Funds with strong histories are the ones most eager to join, so the added past returns are better than what the average fund earned. Survivorship bias pushes the same way, because failing funds drop out of the data.

LEWIS Q: In proof of work and in proof of stake, what would an attacker need to control to take over the ledger?

HEART: Answer. A majority of the computing power in proof of work, and a majority of the staked coins in proof of stake. Here is why. In proof of work, blocks are won with computing power, so control means outcomputing everyone else at enormous cost. In proof of stake, the right to validate comes from coins pledged as collateral, so control means owning most of the stake, and cheating puts that collateral at risk. Either way, the network stays safe while honest participants hold the majority, which in proof of stake means honest validators holding most of the stake.

HEART Q: A group of banks wants a shared ledger to settle trades among themselves, with a regulator able to view the history but not add entries. Permissioned or permissionless?

LEWIS: Answer. Permissioned. Here is why. A permissioned network sets each participant's level of access, so the banks can add transactions while the regulator only views, and its tighter controls make it more cost effective. A permissionless network lets anyone join and perform any function.

LEWIS Q: Why can you not value Bitcoin with a discounted cash flow model?

HEART: Answer. Because it produces no cash flows to discount. Here is why. A discounted cash flow model values the interest, dividends or earnings an asset will pay. Bitcoin pays none, so it has no fundamental value in that sense, and its price rests on scarcity, adoption and expectations about future demand.

HEART Q: A token lets its holders vote on changes to the network's fee schedule. What kind of token is it, and how does it differ from a utility token?

LEWIS: Answer. A governance token. Here is why. Governance tokens give holders voting rights over how a network operates. A utility token is what you spend on the network's services, such as transaction fees, and it rewards holders only through network activity.

## Key takeaways

HEART: First, a few memory hooks.

LEWIS: For land: trees can wait, crops cannot. Timber stores itself on the stump.

HEART: For the futures price: spot, plus the cost of carrying the good, minus the convenience of having it. When the convenience yield outweighs the carrying costs, futures sit below spot.

LEWIS: For the curve, from a long only investor's seat: contango costs, backwardation boosts. A futures return has three pieces, spot, roll and collateral, and roll comes from the curve's shape, not the price trend.

HEART: For the four hedge fund families: stocks, events, gaps and the big picture.

LEWIS: For liquidity terms, picture a door. The lockup locks it, the notice period is the wait at it, and the gate narrows it.

HEART: For return sources: market beta is cheap, strategy beta is a style, and alpha is skill. Pay premium fees only for alpha.

LEWIS: For index biases: the losers go missing and the winners bring their history. Survivorship and backfill both push reported returns up.

HEART: For consensus: work burns electricity, stake puts collateral at risk. Miners for work, validators for stake.

LEWIS: For access: permissionless is the public park, permissioned is the members' club.

HEART: For tokens: governance votes, utility pays for service, a security token carries ownership rights, and an N F T is one of a kind. Cryptocurrencies run on their own chains, and tokens ride on someone else's.

LEWIS: And one rule for the digital reading: a secure ledger is not a safe investment.

HEART: Now, how you would say the big ideas to a senior leader.

LEWIS: On commodities: they can diversify our portfolio and help when inflation runs hot, but held through futures, the shape of the futures curve can add or subtract return even when prices go nowhere. The jargon is contango, backwardation and roll yield.

HEART: On farmland and timberland: they earn from what they grow and from the land itself, but they are hard to sell and exposed to weather and disaster. The jargon is illiquidity and natural resource risk.

LEWIS: On hedge funds: before paying premium fees, ask how much of the return we could have bought cheaply from an index, and pay up only for genuine skill. The jargon is market beta, strategy beta and alpha.

HEART: On hedge fund track records: industry averages flatter the industry, because failed funds vanish from the data and new funds arrive with their best years attached. The jargon is survivorship bias and backfill bias.

LEWIS: On liquidity: money in these funds can be locked up and withdrawals limited, so commit only cash we will not need on short notice. The jargon is lockup period, notice period and liquidity gate.

HEART: And on digital assets: a blockchain is a shared, tamper evident history that no single party controls, and it can make transfers faster and more transparent. But most coins pay no cash flows, so their prices move on adoption and sentiment, and the rules still differ widely by country. The jargon is distributed ledger technology, consensus mechanism and fundamental value.
