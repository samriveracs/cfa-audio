LEWIS: Reading 84 finishes portfolio risk and return with the capital market line and the market portfolio, the split between risk you can diversify away and risk you cannot, and beta as the measure of the risk that remains. It then covers the capital asset pricing model and its assumptions, the security market line and how to spot a mispriced stock with it, and four ways to grade a manager: the Sharpe ratio, the Treynor measure, M squared and Jensen's alpha.

## Module 84.1, Systematic Risk and Beta

HEART: The last reading ended with a ruler pinned at the risk free rate, swung upward until it just grazed the efficient frontier. Because the risk free asset brings no volatility and no correlation of its own, every mix of it with a risky portfolio lies on a straight line, and the steepest such line picked out the optimal risky portfolio.

LEWIS: This reading adds one assumption, homogeneous expectations. Every investor works from the same forecasts of expected returns, standard deviations and correlations for every risky asset. Same inputs, same efficient frontier, same grazing point. So every investor holds the very same risky portfolio, just in different amounts. Without that assumption, investors with different forecasts would each find a different grazing point and a different capital allocation line.

HEART: And that forces something remarkable. Picture a town where every household fills its basket of risky assets from the same recipe card, some buying a big basket and some a small one. Every risky asset in town has to be owned by somebody, so all the baskets added together are the whole market. If every basket follows one recipe, that recipe must match the whole market, each asset held in proportion to its total market value. The shared risky portfolio has to be the market portfolio, the portfolio of all risky assets.

LEWIS: In theory, the market portfolio holds every risky asset there is. In practice, nobody can buy all of that, so investors hold a broad index of risky assets as a stand in, called a proxy for the market.

HEART: Under homogeneous expectations, the best capital allocation line for everyone is the one through the market portfolio, and it gets its own name, the capital market line, or CML. It starts at the risk free rate, where risk is zero, and runs up through the market portfolio. Along it, expected return rises in a straight line with total risk, measured by standard deviation.

LEWIS: Its slope is worth knowing in words. The market's expected return minus the risk free rate is called the market risk premium, the extra reward for holding the market instead of riskless bills. The slope of the capital market line is that market risk premium divided by the market's standard deviation. In plain words, the reward the market offers for each unit of total risk.

HEART: Which gives a neat way to hear any point on the line. Every portfolio on it earns the risk free rate, plus a share of the market risk premium equal to its share of the market's risk. Take on half the market's volatility, and you earn the risk free rate plus half the market risk premium.

LEWIS: Points to the left of the market portfolio are lending portfolios. Part of your money sits in the risk free asset, which amounts to lending at the risk free rate. Points to the right are borrowing portfolios, also called leveraged portfolios. You borrow at the risk free rate, the way an investor borrows through a margin account, and put more than all of your own money into the market portfolio. That stretch of the line exists only because the model assumes investors can borrow, as well as lend, at the risk free rate.

HEART: In real life most investors borrow at a higher rate than the government pays, so beyond the market portfolio the line bends and flattens, and leverage buys a little less than the model promises.

LEWIS: Now the two strategies this sets up. An investor who trusts that market prices are informationally efficient, meaning they already reflect what is known, sees no point in hunting for a better risky mix. She buys a broad index fund as her proxy for the market, keeps the rest in Treasury bills, and decides only the split between them. That is a passive investment strategy.

HEART: Now picture an investor who trusts her own research over the market's prices. She tilts toward the stocks she judges undervalued, holding more of each than its share of the market, and away from the ones she judges overvalued, holding less. That tilting is active portfolio management. Her risky portfolio is no longer the market, so she ends up on a capital allocation line of her own.

LEWIS: Now the second big idea, and the one the rest of this reading hangs on. Why is the market portfolio the right yardstick for risk? Because of what diversification can and cannot remove.

HEART: Think about everything that could hurt a regional chain of coffee shops. A kitchen fire at one location, a falling out with its main bean supplier, a rival opening across the street. Those are its own problems. Now think of a recession, when people everywhere cut back, or a jump in interest rates that raises every company's borrowing costs. Those hit the coffee chain and almost every other business at the same time.

LEWIS: The first kind is unsystematic risk, also called nonsystematic, unique, diversifiable or firm specific risk. The second kind is systematic risk, also called market risk or nondiversifiable risk. It comes from economy wide forces such as GDP growth, inflation and changes in interest rates. A stock's total risk, measured by its standard deviation, is made of both.

HEART: Hold many stocks across different industries and the firm specific surprises tend to cancel. One company's fire is offset by another company's lucky contract. The last reading showed the result. Portfolio risk falls quickly as you add holdings, then levels off at a floor. That floor is systematic risk. It does not cancel, because the recession reaches every holding at once.

LEWIS: And you do not need every security in the market to get there. Studies differ on the exact count, but a few dozen stocks or even fewer, spread across industries, remove most of the unique risk. The market portfolio goes all the way, holding every risky asset, so it carries no unique risk at all. Its risk is entirely systematic.

HEART: The same split applies to a single stock. Some companies are highly sensitive to the economy. A homebuilder sells many more houses in a boom and far fewer in a slump, so a large part of its risk is systematic. Others barely notice the business cycle. People buy toothpaste in good years and bad, so a toothpaste maker carries little systematic risk.

LEWIS: A lender's loan book shows the same split. One borrower failing for reasons of its own is unique risk, and spreading loans across many borrowers dilutes it. A recession touches nearly every borrower at once, and no amount of spreading removes that.

HEART: Now the key question. Why should an investor expect no extra return for bearing unsystematic risk?

LEWIS: Because it can be removed for free. Diversification costs almost nothing, especially through a low cost index fund, and the model assumes it costs nothing at all. Picture an employer setting pay. It pays extra for work on an offshore oil rig, because that hardship comes with the job and anyone doing it must bear it. It will not pay you extra for a two hour commute you chose, because that burden was yours to avoid. Markets work the same way. They pay for the burden nobody can avoid, systematic risk, and nothing for the burden you could have shed by diversifying.

HEART: Here is the mechanism. Suppose a stock offered a high expected return just because it carried a lot of unique risk. Diversified investors would see a bargain. Inside their broad portfolios, that unique risk mostly cancels, so they would take on little extra risk while collecting the extra return. They would buy, the price would rise, and the expected return would fall until the extra reward was gone.

LEWIS: So prices are set by investors who diversify, and all they care about is how much a stock adds to the risk of a broad portfolio. That added risk is its systematic risk. In equilibrium, then, the return investors require is set by systematic risk alone, and the unique part of a stock's risk adds nothing to it.

HEART: That has a striking consequence. The stock with the highest standard deviation does not necessarily have the highest expected return. Picture a small company whose whole future rides on one large patent lawsuit. Win, and the stock soars. Lose, and it collapses. Enormous total risk. But a courtroom verdict has almost nothing to do with the economy, so nearly all of that risk is unique.

LEWIS: Now picture a large trucking company. Its stock is much calmer overall, but its shipments rise and fall with the economy, so most of its risk is systematic. The calm trucking company can therefore offer the higher expected return. That is the trap to hold onto. High total risk does not mean a high required return. Only the systematic part is paid.

HEART: To measure systematic risk, we need a model of what drives returns. A return generating model builds a security's expected return from a few underlying drivers, called factors. Its sensitivity to each factor is called a factor sensitivity or factor loading, in effect a beta for that one factor.

LEWIS: Factors come in three families. Macroeconomic factors are economy wide forces, such as the growth of the economy, unexpected inflation or shifts in interest rates. Fundamental factors are traits of the company itself, such as its size, its earnings growth, its debt load or its spending on research. Statistical factors are patterns pulled out of past returns by number crunching, often with no economic story behind them, and they deserve suspicion. Test the same data enough times and something will appear to work by luck alone, which is called data mining, so such a pattern may hold only for the stretch of history that was searched.

HEART: The general multifactor model is simple in words. A security's expected return above the risk free rate equals the sum, across all the factors, of its sensitivity to each factor times that factor's expected value. Often the first factor is the expected return on the market above the risk free rate.

LEWIS: The best known example is the Fama and French model, with three factors: the market's excess return, firm size, and the ratio of book value to market value. Carhart added a fourth factor, momentum, based on how a stock performed in the recent past. The four together account for much of why some United States stocks have earned more than others, at least over the years the model was tested on.

HEART: The simplest case keeps just one factor, the market. That is a single factor model, also called a single index model. In words, a security's expected return above the risk free rate equals its beta times the market's expected return above the risk free rate. Beta is the only sensitivity that matters.

LEWIS: A simplified working version, the market model, is what analysts fit to data to measure beta. It says a stock's return in any period equals an intercept, plus beta times the market's return in that period, plus an error term. The intercept and beta both come from historical data.

HEART: The error term is the interesting part. It is the abnormal return, the piece of the stock's return that the market's move does not explain. Say the market rises in a given month, and given the stock's beta the model expected it to rise by a certain amount. Then the company announces a surprise contract and the stock jumps by more. That extra jump is the abnormal return, the firm specific surprise for that period.

LEWIS: One technical detail. The market model is written in plain returns, while the single index model is written in returns above the risk free rate. For the two to agree, the intercept has to equal the risk free rate times one minus beta.

HEART: Now beta itself, by picture. Imagine a stock that tends to rise further than the market in good months and fall harder in bad ones. A stock that tends to move twice as much as the market, in the same direction, has a beta near two. One that tends to move half as much has a beta near one half. Beta is the stock's sensitivity to the market.

LEWIS: That makes the benchmark values easy. The market's own beta is exactly one, since the market moves one for one with itself. The average beta across all stocks, weighting each by its market value, is also one. A beta above one means the stock tends to amplify market moves. Below one, it tends to dampen them. A beta of zero means the stock's returns are uncorrelated with the market's, which is why the risk free asset has a beta of zero. And a beta can even be negative, for an asset that tends to rise when the market falls.

HEART: Formally, beta is a standardized version of covariance. It equals the stock's covariance with the market, divided by the market's variance. Dividing by the market's variance scales things so that the market itself lands at exactly one.

LEWIS: A second form shows what beta is really made of. Beta equals the stock's correlation with the market, times the ratio of the stock's standard deviation to the market's. So beta blends two things. How closely the stock tracks the market, and how big its swings are compared with the market's swings.

HEART: That second form explains a common surprise. A stock can be very volatile and still have a low beta, if its swings have little to do with the market. Many gold mining stocks swing hard with the price of gold yet move only loosely with the stock market, so their betas are usually far lower than their swings alone would suggest. Big total risk, with only a modest share of it systematic.

LEWIS: Here are the traps. First, beta is not total risk. Second, when you start from covariance, divide by the market's variance, the squared figure, not by the market's standard deviation. Third, diversification does not drag a portfolio's beta toward zero. A portfolio's beta is simply the weighted average of the betas of its holdings, so a broad portfolio of high beta stocks still has a high beta. Diversification strips out the unique risk and leaves the beta behind.

HEART: Now how beta is measured in practice. Take a few years of monthly data. For each month, plot one dot, with the market's return above the risk free rate on the horizontal axis and the stock's return above the risk free rate on the vertical axis. Then draw the line of best fit through the cloud, using least squares regression, which picks the line that makes the sum of the squared vertical distances from the dots as small as possible.

LEWIS: The slope of that line is the estimated beta. If both axes are drawn to the same scale and the line is steeper than a forty five degree diagonal, the slope is above one, and the stock responds more strongly to systematic factors than the market does. That fitted line has a name, the security characteristic line, and its slope works out to exactly the covariance over the market's variance from before.

HEART: And because beta comes from a regression on past data, it is measured with error, not a fixed fact. Choose a different index, a different stretch of years, or weekly instead of monthly returns, and the figure can change.

LEWIS: The takeaway. Under homogeneous expectations, everyone holds the market portfolio, and the line through it is the capital market line. Diversification removes unique risk for free, so only systematic risk is rewarded. Beta measures that systematic risk as the stock's sensitivity to the market, its covariance with the market over the market's variance.

## Module 84.2, The CAPM and the SML

HEART: The last module gave us two facts. Only systematic risk is rewarded, and beta measures systematic risk. Put them together and you get a pricing rule. An asset's required return should depend on its beta and nothing else. That rule is the capital asset pricing model, the CAPM.

LEWIS: In words, the CAPM says the expected return on any asset equals the risk free rate, plus the asset's beta times the market risk premium. And the market risk premium, again, is the market's expected return minus the risk free rate.

HEART: Think of a taxi fare. There is a flat charge the moment you get in, then a rate per mile, times the number of miles you ride. The risk free rate is the flat charge, the return even a riskless investment pays. The market risk premium is the rate per mile, the price the market sets on one unit of systematic risk. And beta is the number of miles, how much systematic risk this particular asset carries.

LEWIS: So the directions are easy. A stock with a beta of one gets exactly the market's expected return. A beta above one means a required return above the market's. A beta below one, below the market's. A beta of zero earns just the risk free rate. And a negative beta implies an expected return below the risk free rate, because an asset that tends to rise when the market falls works like insurance, and investors accept a lower return for that protection.

HEART: In equilibrium, an asset's expected return and the return investors require from it are one and the same, which is why the CAPM figure doubles as a required return.

LEWIS: Where does beta come from inside the model? Since covariance with the market is the only risk that is priced, you could draw the security market line with that covariance itself along the horizontal axis. Rescale the covariance by dividing it by the market's variance, and the axis becomes beta, which gives the familiar version of the line.

HEART: The CAPM rests on a set of simplifying assumptions, and the exam likes them. First, investors are risk averse. They need a higher expected return to accept more risk. Second, they are rational and maximize their expected utility, each choosing the mix of risk and return that suits their own preferences.

LEWIS: Third, markets are frictionless, with no taxes, no transaction costs and nothing else standing in the way of trading. Fourth, everyone plans over the same single period. Fifth, homogeneous expectations, the same forecasts of expected returns, standard deviations and correlations for every asset.

HEART: Sixth, assets are infinitely divisible, so you can buy any slice of any investment, however small. Seventh, markets are competitive. Every investor is a price taker, and nobody's trades are big enough to move prices.

LEWIS: And notice what is not on the list. The model does not assume everyone has the same risk aversion. Investors share one view of the world, and each still picks a different mix of the market portfolio and the risk free asset.

HEART: Now graph the CAPM, and you get the security market line, or SML. Beta runs along the horizontal axis and expected return up the vertical axis. The line starts at the risk free rate, where beta is zero, and passes through the market portfolio at a beta of one. Its slope is the market risk premium.

LEWIS: Two shifts to know. If the risk free rate rises and the premium stays the same, the whole line moves up in parallel. If investors grow more risk averse and demand a larger market risk premium, the line gets steeper, so high beta assets need even more return than before.

HEART: Now the most tested comparison in this reading, the capital market line against the security market line. They look alike. Both are straight, both start at the risk free rate, and both pass through the market portfolio. But they measure risk differently.

LEWIS: First, the horizontal axis. The capital market line measures risk as total risk, standard deviation. The security market line measures only systematic risk, beta. Second, what plots on each. Only efficient portfolios plot on the capital market line, meaning the market portfolio combined with lending or borrowing at the risk free rate. Every properly priced security and portfolio plots on the security market line, diversified or not, including a single stock. Third, the slopes. The capital market line's slope is the market's reward per unit of total risk, its Sharpe ratio. The security market line's slope is the market risk premium itself.

HEART: Here is a picture that ties the two together. Take a handful of stocks and portfolios that all have a beta of exactly one, but very different amounts of unique risk. On the capital market line chart, they all sit at the same height, the market's expected return, but spread out to the right, because the ones with more unique risk have bigger standard deviations. Only the market portfolio sits on the line. The rest sit below it, inside the efficient frontier, carrying risk the market does not pay for.

LEWIS: On the security market line chart, all of them collapse into a single dot, at a beta of one and the market's expected return. The security market line does not see unique risk at all, because the market does not pay for it.

HEART: Which gives the trap. A fairly priced single stock sits on the security market line, but below the capital market line. Its expected return is right for its beta. It just carries unique risk that earns nothing, so on a total risk chart it looks like a poor deal. And low beta is not the same as safe. A stock can barely move with the market and still swing wildly on its own news. Its low beta tells you only that it adds little risk once it sits inside a broad portfolio.

LEWIS: Now the applications. The first is the required return itself. Plug in an asset's beta and the CAPM gives the return investors should demand for holding it. Companies treat it as a cost of equity, a hurdle that projects funded by shareholders must clear. A bank's capital planners might set a CAPM style cost of equity as the minimum return a business line has to earn on the capital it ties up.

HEART: The second application is spotting mispriced securities. The CAPM tells you what a stock should earn given its beta. Your own research gives you a forecast of what it will actually earn, from where you think its price will be in a year plus the dividends it should pay along the way, all compared with today's price. Then you plot your forecast against the line.

LEWIS: If your forecast return plots above the security market line, the stock offers more return than its systematic risk requires. That means its price today is too low. It is undervalued, and you buy it. If your forecast plots below the line, the stock offers too little return for its risk, so its price is too high. It is overvalued, and you sell it or sell it short. If it plots right on the line, it is properly priced, and you can buy it, sell it or ignore it.

HEART: Picture two rental houses on the same street, alike in every way that matters for risk. One is expected to bring in much more rent relative to its asking price. That higher expected return is telling you the asking price is low. For a given stream of future cash, expected return and price move in opposite directions.

LEWIS: And here is the trap that catches many candidates. Above the line sounds like too high, and too high sounds like overpriced. But the vertical axis is expected return, not price. A high return for the risk means a low price. Above the line is undervalued. Below the line is overvalued. The graph and the formula always give the same verdict.

HEART: And a stock off the line should not stay there. Investors who spot one above the line buy it, the price rises, and the expected return from that higher price falls back to the line. In equilibrium, every asset sits on the security market line.

LEWIS: The third application is grading performance, and it brings four measures. Beating the benchmark is not enough on its own. A manager who loads up on extra risk, above all extra beta, should outrun the benchmark in the long run just by doing that, so performance evaluation has to weigh return against risk. Breaking down where an active portfolio's return differed from a passive benchmark, and why, is called attribution analysis, and it is one part of that evaluation. These risk adjusted measures are the usual way to compare an actively managed fund with a passive one.

HEART: The first measure is the Sharpe ratio. It is the portfolio's return above the risk free rate, divided by its standard deviation. In plain words, excess return per unit of total risk. Higher is better. It rewards return earned with as little total volatility as possible, and it charges for every kind of risk, unique risk included.

LEWIS: On a chart of expected return against standard deviation, a portfolio's Sharpe ratio is the slope of its own capital allocation line, the line from the risk free rate through that portfolio. So you compare it with the slope of the capital market line, which is the market's Sharpe ratio. A portfolio that sits above the capital market line has a higher Sharpe ratio than anything on the line.

HEART: Two details. A Sharpe ratio by itself tells you little. It earns its keep only when set beside another portfolio's. And it can be worked out before the fact, from expected returns and expected risk, which is called ex ante, or after the fact, from average past returns and their sample standard deviation, which is called ex post.

LEWIS: The second measure is M squared. It carries the same information as the Sharpe ratio, but as a percentage return instead of a slope. Picture a volume knob. Turn the portfolio up or down until it is exactly as loud as the market, meaning it has the same standard deviation as the market. If the portfolio is calmer than the market, you turn it up by borrowing at the risk free rate and investing more in it. If it is wilder, you turn it down by putting part of the money in the risk free asset. The return of that risk matched portfolio is M squared.

HEART: Then you compare it with the market's return. The difference, M squared minus the market's return, is called M squared alpha. A positive M squared alpha means the manager beat the market after adjusting for total risk, and that happens exactly when the portfolio's Sharpe ratio is above the market's. Because M squared is built from the Sharpe ratio, the two always rank portfolios in the same order.

LEWIS: Keep the two names straight. M squared is a return in its own right, the return of the risk matched portfolio. M squared alpha is the gap between that return and the market's. Mixing them up is the trap.

HEART: The other two measures look only at systematic risk. The Treynor measure is the portfolio's return above the risk free rate, divided by its beta. In plain words, excess return per unit of systematic risk. It rewards return earned per unit of beta, and it ignores unique risk completely.

LEWIS: On the security market line chart, the Treynor measure is the slope of a line from the risk free rate to the portfolio. The market's own Treynor measure is the slope of the security market line, the market risk premium. A portfolio that plots above the security market line has a steeper line from the risk free rate than the security market line itself, so its Treynor measure beats the market's and that of every fairly priced asset.

HEART: The fourth is Jensen's alpha. It is the portfolio's actual return minus the return the CAPM says it should have earned for its beta. In plain words, how far the portfolio sits above or below the security market line, in percentage points. A positive alpha means it beat the line. It is also the intercept you get when you regress the portfolio's excess returns on the market's excess returns, the same regression that produces beta. That holds for the excess return version only. In the plain return market model from the last module, even a fairly priced asset can show an intercept other than zero, so that intercept is not Jensen's alpha.

LEWIS: Notice the pairings. The Sharpe ratio and the Treynor measure are slopes, reward per unit of risk. M squared and Jensen's alpha are stated as percentage returns. The Sharpe ratio and M squared count total risk, while the Treynor measure and Jensen's alpha count only beta. So Sharpe is to M squared what Treynor is to Jensen's alpha, at least in form. One difference. M squared always ranks portfolios the same way as the Sharpe ratio, but Jensen's alpha is not divided by beta, so it can rank two portfolios differently from the Treynor measure.

HEART: Which measure do you reach for? It depends on whether the portfolio carries unique risk that matters to the investor. If a single manager runs the money, or the portfolio is all the investor holds, its total risk is what the investor actually bears. So judge it on total risk, with the Sharpe ratio or M squared.

LEWIS: If a fund spreads its money across several managers, so that the overall fund is well diversified, each manager's unique risk washes out in the whole. Then the risk that matters is systematic, and the right measures are the Treynor measure and Jensen's alpha. The Sharpe ratio still works for any portfolio, concentrated or diversified, because it counts all the risk there is.

HEART: Picture a manager who holds a handful of stocks with modest betas but a great deal of unique risk, and earns a solid return. Judged on beta, she looks excellent, with a positive Jensen's alpha and a strong Treynor measure. Judged on total risk, the same portfolio can show a poor Sharpe ratio, because all that unique risk inflates its standard deviation.

LEWIS: If she is one manager among many in a diversified fund, her unique risk washes out, and the beta view is fair. If she runs a client's entire savings, the client bears every bit of that unique risk, and the total risk view is the honest one.

HEART: One final caution. Every one of these numbers rests on inputs measured with error. The market's expected return, and with it the premium, cannot be observed, and its long run historical average is only a rough guide. Betas come from noisy data. And the CAPM itself is a simplification, one factor over one period. The multifactor models from the last module are one response, adding drivers beyond the market.

LEWIS: The takeaway. The CAPM says required return equals the risk free rate plus beta times the market risk premium, and its graph is the security market line. Above the line is undervalued, below it is overvalued. Sharpe and M squared judge total risk, for a portfolio that stands alone. Treynor and Jensen's alpha judge beta, for one piece of a diversified whole.

## Recall check

LEWIS Q: Under homogeneous expectations, why must the risky portfolio that every investor holds be the market portfolio?

HEART: Answer. Because when everyone holds the same risky mix, that mix has to add up to everything available. Here is why. Same forecasts give everyone the same efficient frontier and the same tangency portfolio. Every risky asset must be owned by someone, so when every investor holds the identical mix, all holdings together equal the whole market. The only mix that fits is each asset in proportion to its market value, which is the market portfolio.

HEART Q: What is the slope of the capital market line, and what does it tell you?

LEWIS: Answer. The market risk premium divided by the market's standard deviation, which is the market's Sharpe ratio. Here is why. The line runs from the risk free rate to the market portfolio. Its rise is the market's expected return above the risk free rate, and its run is the market's standard deviation. So the slope is the extra return the market offers for each unit of total risk, and every portfolio on the line earns that same reward per unit of risk.

LEWIS Q: Why does the market pay no premium for unsystematic risk?

HEART: Answer. Because it can be diversified away at essentially no cost. Here is why. If a stock offered extra return for its unique risk, diversified investors would buy it, since that risk mostly cancels in their portfolios. Their buying would push the price up and the expected return down until the extra reward disappeared. Prices are set by diversified investors, who only care about the risk a stock adds to a broad portfolio, which is its systematic risk.

HEART Q: Stock A is extremely volatile with a beta of one half. Stock B is far calmer with a beta of one and a half. Under the CAPM, which has the higher required return?

LEWIS: Answer. Stock B, the calmer one. Here is why. The CAPM prices only systematic risk, measured by beta, and Stock B has three times Stock A's beta. Most of Stock A's volatility is unique risk, which diversified investors can remove, so it earns no premium. Total risk does not set the required return.

LEWIS Q: A stock's correlation with the market falls, while its own standard deviation and the market's stay the same. What happens to its beta?

HEART: Answer. Beta falls. Here is why. Beta equals the correlation with the market times the ratio of the stock's standard deviation to the market's. With both standard deviations unchanged, beta moves in proportion to the correlation. The stock is just as volatile as before, but less of its movement is tied to the market, so it carries less systematic risk.

HEART Q: In the market model, what does the error term capture?

LEWIS: Answer. The abnormal return, the firm specific part of the period's return. Here is why. The market model splits a period's return into an intercept, beta times the market's return in that period, and an error term. The first two pieces are what the market's move predicts for the stock. Whatever is left over, such as a jump on surprise company news, is the abnormal return, the unique piece that diversification washes out.

LEWIS Q: What is on the horizontal axis of the capital market line and of the security market line, and what plots on each?

HEART: Answer. Standard deviation for the capital market line and beta for the security market line, with only efficient portfolios on the first and every properly priced asset on the second. Here is why. The capital market line measures total risk, and only combinations of the market portfolio with the risk free asset earn the best possible return for their total risk. The security market line measures only systematic risk, the risk that is priced, so every fairly priced stock or portfolio lands on it, diversified or not.

HEART Q: A fairly priced individual stock plots on the security market line. Where does it plot relative to the capital market line?

LEWIS: Answer. Below it. Here is why. Its expected return is set by its beta, so it is fair for its systematic risk. But it also carries unique risk, which raises its standard deviation without adding any expected return. On a chart of return against total risk, that pushes it to the right, below the capital market line.

LEWIS Q: Your forecast return for a stock plots above the security market line. Is the stock undervalued or overvalued, and what do you do?

HEART: Answer. Undervalued, so you buy it. Here is why. Plotting above the line means the stock is expected to earn more than its beta requires. Expected return and price move in opposite directions, so a high expected return for the risk means today's price is too low. As investors buy, the price rises and the expected return falls back toward the line.

HEART Q: Investors become more risk averse and demand a larger market risk premium. What happens to the security market line?

LEWIS: Answer. It gets steeper. Here is why. The slope of the security market line is the market risk premium. The line still starts at the risk free rate at a beta of zero, but each unit of beta now commands more return, so high beta assets see their required returns rise the most. A rise in the risk free rate with no change in the premium would instead shift the whole line up in parallel.

LEWIS Q: A portfolio's M squared comes out above the market's return. What does that say about its Sharpe ratio, and what is the sign of its M squared alpha?

HEART: Answer. Its Sharpe ratio is above the market's, and its M squared alpha is positive. Here is why. M squared is the return of the portfolio after scaling it, with borrowing or lending at the risk free rate, to the market's standard deviation. At the same risk as the market, it can only out earn the market if it earns more per unit of total risk, which means a higher Sharpe ratio. M squared alpha is the gap between M squared and the market's return, so it is positive.

HEART Q: A client's entire savings sit with one manager who runs a concentrated portfolio. Which measures fit, and why not the others?

LEWIS: Answer. The Sharpe ratio or M squared. Here is why. Because this portfolio is everything the client holds, the client bears its total risk, including the unique risk of a few concentrated positions. The Sharpe ratio and M squared charge for that total risk. The Treynor measure and Jensen's alpha count only beta, so they would ignore the unique risk, which suits a portfolio that is one piece of a well diversified fund.

## Key takeaways

HEART: First, a few memory hooks.

LEWIS: For the market portfolio: same beliefs, same basket, so the basket must be the whole market. The line through it is the capital market line.

HEART: For risk: the market pays for the risk you cannot shed, not the risk you chose to keep. Systematic risk comes from the economy and stays. Unique risk comes from the company and diversifies away.

LEWIS: For beta: how hard a stock tends to echo the market. Twice the market's move, a beta near two. Half the move, near one half. The market is one and the risk free asset is zero. From covariance, divide by the market's variance, never its standard deviation. And a portfolio's beta is the weighted average of its holdings' betas.

HEART: For the CAPM, think of a taxi fare. The flat charge when you get in is the risk free rate, the rate per mile is the market risk premium, and the miles are beta.

LEWIS: For the assumptions: same view, same horizon, no frictions, no giants, any size slice, and every investor risk averse and maximizing utility. Same view, not the same appetite for risk.

HEART: For the two lines: the capital market line counts total risk and holds only efficient portfolios. The security market line counts only beta and holds every fairly priced asset. A fairly priced single stock sits on the security market line and below the capital market line.

LEWIS: For mispricing: above the line, a bargain. Below the line, too dear. The vertical axis is return, not price.

HEART: For grading managers: Sharpe and Treynor are slopes, M squared and Jensen's alpha are percentages. Sharpe and M squared see total risk, Treynor and Jensen see only beta. A portfolio that stands alone is judged on total risk, and a portfolio in a crowd is judged on beta. M squared is the return after turning the volume knob to the market's level, and M squared alpha is the gap to the market.

LEWIS: Now, how you would say the big ideas to a senior leader.

HEART: On reward for risk: investors get paid for exposure to the economy as a whole, not for company specific bets they could have spread away. The jargon is systematic risk, unsystematic risk and diversification.

LEWIS: On sensitivity: this stock tends to move about one and a half times as much as the market, so it needs a higher return to be worth holding. The jargon is beta.

HEART: On hurdle rates: demand the safe rate plus a charge for how much the investment moves with the broader market. The jargon is the capital asset pricing model, the security market line and the cost of equity.

LEWIS: On valuation: if our forecast says a stock will earn more than its market risk calls for, it is cheap, and if less, it is expensive. The jargon is positive or negative alpha, above or below the security market line.

HEART: On performance: a raw return means little until we adjust for the risk taken, and the right adjustment depends on whether this portfolio is all the client owns or one piece of a diversified whole. The jargon is the Sharpe ratio, M squared, the Treynor measure and Jensen's alpha.
