LEWIS: Reading 87 covers the two families of behavioral bias, cognitive errors and emotional biases, each bias with the investing mistake it causes, and how shared biases may show up in bubbles and crashes, the value effect and home bias. Reading 88 covers what risk management is, the framework around it, risk governance, risk tolerance and risk budgeting, financial and nonfinancial risks, how risk is measured, and the four ways to modify it.

## Module 87.1, Cognitive Errors vs. Emotional Biases

HEART: Traditional finance imagines investors as flawless reasoners who miss no relevant fact and never let feelings in, so a market full of them prices assets efficiently. Behavioral finance asks what real people actually do. Starting in the early nineteen seventies, the psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky gave people simple logic problems and found that their intuitive answers went wrong in the same ways, again and again.

LEWIS: That phrase, the same ways, is the point. Random mistakes wash out across millions of investors, but a systematic bias is widespread, so it is predictable. And there is a hopeful idea in the research. If investors and advisers learn to spot these patterns, prices should drift closer to the efficient ideal of traditional theory.

HEART: The whole reading rests on one split. A cognitive error is a mistake in thinking. It comes from faulty reasoning: not understanding statistics, processing information badly, illogical reasoning, or memory that plays tricks. It can also come from deciding on partial information. The person is trying to be rational but lacks the capacity or the facts to pull it off.

LEWIS: An emotional bias does not come from conscious thought at all. It comes from feelings, impulses and intuition. It is a spontaneous reaction to how a decision looks and feels, not a flaw in how the numbers were analyzed.

HEART: Picture two people who are nervous about flying. The first believes flying is more dangerous than driving, because plane crashes get so much news coverage. Show him the accident statistics per mile traveled and his view may well change. That is a cognitive error. The second knows those statistics perfectly well and still grips the armrest on every takeoff. No chart will cure that, because it is emotional.

LEWIS: And that is why the split matters. Cognitive errors can often be reduced with awareness, better training and more information. Emotional biases are hard to overcome, so an adviser usually has to accommodate them, adapting the plan to the client rather than arguing the feeling away. That might mean a somewhat more conservative portfolio than the textbook ideal, because a plan the client abandons in a panic is worse than a slightly less efficient plan he sticks with.

HEART: One wrinkle. Real biases often have both a reasoning part and a feeling part. When you try to reduce a mixed bias, your odds are better if you work on the cognitive part, because that is the part information can reach.

LEWIS: Cognitive errors come in two groups. Belief perseverance biases are a stubborn reluctance to change a conclusion or decision you have already made. Processing errors are mistakes in how the information gets analyzed in the first place. Five biases sit in the first group and four in the second.

HEART: Belief perseverance starts with an idea from psychology called cognitive dissonance, the stress you feel when new information calls a belief into question. Picture someone who has just paid top dollar for a house and then reads that a highway is planned right behind it. He can ease the sting by admitting the purchase may have been a mistake. Or he can dismiss the news, questioning whether it is true, who reported it and whether it really applies to his street. Dismissing it is usually easier, so the result is a bias in favor of what people already believe.

LEWIS: First, conservatism bias. Picture a friend who decided years ago that a certain restaurant is the best in town. The chef left and the food has slipped, but he still recommends it. He formed his first view sensibly. He just never updated it. In investing terms, conservatism bias is a sensible first view that never gets revised enough. The investor clings to his prior probabilities and gives fresh evidence too little weight. Old forecasts and allocations stay put, reactions come late, and information that takes real work to absorb gets skipped, because rethinking is tiring and uncomfortable. The classic mistake is holding an investment too long after the facts behind it have changed.

HEART: Second, confirmation bias. Think of someone who has just started a new diet. Once she has decided it works, every success story online jumps out at her, and every critical study gets scrolled past. She is shopping for evidence that agrees with her.

LEWIS: Confirmation bias means seeking out or focusing on information that supports a belief, while avoiding or downplaying information that contradicts it, sometimes bending new information until it fits. Three consequences to know. Taking in the good news and ignoring the bad. Setting up a decision process, such as a stock screen, that is built to confirm a favored view. And growing overconfident that the current belief is right. The remedy is to go and read the strongest case against your position.

HEART: Third, representativeness bias. Picture a new coffee shop with exposed brick, a chalkboard menu and a line out the door in its first week. People conclude it will be a lasting hit, because it looks like the cafes that became hits. They are judging by resemblance.

LEWIS: Representativeness means putting something into a category based on a few features, then assuming it will behave like everything else in that category. Things alike in some ways get treated as alike in other ways too. It comes in two forms. Base rate neglect means judging an individual case without considering how common the trait is in the whole population. The coffee shop story is base rate neglect, because a large share of new restaurants fail within a few years, however stylish they look.

HEART: Sample size neglect means drawing a conclusion from a small sample, as if it reliably described the whole population. A basketball player hits five shots in a row and the crowd decides he is on fire, though streaks like that turn up by chance far more often than people expect. The investing mistakes are attaching too much importance to a few features or a short stretch of results, and leaning on simple labels instead of a thorough analysis.

LEWIS: Fourth, illusion of control bias, which you may also hear called control bias. Think of the craps player who throws the dice gently when he wants a low number and hard when he wants a high one. Or the lottery player who insists on picking her own numbers, because a ticket she chose feels luckier.

HEART: Illusion of control means believing you can steer outcomes that are really out of your hands. It tends to travel with some emotional cousins: illusion of knowledge, self attribution and overconfidence. The investing mistake is overweighting securities you feel some control over, such as shares of the company you work for or are otherwise tied to. The result is a portfolio that is not diversified enough.

LEWIS: Fifth, hindsight bias. After the big game, everybody at the office says they knew the underdog would win. People remember the predictions that came true, forget the ones that did not, and overrate what could have been known, because they only ever see the outcome that happened. It is often called the knew it all along effect.

HEART: It shows up as remembering your earlier forecasts as better than they were, seeing whatever happened as inevitable, and assuming you could have foreseen it. The investing mistakes are overconfidence about predicting, and a quieter one. An investor may throw out a sound method because it happened to be wrong last year, and adopt a poor one that happened to work by luck.

LEWIS: Now the second group, processing errors, also called information processing biases. These are less about protecting a belief and more about how the information itself gets handled.

HEART: First, anchoring and adjustment. Think of a store tag that shows a high original price crossed out next to the sale price. That first number anchors your sense of value, and the sale price feels like a bargain even if nobody ever paid the original.

LEWIS: Anchoring means starting from a prior number, giving it too much weight, and adjusting away from it too little as new information comes in. Say an analyst hears that a company has won a huge new contract and raises her profit forecast only a little, because she starts from her old figure and nudges it. The mistake is underestimating what the new information implies. The fix is to judge new data on its own merits, as if the anchor never existed.

HEART: Anchoring and conservatism often appear together, and the difference is focus. Anchoring is about a specific starting number. Conservatism is about failing to update a belief in general.

LEWIS: Second, mental accounting bias. Picture a family with a jar labeled vacation. They will not touch that money, even while they carry a credit card balance at a painful interest rate. A dollar is a dollar, but the label makes it feel different.

HEART: Mental accounting means treating money differently depending on which account it sits in or where it came from. That clashes with a core principle: every investment decision should be made for the whole portfolio, given the investor's goals and risk tolerance. Manage each bucket on its own and the overall portfolio is not the best one for the investor. Positions in different buckets can even offset each other, because nobody looks at how they move together.

LEWIS: A very common form is treating income differently from price gains. An investor who will happily spend dividends and interest but never sell a share may load up on income producing securities, and end up with a mix that does not fit his real needs.

HEART: Third, framing bias. Ground beef labeled seventy five percent lean sounds healthier than the same beef labeled twenty five percent fat. Same package, different frame, different choice.

LEWIS: Framing means the decision depends on how the question or the data is presented. One pattern to know. When a choice is framed as a gain, most people play it safe and take the sure thing. Frame the identical choice as a loss and many will gamble to avoid it. That flip ties straight into loss aversion in the next module.

HEART: In investing, framing shows up when someone stares at short term price swings and loses sight of the long horizon. Shown daily, a stock portfolio looks frightening. Shown over decades, it looks steady. The investor who sees only the daily frame tends to end up too conservative. And a badly worded risk tolerance questionnaire can label a client more or less risk averse than he really is, so the portfolio built on it does not fit him.

LEWIS: Fourth, availability bias. After a shark attack makes the national news, plenty of people stay out of the ocean that summer, though their real chance of being bitten is tiny and has not changed.

HEART: Availability means putting too much weight on information that is easy to get, easy to recall, or drawn narrowly from your own experience. People judge how likely something is by how quickly examples come to mind. Recent events and anything heavily covered in the media come to mind easily, so they feel more probable than they are.

LEWIS: The investing mistakes. Picking a fund or a manager because you have seen the ads or recognize the name. Limiting your choices to companies you know from daily life, which leaves the allocation unbalanced and poorly diversified. And overreacting to the last year or two of markets while ignoring the long history.

HEART: The trap in this module is the sorting. Illusion of control sounds like ego, but it is a belief perseverance error. Mental accounting sounds like a feeling about money, but it is a processing error. Go by the list, not by how the bias sounds.

LEWIS: The takeaway. Cognitive errors are faulty reasoning, often reduced with better information and training. Belief perseverance has five members: conservatism, confirmation, representativeness, illusion of control and hindsight. Processing errors have four: anchoring and adjustment, mental accounting, framing and availability.

## Module 87.2, Emotional Biases

HEART: A few names from the cognitive side come back here, so start with a sorting rule. If a view rests on an unconscious feeling the person is unwilling or unable to change, treat it as an emotional bias. If a fairly simple change in thinking, or in information, would fix it, treat it as cognitive.

LEWIS: Nobody has written an official rulebook for this, but six biases are generally placed on the emotional side, rooted in feeling rather than conscious thought. Loss aversion, overconfidence, self control, status quo, endowment and regret aversion.

HEART: First, loss aversion. Imagine finding a twenty dollar bill on the sidewalk. Nice. Now imagine a twenty dollar bill falling out of your pocket. For most people the sting of the loss is much bigger than the pleasure of the find. Loss aversion means feeling more pain from a loss than pleasure from a gain of the same size.

LEWIS: Kahneman and Tversky studied how people choose between a sure outcome and a gamble with the same expected value. When the choice is about gains, most people take the sure thing. When the identical choice is described as a loss, most people gamble, hoping to avoid the loss entirely. What sets the frame is a reference point, often the price paid. Above it feels like a gain and below it feels like a loss. This work became known as prospect theory.

HEART: Here is a distinction the exam likes. A risk averse investor, offered two investments with the same expected return, simply picks the less risky one. A loss averse investor feels losses more than gains, and that can make him take more risk, not less, when he is trying to avoid a loss. Picture a gambler who is down for the night and doubles his bets to get back to even.

LEWIS: The investing mistakes. Selling winners quickly for small gains, which means trading too much, paying more costs and earning less. Hanging on to assets that have fallen in value and deteriorated in quality. After an initial drop, taking extra risk in the hope of getting back to even, which professional managers are especially prone to. And calling a position a gain or a loss purely because of where the reference point happens to be framed.

HEART: Selling winners too early and holding losers too long is often called the disposition effect. The market neither knows nor cares what you paid, so the purchase price has no bearing on what the asset does next.

LEWIS: Second, overconfidence bias. Ask a room full of people whether they are better than the typical driver and most of the hands go up, though only half of them can be above the middle. Overconfidence means overestimating your own intuitive ability or reasoning.

HEART: It often shows up as illusion of knowledge, believing you predict better than you actually do. And it pairs with self attribution bias, taking personal credit when things go right, the self enhancing part, and blaming other people or circumstances when things go wrong, the self protecting part.

LEWIS: Two forms to know. Prediction overconfidence means underestimating uncertainty. Forecast ranges are too narrow, so the person understates the standard deviation of his own predictions. Picture a contractor who swears the kitchen renovation will take five to six weeks, and it takes eleven. Certainty overconfidence means putting too high a probability on being right, like a trivia player who is sure of every answer and gets half of them wrong.

HEART: Why is overconfidence filed as emotional, when it clearly has a reasoning element? Because it is hard for most people to correct, and it is rooted in the desire to feel good about ourselves. The investing mistakes are underestimating risk, overestimating return and failing to diversify enough. Overconfident investors also tend to trade too much. And watch the near twin. Illusion of control, believing you can influence outcomes, is a cognitive error. Overconfidence, overrating your knowledge and predictions, is emotional.

LEWIS: Third, self control bias. Think of someone who joins a gym in January with big plans, and by March the membership card has not left his wallet. Self control bias means lacking the discipline to put long term goals ahead of short term satisfaction. Preferring a smaller payoff now over a bigger payoff later is called hyperbolic discounting.

HEART: The investing mistakes. Saving too little for retirement, then trying to make up for it by taking too much risk later. And leaning too heavily on income producing assets to fund current spending, which can throw the allocation out of balance. The remedy is structure: a proper investment plan, meaning an asset allocation, and a budget that saves enough, both reviewed regularly.

LEWIS: Fourth, status quo bias. Think of how long people stay with the same phone plan or the same bank, even when a better deal is obvious. Status quo bias means comfort with the current situation makes a person resist change. Whatever is already in place, or whatever happens automatically unless you opt out, tends to win. The investing mistakes are holding a portfolio whose risk no longer suits the investor, and never even considering better alternatives.

HEART: Fifth, endowment bias. Think of a garage sale where the owner prices a battered old lamp at far more than he would ever pay for one, because it is his lamp. Endowment bias means feeling that an asset is special, and worth more, simply because you already own it. In studies, the price people demand to sell something they own tends to be higher than the price they would pay to buy the very same thing. That gap is called the endowment effect.

LEWIS: It is especially common with inherited assets, where sentiment gets mixed into the value. A good test question is this. If this holding were cash today, would I spend that cash buying it? The investing mistakes are failing to sell assets that no longer fit the investor's needs, and keeping familiar holdings simply because they feel comfortable.

HEART: Sixth, regret aversion. Picture a driver in heavy traffic who could switch lanes and does not, because if he switches and his new lane stops, he will kick himself. If he stays put and the other lane speeds up, somehow it stings less. Regret aversion means avoiding action out of excessive fear that it will turn out to be wrong.

LEWIS: Two terms carry it. An error of commission is doing something that turns out badly. An error of omission is failing to do something that would have turned out well. Regret aversion puts too much weight on commission and too little on omission, because regret feels sharper when you acted.

HEART: Herding is a form of regret aversion. Go with the crowd, and if everyone turns out to be wrong, nobody can single you out for blame. The other investing mistake is excess conservatism. Riskier assets visibly fall from time to time, so the investor avoids them to avoid the regret, and gives up return.

LEWIS: Here is the biggest trap in this module. Status quo, endowment and regret aversion all look the same from the outside. The investor does nothing. You tell them apart by the reason. Status quo is comfort with what is already there. Endowment is extra value placed on something because you own it. Regret aversion is fear that acting will turn out to be a mistake.

HEART: Now from individuals to markets. An anomaly is a result that does not fit the prevailing model of risk and return. A market inefficiency is an anomaly that offers a real chance to earn positive risk adjusted returns. Many reported anomalies have duller explanations: a small sample, a lucky time period, or a poorly specified model, including risk the model measured badly. But some persist, and behavioral finance tries to explain them with biases.

LEWIS: Start with bubbles and crashes, which go back centuries, and part of the story can be rational. Take a fund manager in nineteen ninety seven who is sure technology shares are overpriced. If she sells out, she may watch them keep soaring for more than two years while clients leave. If she stays in, planning to sell just before the peak, she will very likely mistime it. Either way her career can take the hit. Nor can she simply bet against the bubble, because short selling is restricted and dangerous while prices are still racing upward.

HEART: Behavioral finance has not explained bubbles as a whole, but several biases may help feed them. Overconfidence brings too much trading, underestimated risk and too little diversification. A long run of good results, combined with self attribution and hindsight, makes investors credit their own skill for gains the whole market handed them. Confirmation bias filters out signs that valuations cannot keep rising, and treats the first drops as buying opportunities. Anchoring makes recent highs look like rational prices even as the decline begins. And fear of regret keeps even skeptical investors in the market.

LEWIS: Next, the value effect, also called the value versus growth anomaly. Value stocks have low prices relative to book value or earnings, and high dividend yields. Growth stocks have high price to earnings ratios and low dividend yields. Over long periods, value stocks have outperformed growth stocks.

HEART: The traditional explanation is risk. In the early nineteen nineties, Eugene Fama and Kenneth French found that once a model of stock returns included factors for company size and the book to market ratio, the evidence of value outperformance went away. That suggests the extra return was compensation for risks those factors capture.

LEWIS: The behavioral explanation is the halo effect, a version of representativeness. Think of a phone maker everyone admires, with sales booming and its shares climbing month after month. The glow around the business spills over into a belief that the shares must be a great buy whatever they cost, and that enthusiasm pushes growth stocks above their fair value. A wonderful company and a wonderful investment are different things, because the price you pay matters.

HEART: Last, home bias. Take a saver in Toronto with nearly all her stock money in Canadian companies, though Canada is only a small slice of the world market. Investors everywhere lean toward their own country, and often toward their own region within it, far more than a global portfolio would suggest. The usual explanations are a belief that they know local firms better, or simply the comfort of investing close to home. A related pattern is underestimating the risk, or overestimating the returns, of companies whose products people buy every day or whose marketing they see constantly.

LEWIS: The takeaway. The six emotional biases are loss aversion, overconfidence, self control, status quo, endowment and regret aversion, and they are usually accommodated rather than corrected. At the market level, many anomalies come down to mismeasured risk, but biases may help explain bubbles and crashes, the value effect through the halo effect, and home bias.

## Module 88.1, Introduction to Risk Management

LEWIS: Start with a misconception. Many people think risk management means making risk as small as possible. It does not. Any return above the risk free rate is payment for bearing some risk. No manager decides what the markets deliver in a given quarter, but every manager decides which risks to carry and how large each one is.

HEART: So risk management is choosing the best bundle of risks for an organization and keeping it there. It has three steps. Identify the organization's risk tolerance. Identify and measure the risks it actually faces. Then modify and monitor those risks.

LEWIS: Think of a restaurant. It happily takes the risk that a bold new menu flops, because cooking is what it does well, but it buys liability insurance so a lawsuit over a wet floor cannot wipe it out. Unwanted risks can be cut with organizational changes, insurance or hedging.

HEART: The same thinking fits a portfolio manager, a bank or a household. Only the goal differs. A company or a portfolio aims at value or return, while a person aims at the most expected satisfaction, which economists call expected utility.

LEWIS: A risk management framework is the full structure that makes this work, and it should cover seven activities. Establishing the processes and policies for risk governance. Determining risk tolerance. Identifying and measuring the risks. Managing and mitigating them to reach the best bundle. Monitoring exposures over time. Communicating across the organization. And performing strategic risk analysis, which feeds risk information back into business decisions. A comprehensive framework addresses all seven.

HEART: Risk governance sits at the top. It is senior management deciding three things: how much risk the organization will tolerate, what its ideal mix of risk exposures looks like, and how the risk management function itself will be overseen. It gives organization wide guidance on three kinds of risk: risks to pursue efficiently, risks to keep within limits, and risks to reduce or avoid. A risk management committee gives different parts of the organization a place to raise how risks are measured, how they combine and how to reduce the unwanted ones.

LEWIS: At a bank or credit union, this is where the board comes in. Senior management proposes the limits, and the board approves them and oversees how they are kept. Many write the overall amount into a board approved risk appetite statement, and in that setting the word tolerance often means the narrower limits set beneath it. For the exam, the overall amount is called risk tolerance.

HEART: Risk tolerance is the total amount of risk the organization is willing to carry while chasing its goals, and the decision belongs to top management. That means sorting out which risks the organization can take effectively and which it should reduce or avoid. Four factors shape it. Expertise in its lines of business. Skill at responding to bad events from outside. Its regulatory environment. And its financial strength, meaning its ability to absorb losses.

LEWIS: Picture two lenders. One has decades of experience in farm loans, plenty of capital and staff who have been through droughts. The other is new to farm lending and thinly capitalized. Same risk, very different tolerance. Management looks at risks arising inside the organization and from outside, and weighs each against the expected benefit of bearing it and its fit with the organization's goals.

HEART: Risk budgeting is how that tolerance gets spent. The organization hands out its total allowance of risk across assets or investments, looking at how risky each one is and how they behave together. The goal is to put the acceptable amount of risk where it earns the greatest expected return over time. Like a household budget, the total is fixed, so every unit of risk has to earn its place.

LEWIS: The budget can be a single measure, such as portfolio beta, value at risk, portfolio duration or the variance of returns. It can be split by category of investment, so much for domestic stocks, so much for international bonds, and so on. Or it can be built from risk factors such as interest rate risk, equity market risk and currency risk, each one estimated and then added together to see whether the total matches the organization's tolerance.

HEART: Now the sources of risk. Financial risks come from exposure to financial markets, and there are three. Market risk is uncertainty about the prices of stocks, commodities and currencies, and about interest rates. Credit risk is uncertainty about whether the other side of a transaction will meet its obligations. Liquidity risk is the risk of loss when you sell an asset at a time when market conditions push the price below its fair value.

LEWIS: An easy way to hold the three is prices, promises and buyers. Market risk is about prices moving. Credit risk is about promises being broken. Liquidity risk is about buyers being scarce just when you need to sell.

HEART: Nonfinancial risks come from the organization's own operations and from outside sources other than financial markets. There are eight to know. Operational risk is the risk of losses from human error, faulty processes, weak security or business interruptions, and cyber risk, a disruption of the organization's information technology, is one type. Solvency risk is the risk that the organization cannot keep operating because it has run out of cash. Regulatory risk is the risk that the rules change, adding costs or restricting what it can do. Governmental or political risk, which includes tax risk, is the risk that political actions outside the regulatory framework, such as a jump in tax rates, impose big costs.

LEWIS: Legal risk is uncertainty about exposure to future legal action. Model risk is the risk that the values coming out of the organization's models are wrong. Tail risk is the risk that extreme events are more likely than the organization's analysis suggests, especially when it wrongly assumed a normal distribution. And accounting risk is the risk that its accounting policies and assumptions are judged to be wrong.

HEART: Notice two that sound financial but are filed as nonfinancial: solvency risk and tail risk. A third you may also meet is settlement risk, the chance that one side of a trade pays or delivers but never receives what it is owed in return, and it is filed as nonfinancial too. And keep liquidity and solvency apart. Liquidity risk is about selling an asset below its fair value. Solvency risk is about the whole organization running out of cash.

LEWIS: Individuals face their own versions. Mortality risk is dying before you have provided for your family's future needs, usually addressed with life insurance. Longevity risk is living longer than planned, so the money runs out, and a lifetime annuity can reduce it. The risk of large health care costs is addressed with health insurance. The approach matches a company's: decide which risks to bear yourself, which to prevent or avoid, and which to take for the best expected outcome.

HEART: Risks also interact. Picture a bank or credit union when interest rates jump. That is market risk, and it cuts the value of the long fixed rate bonds and loans it holds. Depositors notice better rates elsewhere and move money out. To meet those withdrawals, the institution sells bonds into a weak market below fair value, which is liquidity risk. If withdrawals keep coming faster than it can raise cash, it risks running out, and that is solvency risk. Interactions like that are frequent, and they matter most during market stress.

LEWIS: Now measuring risk. For individual assets there are three measures. Standard deviation measures the volatility of asset prices and interest rates, though it works less well when outcomes are not normally distributed, especially with negative skew or positive excess kurtosis, meaning fat tails. Beta measures the market risk of a stock or a stock portfolio. It reflects the benefit of diversification, so it suits a security held inside a well diversified portfolio, while standard deviation measures risk on a stand alone basis. Duration measures how sensitive a bond's price is to changes in interest rates.

HEART: Derivatives have their own measures, often called the Greeks. Delta is how much the derivative's value changes when the price of the underlying asset changes. Gamma is how much delta itself changes when the underlying price moves. If delta is your speed, gamma is your acceleration. Vega is sensitivity to the volatility of the underlying asset's price. And rho is sensitivity to the risk free interest rate.

LEWIS: Tail risk, the uncertainty about how likely extreme losses are, has its own downside risk measures. Value at risk, or VaR, is the minimum loss over a stated period that will occur with a stated probability. Think of the hundred year flood level on a river. In any given year there is about a one in a hundred chance the water rises at least that high. That level marks where the bad outcomes begin. It says nothing about how high the water goes beyond it.

HEART: VaR works the same way. Suppose a fund reports a one day VaR with a five percent probability. On about one trading day in twenty, the loss should be at least the VaR amount. And that is the trap. VaR is not the maximum loss. It is the minimum loss on the bad days, and the losses beyond it can be far larger.

LEWIS: VaR became an accepted risk measure for banks and has been used in setting minimum capital requirements. The newer international standard for banks that model their own trading risk swaps it for expected shortfall, which is the conditional VaR idea coming up in a moment. But the result depends heavily on the method and the inputs, and bad inputs or the wrong assumption about the distribution give misleading answers. So VaR should be paired with other risk measures, never trusted alone.

HEART: Conditional VaR fills the gap. It is the expected loss given that the loss is at least as large as the VaR, the probability weighted average of all the losses in that bad tail. In the flood picture, it is the average height of the water in the years it goes over the line. It is similar in spirit to loss given default for a bond, which asks how much you lose once the bad event has happened.

LEWIS: Two more tools supplement them. Stress testing looks at the effect of a specific, usually extreme, change in one key variable, such as a sudden jump in interest rates. Scenario analysis is a similar what if exercise that changes several inputs at once. A bank or credit union shocking interest rates to see what happens to net interest income is running a stress test. A recession story with rising unemployment, falling home prices and higher loan losses together is scenario analysis.

HEART: Some risks resist hard numbers. An event with almost no history, such as a week long outage at a major cloud provider, usually needs a subjective judgment of how likely it is. Market prices help, because the cost of insurance or hedges reflects the market's combined view of the expected loss. For operational risk, one approach is to study a large sample of firms to see how often big operational losses happen and how large they are on average. Tax and regulatory changes are political, so they need judgment too. Even a rough subjective view beats ignoring a risk.

LEWIS: Finally, modifying risk. The goal is the best mix of risks, not zero risk, so it may mean taking more of some, less of others and eliminating some altogether. There are four ways to modify a risk: prevent or avoid it, accept it, transfer it, or shift it.

HEART: Risk avoidance means simply not doing the activity whose outcome is uncertain. A mountain guide company that stops running winter climbs has avoided avalanche risk entirely, along with whatever those trips would have earned. Avoiding a risk is usually a top management decision, made when setting risk tolerance, because the risk is judged to outweigh the benefits. Risk prevention means spending to stop a loss before it happens, like a hospital that requires two nurses to check every medication dose. It makes sense when the benefit of reducing the risk is greater than its cost.

LEWIS: Risk acceptance means keeping the risk and bearing it as efficiently as possible, and diversification is one way to do that. Keeping a risk is sometimes called self insurance. That simply means the organization absorbs any losses itself, and the trap is that the label can describe plain inaction rather than a considered decision. Some organizations set up a reserve account so losses hit less hard.

HEART: Risk transfer means another party takes on the risk, and insurance is the main example. Insurers diversify across many policyholders, so the premiums of many pay for the losses of a few. That only works well when the risks are not highly correlated. An insurer covering thousands of homes along one hurricane coast holds correlated risk, so it may pass some on to another company by buying reinsurance.

LEWIS: Two special forms. A surety bond pays the organization if a third party fails to perform under a contract with it, like a builder who walks off a town's new library halfway through. A fidelity bond pays for losses from employee theft or misconduct, such as a cashier quietly skimming from the till.

HEART: Risk shifting changes the distribution of possible outcomes, mainly with derivatives. Forwards, futures and swaps can reduce or remove a risk by locking in a price. An airline that buys fuel futures shifts away much of the risk of a fuel price spike, and gives up the benefit if fuel gets cheaper. Options change one side of the outcomes far more than the other. Buy a put on a holding and you set a floor under its value while keeping the upside, in exchange for the premium. Sell a call and you give up some upside, and the premium you collect softens the downside by that amount.

LEWIS: So keep the labels straight. Insurance, surety bonds and fidelity bonds transfer risk. Derivatives shift it. Diversification and self insurance are forms of accepting it. The choice always comes down to costs against benefits, and one risk can get several methods: diversify part, insure part where insurance is sensibly priced, hedge part and bear the rest. The end result should match the organization's risk tolerance and hold the risks top management judged worth their cost.

HEART: The takeaway. Risk management is choosing the best bundle of risks, not minimizing risk. Governance and tolerance are set at the top, and the risk budget spends that tolerance. VaR is a minimum loss on bad days, never a maximum. And risks are modified by preventing or avoiding, accepting, transferring or shifting.

## Recall check

LEWIS Q: Why does it matter whether a bias is a cognitive error or an emotional bias?

HEART: Answer. Because cognitive errors can often be corrected, while emotional biases usually have to be accommodated. Here is why. A cognitive error comes from faulty reasoning or missing information, so better information, training and awareness can reach it. An emotional bias comes from feelings and impulses, which facts rarely change, so the adviser adapts the plan around it.

HEART Q: An analyst has long believed a retailer is a turnaround story, and after several reports of falling store traffic he barely changes that view, because rethinking it feels like too much work. Which bias is this, and which group?

LEWIS: Answer. Conservatism bias, a belief perseverance cognitive error. Here is why. He formed a reasonable view at first, but now he overweights it and underweights the new information, avoiding the effort of updating. It is not anchoring, because he clings to a belief in general, not to one specific starting number.

LEWIS Q: An investor is sure a newly listed company will be the next giant, because its founder and its pitch remind him of a famous success. Which bias is this, and which form?

HEART: Answer. Representativeness bias, in the form of base rate neglect. Here is why. He is judging the company by how much it resembles a familiar winner, and ignoring the base rate, which is how few newly listed companies ever become giants. Resemblance in a few features does not make two companies alike in the ways that matter.

HEART Q: How does loss aversion differ from risk aversion, and which one can make an investor take more risk?

LEWIS: Answer. Loss aversion is the one that can push an investor toward more risk. Here is why. Risk aversion just means choosing the less risky of two investments with the same expected return. Loss aversion means a loss hurts more than an equal gain pleases, judged from a reference point such as the purchase price. Faced with a loss, many people gamble rather than lock it in, so they hold losers and even add risk.

LEWIS Q: An investor's forecast ranges for a stock keep turning out too narrow, and the actual result lands outside them again and again. Which form of overconfidence is this?

HEART: Answer. Prediction overconfidence. Here is why. Ranges that are too narrow mean she underestimates uncertainty, the standard deviation of her own predictions. Certainty overconfidence is the other form, stating too high a probability of being right.

HEART Q: Three clients will not touch an outdated portfolio. The first says it has always been set up this way. The second says the shares feel special simply because they are his. The third says that if he sells and the shares then rise, he will never forgive himself. Which bias is each?

LEWIS: Answer. Status quo bias, endowment bias and regret aversion, in that order. Here is why. All three show up as doing nothing, so the reason decides. Comfort with the current arrangement is status quo. Valuing something more because you own it is endowment. Fear that acting will turn out wrong, weighting errors of commission over errors of omission, is regret aversion.

LEWIS Q: Value stocks have outperformed growth stocks over long periods. What is the traditional explanation, and what is the behavioral one?

HEART: Answer. The traditional explanation is compensation for risk, and the behavioral one is the halo effect. Here is why. Fama and French found that adding factors for company size and the book to market ratio removed the evidence of value outperformance, which points to risk. The halo effect, a version of representativeness, has investors assume a great company must be a great stock, so they overpay for growth stocks.

HEART Q: Are solvency risk and tail risk financial or nonfinancial risks?

LEWIS: Answer. Both are nonfinancial. Here is why. Financial risks are the three that come from exposure to financial markets: market, credit and liquidity risk. Solvency risk is the organization running out of cash, and tail risk is extreme events being more likely than its analysis assumed. Both sit with the risks that come from the organization's own operations and outside sources.

LEWIS Q: Which comes first, risk tolerance or risk budgeting, and what does the budget do?

HEART: Answer. Risk tolerance comes first, and the budget allocates it. Here is why. Top management sets tolerance, the total amount of risk the organization will take. Risk budgeting then spreads that fixed total across assets, asset classes or risk factors, aiming it where it earns the greatest expected return. The budget can be one measure, such as beta, duration or VaR, or a sum of risk factor exposures checked against the tolerance.

HEART Q: A fund reports its one day VaR at five percent. Is that figure the most it can lose on a bad day?

LEWIS: Answer. No, it is the minimum loss on the worst days, roughly one day in twenty. Here is why. VaR marks where the bad tail begins, not how far the tail runs, and losses beyond it can be much larger. That is why conditional VaR, the average loss within that tail, and stress tests are used alongside it.

LEWIS Q: A company buys a fidelity bond, and it also buys put options on a large investment holding. Which way of modifying risk is each?

HEART: Answer. The fidelity bond is risk transfer, and the puts are risk shifting. Here is why. A fidelity bond is a form of insurance, so another party takes on the loss from employee theft or misconduct for a premium. Put options are derivatives, and they change the distribution of outcomes by putting a floor under the holding's value.

## Key takeaways

HEART: First, a few memory hooks.

LEWIS: The master rule is cognitive, correct, emotional, accommodate. Facts can often fix a thinking mistake, but a feeling usually has to be planned around.

HEART: For belief perseverance, Cats Can Really Climb High. Conservatism, confirmation, representativeness, control and hindsight. Like a cat stuck up a tree, a belief that has climbed up will not come down.

LEWIS: For processing errors, picture the anchor, the jars, the frame and the headline. Anchoring on a starting number, mental accounting with money kept in separate jars, framing by how a choice is presented, and availability, judging likelihood by whatever headline comes to mind first.

HEART: For the six emotional biases, Losing Our Savings Stings Every Retiree. Loss aversion, overconfidence, self control, status quo, endowment and regret aversion.

LEWIS: For the three that look like doing nothing, comfy, mine and scared. Status quo is comfort with what is there. Endowment is valuing it more because it is mine. Regret aversion is being scared that acting will prove wrong.

HEART: Loss aversion is not risk aversion. Facing a loss, the loss averse investor tends to gamble. And assuming a great company must be a great stock is the halo effect.

LEWIS: For financial risks, prices, promises and buyers. Market, credit and liquidity. Everything else, from operations to tax law to a broken model, is nonfinancial, and that includes solvency and tail risk.

HEART: VaR is the flood line, not the height of the water. It marks where the bad days begin. Conditional VaR is the average height of the water in the years it goes over the line.

LEWIS: For modifying risk, stop or skip it, keep it, hand it off, or reshape it. That is prevent or avoid, accept, transfer and shift. Insurance hands it off, and derivatives reshape it.

HEART: Now, how you would say the big ideas to a senior leader.

LEWIS: On investor behavior: some mistakes come from bad information and fade once people see the facts. Others come from feelings, and the practical answer is a plan people will stick with. The jargon is cognitive errors, which are corrected, and emotional biases, which are accommodated.

HEART: On a common investor trap: people hate losses more than they like equal gains, so they hang on to losers and sometimes gamble to get back to even. The jargon is loss aversion and the disposition effect.

LEWIS: On how much risk to take: our job is not to minimize risk but to take the risks we are good at and paid for, within limits the board approves, and spend that risk where it earns the most. The jargon is risk governance, risk tolerance and risk budgeting.

HEART: On measurement: our headline risk number tells us where a bad day starts, not how bad it can get, so we pair it with tail averages and stress tests. The jargon is value at risk, conditional VaR, stress testing and scenario analysis.

LEWIS: And on managing risk: for each risk we decide whether to stop it, keep it, insure it or hedge it, based on cost against benefit. The jargon is risk prevention and avoidance, risk acceptance, risk transfer and risk shifting.
