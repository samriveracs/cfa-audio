LEWIS: Reading 89 covers what ethics means in investing, how professions earn trust, why this industry needs especially high standards, suitability versus fiduciary duty, the forces that push good people off course, the gap between ethics and law, and a four step framework for hard decisions. Reading 90 covers how CFA Institute investigates and disciplines its members and candidates, the six principles of the Code of Ethics, and the seven Standards of Professional Conduct with their twenty three parts.

## Module 89.1, Ethics and Trust

HEART: Start with a small, ordinary moment. You and a colleague build a forecasting model together. In a meeting, your boss praises it as your work. Saying nothing helps your next review, and it quietly takes something from your colleague. Nothing illegal has happened, yet most people feel the pull of a question. That question is ethics.

LEWIS: Ethics is the line a community draws between conduct it accepts as good and conduct it rejects as bad, a common understanding of right and wrong. Acting ethically means holding to moral principles in a way society would recognize as right. A second way to see it: ethical conduct leaves the people your actions touch better off.

HEART: Those people are called stakeholders, anyone affected directly or indirectly by what you do. For an investment professional, that means clients, coworkers, the employer and the profession as a whole. The hard cases are the ones where a choice is good for you and bad for one of them.

LEWIS: So here is the definition worth carrying. Ethical conduct balances your own interest against the effect on others. Notice what that does not say. It does not ask you to ignore your own interest. Earning a fee, wanting a promotion and building a career are all fine. The question is whether you pursue them at someone else's unfair expense.

HEART: Next, a code of ethics. A code of ethics is a group's moral principles put in writing. It tells members and outsiders alike what the group values and what conduct it expects, mostly as broad guidance. Some codes go further and spell out specific rules or standards, a floor of conduct every member has to clear.

LEWIS: And a code is part of what makes a group a profession. A profession is a body of people who share specialized knowledge, put it to work for others, and commit to a code of ethics. A professional code is a public promise. It tells outsiders that members will apply their expertise to serve clients honestly, and that raises public confidence that they will.

HEART: Think of airline pilots or structural engineers. You cannot check their work yourself. You board the plane or walk across the bridge because a professional system stands behind them. Professions tend to share a few features. They require specialized expert knowledge. They have a code and standards of behavior, and a regulatory body that enforces those rules and monitors members. They focus on clients' needs and on service to society, they expect members to put client interests first, and they encourage or require continuing education.

LEWIS: Those same features are how a profession earns trust. It demands high levels of expertise and skill. It sets ethical standards. It monitors conduct. It pushes members to keep learning so their competence grows rather than fades. It keeps the focus on clients. And experienced members mentor and inspire newer ones.

HEART: The short version for the exam: professions earn trust in three main ways. They demand real expertise, they set ethical standards, and they monitor how members actually behave.

LEWIS: Now, why does investment management need especially high ethical standards? Two reasons sit at the heart of it. First, clients hand over their wealth, and the professional takes on the job of both protecting it and growing it. That is a special responsibility.

HEART: Second, the product is intangible. Buy a pair of running shoes and you can try them on, run in them, and find out within a week whether they hold up. Investment advice and portfolio management give you nothing to hold. Results swing for reasons that have nothing to do with skill, and fees and conflicts are hard for a client to see. So the client struggles to tell what the service was really worth, and trust ends up doing far more of the work than it does in most businesses.

LEWIS: When that trust breaks, the damage spreads in rings. The client loses money. The firm and the professional lose business, because potential investors stay away. And then society pays.

HEART: Here is how. The financial industry stands between savers and the businesses that need funding. If savers do not trust their advisers, they entrust less money to the system. Less money available to fund businesses means a higher cost of capital, and that slows investment and growth.

LEWIS: There is a subtler cost too. An investor who cannot believe what advisers say carries an extra risk on top of the usual investment risks, the risk of being misled. That risk does not even have to be real to do damage. If investors merely suspect it, they put in less money and ask for higher returns before they commit.

HEART: And misleading information does not just shrink the pool of capital. It points capital in the wrong direction. Money that should have reached the firms with the best prospects for growth and for doing society good goes instead to whoever told the best story. That is called a misallocation of capital, and it slows the economy and hurts the well being of everyone in it.

LEWIS: The same logic shows up on a bank or credit union balance sheet. Deposits are cheap, stable funding only while depositors trust the institution. Lose that trust and the funding gets expensive fast.

HEART: That brings us to professionalism in investment management. Clients rely on the professional's expertise, judgment and ethics, often because they know little about securities, about how fees work, or about where bias can creep into a recommendation. So most of the features of a profession apply here.

LEWIS: And there are two levels of duty to know. Depending on the role and the rules that apply, an adviser may owe a client a suitability standard or a fiduciary standard.

HEART: Suitability is about fit. Do the investment's characteristics match the client's return requirements and risk tolerance? Picture two bond funds, both a reasonable fit for a cautious retiree. One costs more and pays the adviser a bigger commission. Under a pure suitability standard, recommending either one can pass, because both fit.

LEWIS: A fiduciary standard sets the bar higher. The professional has to put all that expertise to work for whatever is truly best for the client. Now choosing the pricier fund because it pays the adviser more is out. The trap is to treat suitable as if it meant best. Suitable means it fits. Fiduciary means the client's interest comes first.

HEART: Now the uncomfortable question. If almost everyone agrees ethics matters, why do capable, decent people still cross the line? There are three challenges to ethical behavior.

LEWIS: The first is overconfidence in your own ethics. Most of us believe we behave more ethically than the people around us. And we lean too hard on our own good character to keep us straight, as if the situation hardly mattered.

HEART: Picture a manager who thinks, I am an honest person, so whatever I decide here will be honest. That belief feels like a strength, but it is a blind spot. It skips the step of testing the decision against anyone else's view, and it ignores how much the situation is shaping the choice.

LEWIS: Which is the second challenge, situational influences. These are external pressures, and many researchers argue they shape ethical behavior more than personal traits do. Social pressure from others is one. Loyalty is a powerful one, to an employer, a supervisor, the organization, or the coworkers you sit with every day. And the prospect of more money or more prestige is the most obvious.

HEART: Loyalty is the sneaky one, because it feels like a virtue. On a team that has always accepted a vendor's lavish dinners, the next person to decline feels disloyal.

LEWIS: Situational influences also hide inside compliance culture. A firm that runs on strict, rules based compliance can slide into a culture where the only question anyone asks is, am I allowed to do this? The better question, should I do this, judged by principles and the long run, often goes unasked. A rulebook answers can I. Ethics answers should I.

HEART: The third challenge is a short term focus. Loyalty and the pull of money push people to weigh near term results and their own interest above long term consequences. The bonus arrives this quarter and feels real. The damage to clients, to the firm's name and to your own career is years away and feels abstract, so it gets too little weight.

LEWIS: Next, ethics versus law. They overlap, but they are not the same thing. Picture two yes or no questions. Is it legal? Is it ethical? That gives four combinations. Most daily work is both legal and ethical, and fraud is neither. The two interesting boxes are the mixed ones.

HEART: Legal but unethical. Go back to the forecasting model from the start of this module. No law stops you from quietly accepting sole credit for work you built with a colleague. Most people would still call it wrong, because it takes something your colleague earned.

LEWIS: Illegal but ethical. Picture a lab technician at a food company who finds test results showing that a product already on store shelves is contaminated. Suppose that where she works, giving internal test data to outsiders breaks a secrecy law, but she hands the results to the food safety inspector anyway. Many people would call that ethical whistleblowing, even though it is illegal. Civil disobedience, openly breaking a law you believe is unjust, is the other classic act that many people regard as ethical despite being illegal.

HEART: So not every unethical act is illegal, and not every illegal act is unethical. And in general, ethical principles set a higher bar than laws and regulations do.

LEWIS: Why can the law not carry the whole load? First, laws tend to follow scandals. They are usually written in reaction to bad behavior that has already happened. After the dot com bust, for example, new rules arrived to wall off stock research from investment banking, because some analysts had been talking up companies their own firms wanted banking business from. The rule came after the harm.

HEART: Second, laws are specific, and specific rules leave gaps. A new law can even open the door to new kinds of unethical behavior that its authors never imagined, as people search for whatever the precise wording does not forbid. Ban one kind of fee, and a differently named fee can appear.

LEWIS: Third, ethical decisions demand more judgment. A legal question asks whether the rule allows something. An ethical question asks you to weigh the effect on many stakeholders, now and later. That is why regulation alone is not enough. The law is the floor, and ethics asks for more.

HEART: Last in this module, a framework for ethical decision making. The starting point is the firm. When ethics is part of how a firm reaches its decisions, teams get in the habit of laying out other courses of action and looking at near and far consequences through several sets of eyes, and the decisions improve. The firm's first step is a written code of ethics to anchor that process.

LEWIS: Making ethics routine also lets the firm train people in good decisions, give them practice and keep reinforcing the habit, which is how an ethical culture takes root. And senior management support is essential. If leaders treat the framework as paperwork, so will everyone else.

HEART: For you as an individual, a framework brings the real issues to the surface, makes you look at them from more than one side, builds the judgment that hard calls need, and catches ethical fallout before it can surprise you. It has four steps. Identify. Consider. Decide and act. Reflect.

LEWIS: Step one, identify. Identify the relevant facts, both the facts you have and the facts you wish you had. Identify the stakeholders and the duties you owe each of them, which can include your employer, clients, coworkers, yourself, your family and others in the industry. That naturally brings out the conflicts of interest among them. And identify the ethical principles involved, though those often get clearer as you go.

HEART: Step two, consider. Consider the situational influences pressing on you, and your own personal biases. Seek additional guidance, from a mentor, or from colleagues or friends whose judgment has held up in the past, and from the firm's legal and compliance staff. Outside views give you more angles on the problem and help you come up with options you would not have thought of. Then weigh every alternative action, including its short term and long term effects and any ethical side effects nobody intended.

LEWIS: Step three, decide and act.

HEART: Step four, reflect. Afterward, look at the outcome. Was it what you expected? Why or why not? Did the decision have its intended result, and did you give proper weight to the ethical principles, the situational influences, and your duties to clients and other stakeholders?

LEWIS: Now one story, walked through all four steps. Nora is a portfolio manager at a midsize asset manager, advising individual clients. The firm launches a new in house bond fund, and the head of sales announces a competition. The team that moves the most client money into the fund by quarter end wins a trip and a bonus. Two of Nora's longtime clients, a retired couple, hold an outside bond fund that is cheaper and suits them well.

HEART: Identify. The facts she has: the new fund costs more, the competition rewards moving money regardless of fit, and the couple's current fund works for them. The facts she would like: an honest comparison of the two funds' risks and costs, and whether clients will be told about the competition. The stakeholders: the couple and her other clients, to whom she owes loyalty and care, her employer, which deserves her effort but not at clients' expense, her teammates who want the trip, and herself and her family, who would enjoy the bonus. The conflict is plain. The competition pays her to move money whether or not the move helps the client. The principle at stake is putting clients first.

LEWIS: Consider. The situational influences are strong. Her team is counting on her, the head of sales is senior and popular, and the deadline is weeks away, which is short term pressure in its purest form. She also checks her own bias. Her first instinct is, I would never steer a client wrong, so I can just decide case by case. That is the overconfidence trap. So she asks a mentor who has been through sales pushes before, and she asks compliance.

HEART: Then she lays out her alternatives. Move every client who could plausibly hold the new fund. Move only the clients for whom it is genuinely better, and tell them about the competition. Sit the competition out entirely. Or raise the competition's design with compliance. In the short term, the first option wins the trip. In the long term, it puts at risk her clients' trust, the firm's reputation, and her own name if the moves ever come to light, which is exactly the unintended consequence the framework is built to catch.

LEWIS: Decide and act. She reviews each client on the merits. The couple stays where they are. The one client for whom the new fund is clearly a better fit gets the recommendation, along with a plain mention of the competition. And she tells compliance that the competition rewards moving money regardless of fit.

HEART: Reflect. At quarter end, she compares what happened with what she expected. Compliance changed the competition to reward client outcomes rather than raw sales, which she had hoped for. But her teammates were annoyed she had not warned them sooner, which she had not expected. So her lesson is to bring colleagues in early next time. Later episodes tie a story like this to specific Standards on loyalty to clients and on conflicts of interest.

LEWIS: The takeaway. Ethical conduct balances your own interest against the effect on stakeholders, and the law is only the floor. Overconfidence, situational pressure and a short term focus pull good people off course, and the framework answers with four steps: identify, consider, decide and act, then reflect.

## Module 90.1, Code and Standards

HEART: Reading 89 asked why ethics matters. Reading 90 is about the rules CFA Institute actually holds people to, and how it enforces them. And they apply to you already. The Code and Standards bind every member of CFA Institute, charterholders included, and every candidate in the CFA Program.

LEWIS: Start with enforcement, which runs through the Professional Conduct Program. Two documents govern it, the CFA Institute Bylaws and the Rules of Procedure for professional conduct proceedings. The whole program rests on two principles: a fair process for the member or candidate, and confidentiality of the proceedings.

HEART: Who does what? The Board of Governors holds overall responsibility for the program. Enforcement of the Code and Standards rests with the Disciplinary Review Committee, a volunteer committee of charterholders set up by the Board, working together with the Professional Conduct staff. The staff do the hands on work of looking into possible violations, and that investigation is called an inquiry. When a case is contested, members of the committee hear it and decide.

LEWIS: What can start an inquiry? Five things. First, self disclosure. Members and candidates fill out an annual Professional Conduct Statement, and on it they must disclose whether they are involved in civil litigation or a criminal investigation, or are the subject of a written complaint.

HEART: Second, a written complaint about someone's professional conduct, sent to the Professional Conduct staff by a client, a colleague or anyone else. Third, evidence of misconduct from public sources, such as a news story or a broadcast. Fourth, a report from an exam proctor about a possible violation during the exam. Fifth, CFA Institute's own analysis of exam materials and its monitoring of social media.

LEWIS: So here is the trap. An inquiry does not need anyone to complain. A news report, a proctor's note or a post found online is enough.

HEART: Once an inquiry is open, the staff can ask the member or candidate for a written explanation. They can also interview that person, interview the complainant or other third parties, and gather documents and records.

LEWIS: Then the staff reach one of three conclusions. One, no disciplinary sanction is appropriate. Two, issue a cautionary letter, which is a warning rather than a sanction. Three, find that a violation occurred and propose a disciplinary sanction.

HEART: And the person gets a choice. Accept the proposed sanction, and it takes effect. Reject it, and the case is referred to a disciplinary review panel made up of CFA Institute members, which holds a hearing and decides the matter.

LEWIS: What can a sanction look like? For members, the range runs from a private reprimand, through a public censure, which is a formal rebuke from the profession made public, up to suspending or revoking membership and the right to use the designation. For candidates, it can mean suspension from the CFA Program or being barred from it altogether.

HEART: Notice what is missing. CFA Institute is a professional body, not a court or a government regulator. It does not fine anyone or send anyone to prison. Its power is over membership, the designation and access to its programs.

LEWIS: Here is the whole process in one story. Owen, a charterholder, is named in a lawsuit by a former client, and he reports it on his annual statement, as he must. Staff open an inquiry, ask Owen for a written explanation, interview the client and gather his trading records. They conclude he broke the rules and propose suspending his membership. Owen rejects that. So a hearing panel of members reviews the case and makes the decision.

HEART: Now the Code of Ethics. It has six components, and they are principles rather than detailed rules. Think of the Code as the spirit and the Standards as the letter. Here are the six in plain words.

LEWIS: One. Be honest, capable, hardworking and respectful, and behave ethically toward everyone your work touches, from clients and prospects to bosses, staff, peers and the wider market. The four words to hold onto are integrity, competence, diligence and respect.

HEART: Two. When your personal interest pulls one way, and the integrity of the investment profession or your clients' interests pull the other, your personal interest loses.

LEWIS: Three. Be careful and think for yourself, whether you are analyzing an investment, recommending one, acting on one, or doing any other professional work. The terms are reasonable care and independent professional judgment.

HEART: Four. Set a professional, ethical example and nudge others to follow it, so that how you work brings credit to you and to the profession.

LEWIS: Five. Help keep the global capital markets sound and trustworthy, because in the end society is the one that benefits. The terms are the integrity and viability of the markets.

HEART: Six. Keep building your own professional competence, and try to help other investment professionals build theirs.

LEWIS: Three traps hide in those six. First, component two names the integrity of the profession and the interests of clients. It does not mention your employer. An answer choice that says the Code puts your employer's interests above your own is the distractor.

HEART: Second, the verbs in component six differ. For your own competence, keeping it up and improving it is required. For other professionals, you only strive to help. Third, component five is about markets that serve society, not about maximizing any one client's return.

LEWIS: Now the Standards of Professional Conduct. There are seven of them, each a group with a Roman numeral, and between them they hold twenty three individual Standards. They turn the Code's principles into specific duties. The current version took effect at the start of twenty twenty four, when one new Standard was added and two were revised, and it is the version you will be tested on.

HEART: Here is the order, with the purpose of each, and then a one line picture of every part. Later episodes go deep, so for now the goal is the number, the name and the idea.

LEWIS: Standard one, Professionalism. Its purpose is the baseline of being someone clients can trust: know the rules, keep your judgment your own, tell the truth, deal honestly, and be good at the job. It has five parts.

HEART: Standard one A, Knowledge of the Law. Know and follow the laws and rules that govern your work, and remember that the Code and Standards count among them. When two sets of rules disagree, follow the stricter one. And if you find a violation going on, do not take part or help, and step away from it, which is called dissociating.

LEWIS: Standard one B, Independence and Objectivity. Your opinion has to stay yours. Do not hand out, ask for or take gifts, perks or payments that a reasonable person would expect to bend your judgment, or someone else's.

HEART: Standard one C, Misrepresentation. Never knowingly mislead anyone about your work, whether that is your research, your advice or what you did with a client's money.

LEWIS: Standard one D, Misconduct. Do not lie, cheat or defraud in your professional dealings, and avoid behavior that would make people question your character or your ability as a professional.

HEART: Standard one E, Competence. Have the knowledge and skill your job requires, and keep it up to date. This is the newest Standard, added in twenty twenty four.

LEWIS: Standard two, Integrity of Capital Markets. Its purpose is a fair playing field, where prices reflect real information and nobody rigs the game. Two parts.

HEART: Standard two A, Material Nonpublic Information. If you know something that has not been released to the market and would likely move the price, you cannot act on it, and you cannot get anyone else to act on it. Material means a reasonable investor would want to know it before deciding, or it would probably move the price. Nonpublic means it has not reached the market as a whole, so sharing it with a small group of analysts does not make it public.

LEWIS: Standard two B, Market Manipulation. Do not distort prices or fake trading activity to fool other investors. Talking up a thinly traded stock with false posts online, then selling into the rise, is the picture.

HEART: Standard three, Duties to Clients. Its purpose is simple: the client comes first. Five parts.

LEWIS: Standard three A, Loyalty, Prudence, and Care. Work for your clients' benefit, with the caution a sensible expert would bring, and rank their interests ahead of your employer's and your own. Notice that this Standard does name the employer, and puts it behind the client.

HEART: Standard three B, Fair Dealing. When you publish research, change a recommendation or take investment action, treat every client fairly and objectively. Fair does not have to mean identical, but no client should be quietly shortchanged.

LEWIS: Standard three C, Suitability. When you advise a client, first learn how much investing experience they have, what they need to earn, how much risk they can bear, and what limits their money faces. Refresh that picture regularly, make sure each recommendation suits their situation, and judge it by how it fits the whole portfolio. If instead you run a fund to a stated mandate or style, stay inside that mandate. And do not confuse this Standard with the bare suitability standard from earlier. Standard three A already asks every member and candidate to put clients first, whether or not the law makes them a fiduciary.

HEART: Standard three D, Performance Presentation. When you show investment results, make a genuine effort to keep them fair, accurate and complete. Showing only your best years is the classic failure.

LEWIS: Standard three E, Preservation of Confidentiality. Keep what you know about clients private, including former clients and prospective ones. There are three exceptions: the information concerns illegal activity by the client, the law requires disclosure, or the client agrees to it.

HEART: Standard four, Duties to Employers. Its purpose is to make you a loyal, honest employee without ever trading away the client. Three parts.

LEWIS: Standard four A, Loyalty. At work, act for your employer's benefit. Give it the full value of your skills, keep its secrets, and do not harm it. Quietly copying the client list before you leave for a competitor is the picture.

HEART: Standard four B, Additional Compensation Arrangements. If outside pay or perks could compete with your employer or pull against its interests, get written permission from everyone involved before you accept. A client who offers you a personal bonus for beating a benchmark is the picture.

LEWIS: Standard four C, Responsibilities of Supervisors. If anyone works under your supervision or authority, take reasonable steps to make sure they follow the law, the rules, and the Code and Standards.

HEART: Standard five, Investment Analysis, Recommendations, and Actions. Its purpose is the quality of the work itself and how you explain it. Three parts.

LEWIS: Standard five A, Diligence and Reasonable Basis. Do careful, independent and thorough work, and make sure every recommendation or action rests on research solid enough to justify it.

HEART: Standard five B, Communication with Clients and Prospective Clients. Tell clients what services they are getting and what those services cost them, which is the part added in twenty twenty four. Explain the basic shape and principles of your investment process, and tell them promptly when it changes in a way that matters. Point out the significant limitations and risks. Include the factors that drove your recommendation. And always keep fact and opinion apart.

LEWIS: Standard five C, Record Retention. Keep the records that back up your analysis, your recommendations, your actions, and what you told clients about them. If someone asks why you did something, the file should answer.

HEART: Standard six, Conflicts of Interest. Its purpose is to keep conflicts from secretly bending your work. Three parts.

LEWIS: Standard six A, Avoid or Disclose Conflicts. Anything that could fairly be expected to cloud your judgment, or to pull against what you owe clients, prospects or your employer, should be avoided whenever that is reasonably possible. When it cannot be avoided, disclose it in full, up front and in words a client will actually understand. The twenty twenty four revision added avoidance, and it is the preferred route.

HEART: Standard six B, Priority of Transactions. Clients and your employer trade first. Your own trades, in anything where you are the beneficial owner, wait their turn.

LEWIS: Standard six C, Referral Fees. Whenever money or any other benefit changes hands because a product or service was recommended, whether you are the one paid or the one paying, tell the people who need to know: your employer, your clients and your prospects, as the situation calls for.

HEART: Standard seven, Responsibilities as a CFA Institute Member or CFA Candidate. Its purpose is to protect the program and the designation themselves. Two parts.

LEWIS: Standard seven A, Conduct as Participants in CFA Institute Programs. Do nothing that harms the reputation of CFA Institute or the designation, or that undermines the fairness, credibility or security of its exams and programs. Cheating, or sharing exam questions with others, are the obvious pictures.

HEART: Standard seven B, Reference to CFA Institute, the CFA Designation, and the CFA Program. Whenever you mention membership, the charter or your candidacy, describe it accurately and never inflate what it says about you.

LEWIS: Count them up. Five, two, five, three, three, three and two, which makes twenty three.

HEART: Now practice the real exam skill, matching a story to a Standard. Case one. Mira, an analyst, is at a conference dinner when a company's finance director lets slip that a major contract was just lost and the news goes out next week. Mira texts her brother, and he sells his shares the next morning. That is a violation of Standard two A, Material Nonpublic Information. She did not trade herself, but she caused someone else to act on information the market did not have.

LEWIS: Case two. Theo manages client portfolios. He decides to buy a small company's stock across his clients' accounts, and the morning before he places those orders, he buys some for his personal account. That is a violation of Standard six B, Priority of Transactions. His own trade jumped the line ahead of his clients' trades.

HEART: Case three. Grace passed Level one and is registered for Level two. On her online profile, she writes her name followed by CFA Level two, as if it were a title. That is a violation of Standard seven B. There is no partial designation, so presenting it that way overstates what candidacy means. She could instead say she is a Level two candidate in the CFA Program.

LEWIS: Case four. Ruth's longtime client sends her a pair of concert tickets to thank her for a good year. She tells her employer about the gift. No violation. A thank you from a client for work already done does not threaten her independence the way a gift from someone seeking influence would, and she disclosed it.

HEART: Now the exam traps for this module. First, do not mix up the Code and the Standards. The Code has six components and states principles. The Standards have seven groups and state specific duties. An answer that swaps those numbers is wrong.

LEWIS: Second, the enforcement roles. Staff investigate and propose. If the member or candidate rejects the proposed sanction, a hearing panel of members takes the case. Third, an inquiry can start without any complaint. Fourth, a cautionary letter is not a sanction. Fifth, know the current titles. Competence is Standard one E, Standard six A is Avoid or Disclose Conflicts, and Standard five B now includes disclosing costs.

HEART: The takeaway. The Code gives six principles, and the Standards give seven groups with twenty three specific duties. Staff investigate and propose, a hearing panel of members steps in when a proposed sanction is rejected, and the sanctions reach membership, the designation and the program, never a fine.

## Recall check

LEWIS Q: Does acting ethically mean setting your own interests aside completely?

HEART: Answer. No, it means balancing your own interest against the effect on others. Here is why. Ethical conduct is judged by its effect on stakeholders, the people your actions touch directly or indirectly. Pursuing a fee or a promotion is fine. What makes a choice unethical is gaining at a stakeholder's unfair expense, like taking credit for a colleague's work.

HEART Q: Why does the intangible nature of investment management make ethics more important than in many other businesses?

LEWIS: Answer. Because clients cannot easily judge the quality or value of what they receive, so they have to rely on trust. Here is why. A physical product can be inspected and tested. Investment results are noisy, and fees and conflicts are hard to see, so a client often cannot tell good advice from bad luck or bad faith. When the product cannot be checked, the professional's integrity is most of what the client is buying.

LEWIS Q: Two funds both fit a client's risk tolerance and return needs, but one pays the adviser more. Which standard allows recommending either fund, and which standard is stronger?

HEART: Answer. A suitability standard can allow either, and a fiduciary standard is the stronger one. Here is why. Suitability only asks whether the investment's characteristics match the client's return requirements and risk tolerance, and both funds do. A fiduciary standard requires acting in the client's best interest, so choosing the costlier fund because it pays the adviser more fails that test.

HEART Q: A manager says, I am an honest person, so I do not need to run this decision past anyone. Which challenge to ethical behavior is that?

LEWIS: Answer. Overconfidence in one's own ethics. Here is why. People tend to rate their own ethical behavior above other people's and to give too much credit to their own character. That belief makes them skip outside checks and ignore the situational pressures actually shaping the choice, which is where good people most often go wrong.

LEWIS Q: How can a strict, rules based compliance program end up weakening ethical behavior?

HEART: Answer. It can train people to ask only what they are allowed to do, not what they should do. Here is why. When the culture centers on the rulebook, anything the rules do not forbid starts to feel acceptable. The question of what is right, based on principles and long term consequences, drops out. Rules answer can I, while ethics answers should I.

HEART Q: Can an action be illegal and still be considered ethical?

LEWIS: Answer. Yes. Here is why. Laws and ethics overlap, but they are not the same. Civil disobedience against a law seen as unjust, or whistleblowing that breaks a secrecy rule to expose wrongdoing, may be illegal yet viewed by many as ethical. The reverse holds too, since plenty of unethical behavior breaks no law.

LEWIS Q: Why is regulation alone not enough to ensure ethical conduct?

HEART: Answer. Because laws lag behind misconduct, leave gaps, and call for less judgment than ethics does. Here is why. New laws usually respond to scandals that already happened. Because they are specific, they can open the door to new forms of misconduct their authors did not foresee. And a legal test asks only whether a rule allows something, while an ethical decision weighs the effect on many stakeholders over time.

HEART Q: In the ethical decision making framework, which step includes weighing situational influences, seeking guidance and comparing alternative actions?

LEWIS: Answer. Consider, the second step. Here is why. Identify comes first and gathers the facts, the stakeholders and duties owed, the ethical principles and the conflicts of interest. Consider then looks at the pressures and personal biases at work, brings in outside views from a mentor or compliance, and weighs each alternative's short and long term effects. Only then do you decide and act.

LEWIS Q: What does the reflect step ask?

HEART: Answer. Whether the outcome matched what you expected, and why or why not. Here is why. Reflection checks whether the decision had its intended results and whether you gave proper weight to the ethical principles, the situational influences and your duties to stakeholders. A surprise usually means you missed a stakeholder or underrated a pressure, and spotting that improves the next decision.

HEART Q: Does the Professional Conduct staff need a complaint before it can open an inquiry?

LEWIS: Answer. No. Here is why. A written complaint is only one of five triggers. An inquiry can also start from self disclosure on the annual Professional Conduct Statement, from public sources such as the news, from an exam proctor's report, or from CFA Institute's own analysis of exam materials and monitoring of social media.

LEWIS Q: Staff find a violation and propose a sanction, and the member rejects it. What happens next?

HEART: Answer. The case is referred to a disciplinary review panel of CFA Institute members for a hearing. Here is why. Staff can investigate and propose, but they do not get the final word over the member's objection. That right to a hearing before a panel of peers, who decide the matter, is part of the fair process the program promises to members and candidates.

HEART Q: The Code of Ethics says to place two things above your own personal interests. What are they, and is your employer one of them?

LEWIS: Answer. The integrity of the investment profession, and your clients' interests. No, the employer is not named. Here is why. That part of the Code is about the profession and the people it serves. The employer does appear in Standard three A, Loyalty, Prudence, and Care, but only to say the client comes before the employer. So any answer that says the Code puts your employer first is wrong.

## Key takeaways

HEART: First, a few memory hooks.

LEWIS: For what ethics is: balance, not sacrifice. Weigh your own interest against the effect on every stakeholder.

HEART: For the two levels of duty: suitable means it fits, fiduciary means the client's best interest comes first.

LEWIS: For the three challenges: me, them and now. Overconfidence about me, situational pressure from them, and the pull of now over later. And a rulebook answers can I, while ethics answers should I.

HEART: For law versus ethics: the law is the floor, and it usually arrives after the damage. Not every unethical act is illegal, and not every illegal act is unethical.

LEWIS: For the framework: I Consider Doing Right. Identify, consider, decide and act, reflect. Facts, stakeholders, duties, principles and conflicts belong to identify. Influences, guidance and alternatives belong to consider.

HEART: For what starts an inquiry, five sources: self disclosure, a written complaint, the news, a proctor, and CFA Institute's own eyes on exam materials and social media. Any one of them is enough.

LEWIS: For enforcement: staff investigate and propose. Accept, and it takes effect. Reject, and a panel of members hears the case. A cautionary letter is a warning, not a sanction, and there are no fines.

HEART: For the Code: six principles, the spirit. The Standards: seven groups, the letter. The Code puts the profession and clients above yourself, and the employer is not in that line.

LEWIS: For the order of the seven Standards, tell it as a story: me, the market, my clients, my employer, my work, my conflicts, and the CFA name. That is Professionalism, then Integrity of Capital Markets, then Duties to Clients, then Duties to Employers, then Investment Analysis, Recommendations, and Actions, then Conflicts of Interest, and last, Responsibilities as a CFA Institute Member or CFA Candidate.

HEART: For the counts, read them like a phone number: five two five, three three three, two. Twenty three in all.

LEWIS: And for the twenty twenty four changes: competence arrived, costs get disclosed, and conflicts get avoided where possible. That is Standard one E, Standard five B and Standard six A.

HEART: Now, how you would say the big ideas to a senior leader.

LEWIS: On why ethics pays: when investors suspect they are being misled, they invest less, demand higher returns, and money flows to the wrong businesses. The jargon is stakeholders, the cost of capital and the misallocation of capital.

HEART: On the law: being legal is the minimum. Rules arrive after the damage, so the real question is whether we should, not whether we can. The jargon is ethical versus legal standards and a rules based compliance culture.

LEWIS: On hard calls: before deciding, list everyone affected and every conflict, get outside views, compare the options, then check the result afterward. The jargon is the ethical decision making framework: identify, consider, decide and act, reflect.

HEART: On advice: suitable means the product fits the client. Fiduciary means the client's best interest wins, even when it pays us less. The jargon is the suitability standard versus the fiduciary standard.

LEWIS: And on the CFA rules: six principles set the tone, seven groups of rules spell out the duties, and enforcement can take the designation away. The jargon is the Code of Ethics, the Standards of Professional Conduct and the Professional Conduct Program.
