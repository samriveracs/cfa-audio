LEWIS: This episode covers Standard one, Professionalism, in its five parts: knowing and following the law, keeping your judgment independent, never misrepresenting your work, avoiding misconduct, and keeping up your competence. For each part you will hear what it requires, why it exists, the procedures that prevent violations, the classic traps, and short cases that each end in a verdict.

## Module 91.1, Guidance for Standards I(A) and I(B)

HEART: Ethics questions on this exam are short stories. Someone does something, and you decide whether it breaks a Standard, which one, or what the person should do next. Ethics, together with the short reading on the Global Investment Performance Standards, carries roughly fifteen to twenty percent of the exam, and nearly all of it comes down to applying a small set of rules to situations like these.

LEWIS: The Standards of Professional Conduct come in seven groups, Standard one through Standard seven, and between them they hold twenty three subsections, each one an individual Standard. They bind every CFA Institute member and every candidate in the program, so they apply to you now, and every person in this episode's cases is one of them. Standard one, Professionalism, holds five of the subsections, and this episode covers all five. Standard one A, Knowledge of the Law. Standard one B, Independence and Objectivity. Standard one C, Misrepresentation. Standard one D, Misconduct. And Standard one E, Competence.

HEART: Three habits will carry you through these questions. First, many Standards turn on judgment words, such as reasonable or token, that come with no exact line. Exam stories are built so the facts land well clear of that line, on one side or the other. When you hear lavish, luxury, first class or all expenses paid, the story is telling you the line was crossed.

LEWIS: Second, the codes matter less than the ideas. You will seldom need to recall that a rule is one B rather than six A. What you do need is to spot the duty at stake and match it to the right Standard by name. And one act can break more than one Standard at the same time.

HEART: Third, and this is the big one, watch for the excuse built into the story. My boss told me to. The client agreed. It is legal here. Everyone does it. It was a small amount. Almost always, the excuse changes nothing. When the act itself is off limits, a sympathetic reason for doing it is no defense. The exception is a Standard where intent is part of the test, such as knowingly misleading someone. There, what the person knew does matter.

LEWIS: Standard one A, Knowledge of the Law. In plain words, it asks three things. Know and obey every law, rule and regulation that governs your professional work, whether it comes from a government, a regulator, a licensing body or a professional association. When two of those rules disagree, follow the stricter one. And never knowingly take part in or help with a violation, and if you find yourself connected to one, step away from it. That stepping away is called dissociation.

HEART: Notice one item hiding in that list. The Code and Standards themselves count as one of the rule sets you must obey. So, technically, a violation of any other Standard is also a violation of Standard one A. That is why it sits first, like an umbrella over everything that follows. On the exam, though, the answer wanted is usually the specific Standard that was broken.

LEWIS: Why does the stricter rule principle exist? Picture two neighboring countries. One bans a practice, and the other has no rule about it at all. If professionals only had to follow local law, anyone could move the questionable business across the border and carry on. Laws also lag behind new products, and they differ widely from place to place. The Code and Standards set a floor that travels with you wherever you work, and any law that is tougher than the Code raises that floor.

HEART: You can answer to three rulebooks at once: the Code and Standards, the law of the country you live in, and the law of the country where the business happens. Run your action past all three. If it passes every one, you are fine, which in practice means the toughest of the three decides.

LEWIS: So there are three outcomes to keep straight. If the law is looser than the Code, follow the Code. If the law is tougher than the Code, follow the law. And if there is no relevant law at all, the Code still applies in full.

HEART: A case for the first outcome. Rafael manages money in a country whose securities law says nothing about trading ahead of clients. He learns that his firm is about to buy a large block of a thinly traded stock for client accounts, and he buys some for himself first, knowing the client orders will push the price up. Is that a violation?

LEWIS: Yes. Local law was silent, but the Code and Standards require client trades to come before your own, so the Code was the stricter rule and it governed. You will meet that rule properly later as Standard six B, Priority of Transactions. For now, the point is that legal does not mean allowed.

HEART: Now the mirror image. Paula works where the regulator bars investment advisers from accepting any meal or entertainment from a broker, however modest. The Code would tolerate an ordinary business dinner. A broker invites her to one, and she goes, reasoning that the Code allows it. Violation?

LEWIS: Yes again. This time the local rule was stricter, so the local rule governed. You never get to pick the looser of the two, in either direction.

HEART: How much law are you expected to know? Not every law in existence. You must understand the rules that relate to your own responsibilities, and the depth grows with your role. A junior analyst needs the rules that touch research. Someone who supervises many functions needs much broader knowledge. And when you are unsure whether something is legal, ask a supervisor, the compliance department or a lawyer. Getting advice is sensible, but advice never takes away your own duty to comply.

LEWIS: Now dissociation, the part of Standard one A that generates the most questions. Suppose you discover that colleagues, or a client, are breaking the rules. The expected response comes in steps.

HEART: Step one, try to get it fixed. Raise it with your supervisor or the compliance department. Step two, if they will not or cannot fix it, step away. Stop working on the matter, ask for your name to come off the report or the pitch book, decline to pass the material along, ask for a different assignment, or end your work for that client. Step three, only in an extreme case where you cannot otherwise get clear of it, you may have to leave the firm.

LEWIS: Throughout, keep written notes of what you saw and what you did about it, and keep encouraging the firm to put an end to the problem. Those notes protect you, and they make clear you were not part of it.

HEART: Here is the idea underneath. Silence is not neutral. If you know about a violation and simply carry on working alongside it, you can be treated as having taken part. Knowingly is the key word. You are not responsible for wrongdoing you had no way to see. But once you know, or plainly should have known, the clock starts.

LEWIS: A case. Kenji works in operations at an asset manager. He notices that his team has been skipping the identity checks the law requires before opening new accounts, to speed things up for the sales team. He tells his manager, who shrugs and says the deadline matters more. Kenji keeps processing the accounts. Violation?

HEART: Yes. He knew the process broke the law, his first step failed, and he kept helping. The right path was to go to compliance next, stop processing accounts that skip the checks, and keep notes of what happened. If the firm still would not fix it, leaving could become the right call.

LEWIS: Now, what Kenji was not required to do. The Standards do not require you to report wrongdoers to a government regulator. Local law sometimes does, and then you must. Members and candidates are strongly encouraged to report violations of the Code by other members and candidates to the CFA Institute Professional Conduct Program, but encouraged is not the same as required.

HEART: The recommended procedures for Standard one A are practical. For members, keep up with rule changes that touch your work, and push your firm to have a system that tells everyone when the rules move. Go back over the firm's compliance manual from time to time, or press the firm to do it. Make sure up to date copies of the rules that matter are on hand. Ask for help when in doubt. And when you dissociate, write down what went wrong and keep pressing your employer to stop it.

LEWIS: For firms, put a code of ethics in place, make sure staff know which laws and rules apply to their jobs, and give people a clear channel for flagging suspected violations.

HEART: Now the traps for Standard one A. Trap one is the answer that says it was legal locally, so there is no problem. If the Code is stricter, local law is not enough. Trap two is the answer that says the member must immediately report to the regulator. Unless the story says the law requires a report, the expected path is to raise it internally and then dissociate.

LEWIS: Trap three is the answer that says resign at once. Resignation is the last resort, after trying to get it fixed and after trying to step away. Trap four is the answer that treats doing nothing as staying neutral. Staying silent while you keep working on the problem counts as taking part.

HEART: The takeaway for Standard one A. Follow the stricter of the law and the Code. Never knowingly take part in a violation. If you are connected to one, speak up, then step away, and leave only in an extreme case.

LEWIS: Standard one B, Independence and Objectivity. In plain words, it asks two things. First, take reasonable care to keep your professional judgment unbiased and your own, and keep it that way over time. Second, never offer, ask for or accept anything of value, whether a present, a perk, a payment or a favor, if it could reasonably be expected to bend your judgment or someone else's.

HEART: Two details in that. The test is what a reasonable outsider would expect, not how you feel. Saying the gift did not influence you does not answer the question. And the ban runs three ways. Offering a compromising benefit to someone else is a violation, just as asking for one or accepting one is.

LEWIS: Why does it exist? A client pays for an honest opinion. If that opinion can be steered by whoever offers the nicest perk, the client is paying for an advertisement. The pressure takes familiar forms. Someone wants a stock rated higher, or a particular fund manager or supplier chosen, or a report tilted kinder or harsher than the evidence supports. Letting any of that pressure shape your analysis is a violation.

HEART: Start with gifts and entertainment. Ordinary business entertainment is fine, such as a working lunch or a modest dinner with a broker you already deal with. What crosses the line is anything that could reasonably be expected to influence you. The Standards give no dollar figure for a token gift. Firms are encouraged to set their own limit, which makes the rule easy to apply day to day. On the exam, the story will tell you which side of the line you are on.

LEWIS: The source of the gift matters a great deal. A gift from a client is treated as less likely to compromise you than a gift from anyone else. A client thanking you for good service wants more of the same, so the gift pulls in the same direction as your duty to that client. The real risk is favoritism, where a generous client gets better treatment than other clients. So the rule is disclosure. Tell your employer about a client gift before you accept it if you can, and promptly afterward if you cannot.

HEART: Gifts from everyone else, such as brokers, companies you cover, or managers who want your business, should be limited to token items. Those are the givers most likely to want something back.

LEWIS: A case. Marcus is a wealth adviser. After a strong year, a long standing client who owns a small vineyard sends him a few bottles of her wine. Marcus emails his compliance officer before accepting, and then keeps them. Violation?

HEART: No. It is a modest client gift, and he told his employer before accepting it. If the wine had arrived while he was away, telling his employer promptly once he found it would also satisfy the Standard.

LEWIS: Compare that with Elena, a buy side analyst at a pension fund that is choosing which brokers will receive its trading business. One competing broker offers Elena and her husband a long weekend at a luxury golf resort abroad, with business class flights and every expense covered. She accepts, and she tells her supervisor about it. Violation?

HEART: Yes. The benefit is lavish, it comes from a party that wants something from her, and it could reasonably be expected to affect her judgment about which brokers to choose. Telling her supervisor does not cure it. Disclosure is the rule for client gifts. It is not a permission slip for lavish gifts from people seeking influence.

LEWIS: Now the offering side. Tomas sells a fund to pension plans. He tells a consultant who advises plans on which managers to hire that his firm will make a generous donation to the consultant's favorite charity if the consultant recommends the fund. Violation?

HEART: Yes, by Tomas. He offered a benefit designed to compromise someone else's objectivity. A donation to a charity is still something of value when it is meant to buy a recommendation. Gifts or political contributions aimed at winning a mandate are often called pay to play, and they are a violation whether you are the one offering or the one asking.

LEWIS: Next, travel. When you visit a company or tour a property, the best practice is for you or your firm to pay for your own travel and hotel, and to ride on the company's own aircraft only when commercial travel is not a realistic alternative.

HEART: A case. Iris covers renewable energy companies. One company invites analysts to see a wind farm it has just built on a remote island that no airline serves. The company flies the group out on its own small plane, and her firm pays for her room at the island's only guesthouse. Violation?

LEWIS: No. Commercial travel was not a real option, and the arrangement was modest and tied to the research. Had the company instead offered her a first class flight to a major city with plenty of commercial service, plus a spa weekend, that would be a violation.

HEART: Now pressure on research. Sell side analysts work at firms that also earn large fees from investment banking, and a negative rating can cost the bank a client. Companies can also lean on analysts directly, for example by cutting off access to management after a critical report. The duty is the same either way. The rating follows the evidence.

LEWIS: A case. Wen is a sell side analyst with a sell rating on a retailer. After the report, the retailer's chief executive phones her boss to complain and tells reporters that her work is careless. Nothing in the company's results has changed. Wen keeps the rating and keeps covering the company. Violation?

HEART: No. That is exactly what the Standard asks. Had she raised the rating to make peace with the company, with no new evidence, that would be a violation. And if her own firm ever refused to publish a negative view of a banking client, the better course is for the firm to put the company on a restricted list and limit what it says about the company to facts, rather than publish an opinion nobody actually holds.

LEWIS: Then issuer paid research. Sometimes a company pays an independent analyst to write research on it, often because no large firm follows the stock. That is allowed, under two conditions. The pay must be a flat fee agreed in advance, not tied to the report's conclusions, its recommendation or its effect on the share price. And the report must disclose that the company paid for it.

HEART: A case. Andre runs a small independent research shop. A young biotech company hires him to write a report for a flat fee agreed up front, and the report states clearly who paid and how. Violation?

LEWIS: No. Now change one fact. The contract adds a bonus if the share price climbs after the report comes out. That is a violation, because his pay now depends on the report's market impact, which rewards a favorable conclusion. Leaving out the disclosure would be a violation on its own, too.

HEART: One more item that surprises people. An IPO, an initial public offering, is oversubscribed when investors ask for more shares than are on offer. Putting shares of an oversubscribed IPO into your own personal account is a violation. Those shares are scarce and valuable, handing them out is a classic way to win favor, and taking them for yourself puts your interest ahead of clients who wanted them.

LEWIS: A case. Sofia is a portfolio manager. A broker her firm trades with calls to say a much anticipated technology IPO is heavily oversubscribed, but he can find a few hundred shares for her personal account. She takes them. Violation?

HEART: Yes. It is a valuable benefit from a party that wants her firm's business, and allocating oversubscribed IPO shares to a personal account is treated as a violation. Firms guard against this by restricting employee participation in IPOs and private placements and requiring approval in advance.

LEWIS: The recommended procedures for Standard one B, briefly. Members should fund their own trips to see companies wherever they reasonably can, and treat a company plane as the exception, for trips where commercial travel is not a real option. Firms should limit how far staff can take part in IPOs and private placements and make them get approval first. They should name a compliance officer, put their independence rules in writing along with a clear way to report breaches, and cap gifts from anyone other than clients at token value.

HEART: The traps for Standard one B. Trap one is believing disclosure fixes everything. Disclosure is the rule for client gifts, but it does not make a lavish gift from someone seeking influence acceptable. Trap two is believing every gift is banned. Ordinary entertainment and token gifts are fine, and client gifts are fine with disclosure. Trap three is assuming issuer paid research is forbidden. It is allowed with a flat fee and disclosure. Trap four is forgetting the offering side. Trying to buy someone else's judgment is a violation too.

LEWIS: The takeaway for Standard one B. Do not offer, ask for or accept anything that could reasonably be expected to compromise judgment. Client gifts are fine with disclosure to your employer. Everyone else gets token items at most, and the opinion always follows the evidence.

## Module 91.2, Guidance for Standards I(C), I(D), and I(E)

HEART: Standard one C, Misrepresentation. In plain words, never knowingly give anyone a false picture of your investment analysis, your recommendations, the actions you take, or any other part of your professional work. A false picture can come from an untrue statement, from leaving out a fact that matters, from showing only the data that flatter you, or from passing off someone else's work as your own.

LEWIS: Why does it exist? Clients make decisions based on what you tell them. A false statement, a missing fact or a borrowed idea corrupts that decision in the same way. And it does not matter where it appears, whether in a research report, a pitch book, a phone call, an email or a social media post.

HEART: The ways it happens fall into a few families. The first is claims about yourself and your firm, meaning overstated qualifications, experience, or services your firm cannot actually deliver.

LEWIS: A case. Ingrid writes the text for her firm's website, which says a team of fifteen analysts covers markets around the world. In fact the firm has four analysts, and two of them follow only domestic stocks. Violation?

HEART: Yes. That is false information about the firm's capabilities and expertise, and she knew it when she wrote it.

LEWIS: The second family is guarantees. You cannot promise a specific return on an investment that is inherently risky. A guarantee is acceptable only when one really exists, made explicitly by a government body or a financial institution, and you describe it accurately.

HEART: A case. Bella tells a retired client that a dividend stock fund will pay six percent a year, guaranteed. Violation?

LEWIS: Yes. Nobody stands behind that return, so she guaranteed the outcome of a risky investment. Compare that with explaining that a certificate of deposit is protected by government deposit insurance up to the legal limit, or that a structured note's principal is explicitly guaranteed by the bank that issued it. Those describe real guarantees accurately, so they are not violations.

HEART: The third family is selective data and omissions. Leaving out a fact can mislead as surely as stating a false one. Showing performance that quietly drops some accounts, or an attribution analysis that leaves out a variable that matters, is a misrepresentation.

LEWIS: Two quieter versions of the same idea. One is choosing a benchmark that does not match the strategy. A case. Greta runs a fund of small emerging market companies. In a year when large domestic stocks did badly, she compares her fund with a large company domestic index and calls the result a big outperformance. Violation?

HEART: Yes. The benchmark is not comparable to the strategy, so the comparison gives a false picture of skill. The other quiet version is shopping for valuations. A case. Oskar runs a fund that holds loans which never trade. Three pricing services quote values for them, and Oskar picks the service that gives the highest values, because it lifts his reported performance. Violation?

LEWIS: Yes. Choosing a valuation service because it puts the highest value on untraded holdings misrepresents what the portfolio is worth. The choice of service has to rest on quality and method, not on which answer flatters you.

HEART: A link to balance sheet work. In a bank or credit union, an interest rate risk report that shows the board only the friendly rate scenarios and quietly drops the painful one is the same kind of misleading omission.

LEWIS: The fourth family is plagiarism. Plagiarism means taking someone else's ideas, reports, forecasts or models, or even their charts and spreadsheets, without giving them credit. Putting it into your own words does not fix it. A paraphrase of another analyst's argument still needs credit.

HEART: A case. Callum hears a strategist at an industry conference lay out an original argument for why shipping rates will fall. The next week, Callum writes that argument up in his own words in a client note, with no mention of where it came from. Violation?

LEWIS: Yes. The idea belonged to someone else, and rewording does not change that. One line crediting the strategist would have made it fine.

HEART: There are two sensible exceptions. First, you do not need to credit projections, statistics and tables taken from recognized financial and statistical reporting services. If Callum drops a standard table of bond yields from a widely known financial data service into his note, no credit is required, though citing it is still a good habit.

LEWIS: Second, research and models built inside your own firm. When a colleague who built a model has since left, you may leave that person's name off as the firm keeps relying on the model, as long as you do not claim the work as yours alone.

HEART: A case. Hugo's team still runs a valuation model built by a colleague who left two years ago. Their reports describe it as the team's model, without naming him. Violation?

LEWIS: No. The firm's work stays the firm's work, and nobody is claiming to have built it single handed. If Hugo told a client he had designed the model himself, that would be a violation.

HEART: The last family is passing off other people's work. Presenting research written by an outside firm as your own, without saying where it came from, is a violation. So is handing clients marketing material from an outside adviser, such as a manager you hire, when you know or should know it is misleading.

LEWIS: One more point. The Standard says knowingly. An honest mistake, such as a brochure that still shows last year's fee schedule by accident, is not a knowing misrepresentation. But once you find it, fix it promptly and stop handing it out. Keep using it after you know, and it becomes knowing.

HEART: The recommended procedures for Standard one C. Keep a short written profile of your own background, credentials and what you can actually do for clients, and hand clients a written list of what the firm offers and who is qualified to deliver it. Push your firm to check any marketing that outside managers or vendors supply about themselves before it reaches clients. Name your source whenever you summarize another person's work. Hold on to everything you drew on when you built a research report. And have the firm look back over its communications from time to time for claims that oversell its people or itself.

LEWIS: The traps for Standard one C. Trap one says paraphrasing is not plagiarism. It is. Trap two says any use of the word guarantee is a violation. It is not, when a real guarantee from a government body or financial institution exists and you describe it accurately. Trap three says an honest typo is automatically a violation. It is not, if it was not knowing and you correct it promptly. Trap four says data from a recognized statistical service always needs a citation. It does not.

HEART: The takeaway for Standard one C. Never knowingly say anything false, never leave out what changes the picture, and give credit for every borrowed idea, except standard data from recognized services.

LEWIS: Standard one D, Misconduct. It has two parts. First, your professional dealings must be free of dishonesty, fraud and deceit. Second, you must not do anything that damages your standing as a professional, meaning your reputation, your integrity or your competence.

HEART: Why does it exist? An investment professional handles other people's money and private information. Dishonesty in professional life tells clients they cannot rely on you, and conduct that damages your professional standing chips away at the trust the whole profession depends on.

LEWIS: The first part is clear cut. A case. Felipe takes his family to the coast for a weekend and submits the hotel bill as research travel for a client account. The amount is small. Violation?

HEART: Yes. That is dishonesty in professional conduct, and the small amount does not matter. Notice that no criminal charge is needed. Misconduct is about dishonesty, not about whether anyone is ever prosecuted.

LEWIS: The second part needs more care. It is not limited to what happens at your desk. It can reach conduct away from the office, but only when the act reflects on you as a professional, and that takes a real link to your professional life. Think of conduct tied to the investment profession or the markets, to your workplace or to teaching, or anything where you lean on your CFA designation, your membership or your candidacy. Without that link, even a dishonest act in your private life falls outside this Standard, however wrong it is, and even if it breaks the law.

HEART: A case. On weekday evenings, Yara teaches a personal finance class at a community college, and she introduces herself as a candidate in the CFA Program. To impress the students, she makes up stellar returns from her own investing. Violation?

LEWIS: Yes. The class is about investing, she leans on her candidacy for credibility, and she lies. That dishonesty reflects directly on her professional integrity, even though it happens outside her day job. It is also a misrepresentation, which is a good reminder that one act can break more than one Standard.

HEART: Now the other side. Nils gets into a shouting match with his landlord over the return of his rental deposit, and it ends up in small claims court. Nothing about it touches his job, the markets or his candidacy. Violation?

LEWIS: No. It is purely personal conduct with no link to his professional life. Others may disapprove, but the Standard is not a general code of private behavior. The same logic applies to money trouble. A personal bankruptcy caused by medical bills does not, by itself, say anything about integrity. If it grew out of fraudulent or deceitful business dealings, the answer could change.

HEART: On the competence side of Misconduct, the classic pattern is behavior that impairs your work, even when nothing illegal is involved. Someone who keeps turning up to work unfit to do the job, so that clients and colleagues cannot rely on them, damages their professional reputation and competence. That is a violation.

LEWIS: One more point. The Standard is not a weapon. Using a conduct complaint to pursue a private grudge, a political quarrel or any other fight that has nothing to do with professional ethics is itself an abuse of the process. A case. Two analysts argue bitterly about politics online. One files a complaint with the CFA Institute Professional Conduct Program claiming the other's political posts are misconduct. Has the poster violated the Standard?

HEART: No. Political views unrelated to professional ethics are not misconduct. The improper act here is the complaint itself, an attempt to turn the conduct process into a weapon in a personal fight.

LEWIS: The recommended procedures for Standard one D sit mostly with firms. Adopt a code of ethics and leave no doubt that unethical behavior carries consequences. Spell out for employees which acts count as violations and what happens for each, with dismissal at the top of the scale. And check references before anyone is hired.

HEART: The trap here runs in both directions. Not every arrest, lawsuit or embarrassing moment is misconduct. And professional dishonesty does not need a conviction to be a violation. Ask two questions. Does the act have a link to your professional life? And if it does, does it involve dishonesty, or damage your reputation, integrity or competence?

LEWIS: The takeaway for Standard one D. No dishonesty, fraud or deceit in professional dealings, and nothing that damages your professional reputation, integrity or competence. Purely personal matters with no professional link fall outside it.

HEART: Standard one E, Competence. In plain words, have the competence your job demands when you do the work, and keep it up as the job and the markets change.

LEWIS: Competence has three parts. Knowledge is what you know. Skills are your ability to carry out the specific tasks your role involves. Abilities are the broader capacities that turn knowledge and skills into good work. What counts as enough depends on the role. A pension fund's chief investment officer needs a different mix from a junior credit analyst.

HEART: Why does it exist? A client usually cannot judge whether you understand the product you recommend. They rely on you to know. Competence is the promise underneath every other piece of advice.

LEWIS: Three features matter for the exam. First, competence is not a one time achievement. It has to be maintained. Second, a new or unfamiliar role puts the burden on you to get up to speed on what it demands, before clients or colleagues depend on it. A promotion that adds supervising other people counts as a new role too. Third, a bad outcome does not by itself prove a lack of competence. Competent people still make calls that go wrong.

HEART: A case. Mateo has spent years as an equity analyst. He is moved onto a team that manages mortgage backed securities, and within days he starts buying them for clients without learning how prepayments change their cash flows. Violation?

LEWIS: Yes. He acted for clients without the competence the new role required. Experience in one area does not carry over automatically to another.

HEART: The contrasting case. Lena is moving from retail banking into a team that hedges interest rate risk with derivatives. Before she approves a single hedge, she completes a course on derivatives, studies the firm's hedging policy, and works alongside a senior colleague for a quarter. Violation?

LEWIS: No. She built the competence before she relied on it, which is exactly what the Standard asks. The same applies at a bank or credit union when someone takes over the interest rate risk model for the first time. Learn it first, then sign off on it.

HEART: Now a bad outcome. Victor researches a food company thoroughly, visits its plants and recommends the stock. Months later, a flood shuts its largest factory and the share price drops sharply. Violation?

LEWIS: No. The loss came from a sudden event, not from a gap in what he knew or how he worked. The Standard judges whether you had the knowledge and skills the role requires and applied them, not whether the result was good.

HEART: And continuing education. The Standard recommends it but does not require it, and it does not set a number of hours or a specific program. A case. Maya has managed the same bond portfolios for years. She has not taken a formal course in a while, but she follows regulatory updates and the research in her field closely and attends her firm's internal briefings. Violation?

LEWIS: No. She is keeping her competence current through self study and employer training. Formal continuing education is a recommendation, not a requirement.

HEART: The recommended ways to build and keep competence are what you would expect: formal courses and training from your firm, further professional designations, conferences in your field, an active role in professional bodies, and informal self study.

LEWIS: Two traps. First, a credential alone does not prove competence. Passing an exam does not satisfy the Standard if you never actually learned what your job requires. Second, keep Competence apart from Misconduct. The word competence appears in both. Turning up unfit to work is the Misconduct angle. Failing to learn what your role requires is the Competence angle.

HEART: And a look ahead. You will later meet a Standard on diligence and a reasonable basis for each recommendation. Competence asks whether you have the knowledge and skills your role requires. That later Standard asks whether you did the work behind a specific recommendation.

LEWIS: The takeaway for Standard one E. Act with, and keep up, the competence your role requires. Build it before you take on something new, and never mistake a bad outcome for a lack of competence.

## Recall check

LEWIS Q: An analyst works where local law allows a practice that the Code and Standards prohibit, and she follows local law. Is that a violation, and of what?

HEART: Answer. Yes, it violates Standard one A, Knowledge of the Law. Here is why. When the law and the Code disagree, a member follows the stricter rule, and here the Code is stricter. Legal does not mean allowed. Had local law been the stricter one, she would have to follow local law instead.

HEART Q: A junior analyst finds that a report he helps prepare overstates how easily a fund's holdings can be sold. His supervisor will not fix it, and neither will compliance. Should he report the firm to the regulator, resign, or remove himself from the report?

LEWIS: Answer. He should remove himself from the report and keep notes of what happened. Here is why. Once raising it internally has failed, Standard one A requires him to dissociate, which means stopping work on it and getting his name off it. The Standards do not require a report to a regulator unless the law does. Resignation is reserved for an extreme case where he cannot otherwise get clear of the problem.

LEWIS Q: A portfolio manager receives a gift basket of fruit from a long time client after a good year, and tells her employer the next day. Is that a violation?

HEART: Answer. No. Here is why. Gifts from clients are treated as less likely to compromise independence, because the gift rewards the very service she owes that client. The rule is disclosure to the employer, before accepting if possible and promptly afterward if not. A modest client gift, disclosed, satisfies Standard one B.

HEART Q: A buy side analyst accepts a luxury cruise from a broker competing for her firm's trades, and tells her supervisor about it. Is that a violation, and of what?

LEWIS: Answer. Yes, it violates Standard one B, Independence and Objectivity. Here is why. The benefit is lavish and comes from a party seeking her firm's business, so a reasonable person would expect it to affect her judgment. Disclosure is the rule for client gifts, but it does not make a lavish gift from someone seeking influence acceptable.

LEWIS Q: An independent analyst is paid by the company she covers. Her fee is flat and agreed in advance, but the report never mentions who paid. Is that a violation?

HEART: Answer. Yes, it violates Standard one B. Here is why. Issuer paid research is allowed only when two conditions both hold, a flat fee not tied to the conclusions and disclosure that the company paid. The flat fee keeps her conclusions free of pressure, and the disclosure lets readers judge the relationship for themselves. Missing either one is a violation.

HEART Q: An analyst rewrites a rival firm's industry thesis in her own words and publishes it with no credit. Does the rewording avoid a violation?

LEWIS: Answer. No, it violates Standard one C, Misrepresentation. Here is why. Plagiarism covers ideas, forecasts and models, not just copied sentences. Publishing someone else's thinking as your own misleads readers about whose work they are relying on. A credit to the original source would have fixed it.

LEWIS Q: An analyst drops a table of standard index returns from a widely known financial data service into a report without citing the source. Is that a violation?

HEART: Answer. No. Here is why. Projections, statistics and tables from recognized financial and statistical reporting services are an exception to the credit requirement. That data is standard and widely available, so nobody is misled about whose analysis they are reading. Citing it anyway is still a good habit.

HEART Q: An adviser tells a client that a certificate of deposit is protected by government deposit insurance up to the legal limit. Is that a prohibited guarantee?

LEWIS: Answer. No. Here is why. Standard one C bars guaranteeing a return that nobody actually stands behind. Here a government body explicitly provides the protection, and the adviser described it accurately, limit included. The violation would be promising a set return on something inherently risky.

LEWIS Q: A charterholder pads an expense claim at work by a small amount. Is that a violation, and of what?

HEART: Answer. Yes, it violates Standard one D, Misconduct. Here is why. The first part of Misconduct bans dishonesty, fraud and deceit in professional conduct, whatever the size. No criminal charge is needed for it to count. Dishonesty in professional dealings tells clients and employers they cannot rely on you.

HEART Q: An analyst is locked in a long running dispute with a neighbor over a fence, with no link to her work, the markets or her designation. Is that misconduct?

LEWIS: Answer. No. Here is why. Standard one D reaches conduct outside the office only when it has a real link to your professional life, for example conduct tied to the investment profession or to your designation. A purely personal dispute has no such link, and that would hold even if she stretched the truth in it. Filing a complaint to settle a quarrel like that would itself be a misuse of the conduct process.

LEWIS Q: A bond analyst is reassigned to run a commodity futures program and starts trading for clients right away, planning to learn as he goes. Is that a violation, and of what?

HEART: Answer. Yes, it violates Standard one E, Competence. Here is why. A new or unfamiliar role puts the burden on you to build the knowledge and skills it requires before you act for clients. Experience with bonds does not carry over automatically to futures. Learning on the job with client money is exactly what the Standard rules out.

HEART Q: Does Standard one E require a set number of continuing education hours each year?

LEWIS: Answer. No. Here is why. The Standard recommends continuing education but does not require it or prescribe any program. What it does require is the result, competence that matches your role and stays current. Courses, designations, conferences, employer training and self study are all ways to get there.

## Key takeaways

HEART: First, a few memory hooks.

LEWIS: Standard one in five short instructions, in order. Obey the law. Stay independent. Tell the truth. Act with integrity. Stay able. That is Knowledge of the Law, Independence and Objectivity, Misrepresentation, Misconduct and Competence.

HEART: For Standard one A, stricter wins, in either direction, and legal does not mean allowed. When you find wrongdoing, speak up, step back, and step out only in an extreme case. Speak up means your supervisor or compliance. Step back means dissociate. Step out means leave the firm. Reporting to a regulator is not required unless the law demands it, while reporting a fellow member to CFA Institute is strongly encouraged.

LEWIS: For Standard one B, clients may thank you, as long as your employer knows, before if possible and after if not. Everyone else gets a token at most. Fly commercial when you can. Issuer paid research is fine when the fee is flat, fixed in advance and disclosed. A hot IPO for your own account is off limits. And buying someone else's judgment is as bad as selling your own.

HEART: For Standard one C, only a real guarantor can guarantee. New words, same idea, still needs credit. The exceptions are standard data from recognized services, and models from colleagues who have left, as long as you never claim them as yours alone. Pick benchmarks and pricing services for accuracy, not flattery.

LEWIS: For Standard one D, dishonesty at work is misconduct at any size. Off the clock counts only when it touches your professional life. And the conduct process is not a weapon for personal fights.

HEART: For Standard one E, learn first, then act. A loss is not proof of incompetence, and a credential is not proof of competence. Continuing education is recommended, not required.

LEWIS: And across all five, the excuse built into the story rarely changes the answer.

HEART: Now, how you would say the big ideas to a senior leader.

LEWIS: On the law: we follow whichever rule is tougher, the law or our professional code, and when we see wrongdoing we raise it and then step away from it. The jargon is Knowledge of the Law, the stricter rule and dissociation.

HEART: On independence: our opinions are not for sale. Gifts stay small, client gifts get disclosed, we pay our own travel, and research a company pays for carries a flat fee and a clear disclosure. The jargon is independence and objectivity, token gifts and issuer paid research.

LEWIS: On communication: everything we tell clients is accurate and complete, including what we choose to leave out, and we credit other people's work. The jargon is misrepresentation, misleading omissions and plagiarism.

HEART: On conduct: dishonesty in our professional dealings ends trust, whatever the amount, while purely private matters stay private unless they reflect on someone as a professional. The jargon is misconduct.

LEWIS: And on capability: people learn a job before they do it and keep learning as it changes, and a bad result alone does not mean someone was not up to it. The jargon is competence, meaning knowledge, skills and abilities.
