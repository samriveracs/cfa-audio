LEWIS: This episode covers Standard two, Integrity of Capital Markets, on inside information and market manipulation, and Standard three, Duties to Clients, on loyalty, fair dealing, suitability, honest performance reporting and confidentiality. For each part you will hear what it requires, why it exists, the procedures that prevent violations, the classic traps, and short cases that each end in a verdict.

## Module 91.3, Guidance for Standard II

HEART: Standard one was about you, your honesty and your competence. Standard two moves outward, to the market itself. Its title is Integrity of Capital Markets, and it has two parts. Standard two A, Material Nonpublic Information. And Standard two B, Market Manipulation.

LEWIS: The idea underneath both is the same. A market is a shared signal. Prices are supposed to reflect information that everyone can reach, and trading volume is supposed to reflect real buyers and real sellers. Insider trading corrupts the first. Manipulation corrupts the second. Either way, honest investors end up on the losing side of a rigged game, and once they sense that, they demand higher returns or walk away.

HEART: Standard two A, Material Nonpublic Information. In plain words, when you know something the market has not heard, and it would matter to an investment's value, you cannot act on it, and you cannot get anyone else to act on it either. Acting on it includes trading, and it also includes changing a recommendation because of it. Getting others to act includes tipping a friend, nudging a client, or hinting to a colleague.

LEWIS: Two things do not matter. How you got the information does not matter. Overheard in an elevator, told by your sister, or handed over by the company itself, the rule is the same. And being an insider does not matter. The Standard covers anyone who holds the information.

HEART: So the whole Standard rests on a two part test, and both parts must be true. Is it material? And is it nonpublic? If either answer is no, Standard two A is not the problem.

LEWIS: Start with material. Information is material if releasing it would likely move the price of a security, or if a reasonable investor would want to know it before deciding to buy or sell. Think of the news that makes a stock jump or slump the day it comes out. An earnings result far from expectations. A merger or a takeover offer. A drug approval or rejection. A big lawsuit. The surprise departure of a chief executive. A dividend cut.

HEART: Two qualities push materiality up or down: how specific the information is, and how reliable the source is. A finance chief telling you next quarter's sales figure is both specific and reliable. A sales director at a trade show who says, with a grin, that the coming quarter should be interesting has told you nothing specific at all. Information that is ambiguous about which way it would push the price, or whether it would push it at all, may not be material.

LEWIS: Now nonpublic. Information stays nonpublic until it has been made available to the marketplace in general, not to a chosen few. Picture a company that shares news on a call or at a meeting limited to a group of analysts. Everyone on that call or in that room now holds nonpublic information, even though the company chose to share it. Selective disclosure like that is exactly how insider trading problems begin.

HEART: And checking is your job. Before you act, you are responsible for confirming that the information really has gone out to the market, for example through a press release or a regulatory filing. Assuming it must be out by now is not enough.

LEWIS: A case that puts both tests together. Clara is a bond analyst at an asset manager. Over coffee, a friend who works at a credit rating agency mentions that the agency will cut a retailer's bonds to junk on Monday. On Friday, Clara sells the retailer's bonds from her fund. Violation?

HEART: Yes. A cut to junk is specific, it comes from inside the agency, and it would clearly move the bonds' price, so it is material. The market has not heard it, so it is nonpublic. And notice that she traded bonds, not shares. The Standard reaches any investment whose value the news would affect.

LEWIS: That reach is wider than people expect. It covers the company's shares and bonds, the options and swaps tied to them, and mutual funds that hold them. Someone who buys call options instead of the stock, or piles into a small sector fund packed with that stock, has not escaped the rule.

HEART: Now a situation where you are supposed to have the information. In investment banking, lending or advisory work, a company hands over confidential details so the work can get done. Working with it on the job it was shared for is fine. Putting it to any other purpose is not, until it becomes public.

LEWIS: A case. Simon works in investment banking, helping a software company plan its purchase of a smaller rival. He spends the week working through the rival's private numbers to shape the offer. On Friday he buys call options on the rival's stock. Violation?

HEART: Yes. Shaping the offer was the intended purpose. Buying options was not, and options tied to the target's stock are squarely covered.

HEART: Now the exception that makes research possible, the mosaic theory. An analyst may combine public information with nonpublic information that is not material, and reach a conclusion that would be material if others knew it. That is not a violation. That is the job.

LEWIS: Picture a mosaic on a wall. Each tile on its own is either public, or private but trivial. The picture appears only because the analyst fits the tiles together. The skill is in the analysis, and nobody handed over the answer.

HEART: A case. Ruby covers a home improvement retailer. She studies the company's filings, public data on new housing permits, and industry surveys of contractor demand. On a train, a store manager from the chain tells her the paint aisle has felt busier lately, with no numbers. She concludes that quarterly sales will beat expectations, and she upgrades the stock. Violation?

LEWIS: No. The filings and data are public. The manager's comment is nonpublic, but it is vague and not material on its own. The conclusion came from her analysis, which is the mosaic theory at work. She should keep documentation of her research, so she can show later how she got there.

HEART: Change one fact. The manager emails her the chain's actual sales figures for the quarter, two weeks before the company reports them. Now one tile is material and nonpublic, and acting on it would be a violation. The mosaic theory protects analysis. It does not protect a leak with some analysis wrapped around it.

LEWIS: So what should you do if material nonpublic information lands in your lap? Do not trade and do not pass it on. Make reasonable efforts to get it released to the public, which usually means encouraging the company it concerns to announce it. Tell your compliance department. And encourage your firm to adopt procedures that stop this kind of information from being misused.

LEWIS: Firms carry most of the procedures, and the central one is the firewall, also called an information barrier. Think of an investment firm as a building with two wings. In one, bankers handle confidential company information every day. In the other sit research, sales and trading, the people who act in the market. The firewall keeps sensitive information from flowing between the wings.

HEART: It has three parts. First, a clearance area, usually compliance or the legal department, that screens sensitive communication between departments. If a banker needs to speak to an analyst, the conversation goes through compliance, which decides what may be said. Second, review of employee trades, so someone notices when a staff member buys a stock just before a client of the firm announces a deal. Third, three lists.

LEWIS: A watch list is a confidential list, kept mostly within compliance, of companies the firm holds sensitive information about. Compliance quietly monitors trading in those names. It stays confidential because putting a name on a widely shared list could itself tip people off. A restricted list is the stronger tool. It limits or bars trading in a name by employees and by the firm, and it often limits what research may say about that company. A rumor list tracks companies caught up in market rumors, so compliance can watch for suspicious trading around them.

HEART: Then the firm's own trading, called proprietary trading. While the firm holds material nonpublic information about a company, it should monitor and restrict its own trading in that name. But here is the subtle point. Halting all of it may be a mistake. If a firm that normally trades a stock all day suddenly stops, other traders notice, and the silence itself signals that something is coming.

LEWIS: So the middle path is to go passive. The firm keeps filling orders that customers bring in on their own, taking the opposite side of those unsolicited customer trades, but it starts no trades of its own in that name. The market sees business as usual, and the firm takes no position based on what it knows.

HEART: The traps for Standard two A. Trap one says the company told me, so it must be fine. A select group is not the market. Trap two says I traded options, or a fund, not the stock. The reach covers related instruments. Trap three says I did not trade, I only told my brother. Causing someone else to act is a violation. Trap four runs the other way. Candidates flag the mosaic analyst as an insider trader. Public pieces plus private pieces that are not material, assembled by skill, add up to legitimate research.

LEWIS: The takeaway for Standard two A. Material and nonpublic means you neither act nor cause anyone else to act, whatever the instrument and however you got it, while the mosaic theory protects real analysis.

HEART: Standard two B, Market Manipulation. In plain words, do not distort prices or puff up trading volume when the aim is to fool other investors.

LEWIS: Why does it exist? Price and volume are signals. Investors take a rising price as news and heavy volume as interest. Faking those signals is like a shop paying actors to line up outside so passersby assume something good is inside. The people fooled are the ones who pay for it.

HEART: The word that decides almost every manipulation question is intent. Plenty of honest activity moves prices and volume. A manager who buys a thinly traded stock because she believes it is cheap pushes its price up. An honest sell rating from a respected analyst knocks a stock down. None of that is manipulation, because nobody is trying to mislead anyone.

LEWIS: Manipulation comes in two forms. The first is information based manipulation, which means spreading false or misleading information to move a price. False rumors, fake news releases and exaggerated promotion all belong here. The classic is the pump and dump. Buy a small, thinly traded stock, hype it with claims you know are false or misleading, then sell into the buying you created.

HEART: A case. Darius owns shares in a tiny mining company. He writes a fake press release in the company's name announcing a huge gold discovery and gets a financial news website to post it. The stock triples, and he sells before the hoax comes to light. Violation?

LEWIS: Yes, of Standard two B. That is information based manipulation. He spread information he knew was false to move the price, and he profited from the buyers he misled. The same logic catches the reverse, spreading false bad news to push a price down after betting against the stock.

HEART: The second form is transaction based manipulation, which means trades designed to give a false picture of price or activity. One version is trading back and forth between accounts you control, or with a partner, so the volume looks busy when no real change of ownership has happened. Those are often called wash trades. And here is the twist. Faking volume can violate the Standard even if the price never moves, because creating a false impression of activity, with intent to mislead, is enough.

LEWIS: Another version is building such a dominant position in an asset that you can push its price around, or the price of a derivative tied to it.

HEART: A case. Cole manages a bond fund with a large position in a bond that rarely trades, and the fund values that bond at the price of its most recent trade. On the last afternoon of the quarter, Cole buys a small amount of the bond at a price well above where it has been trading, purely to lift the value his fund reports. Violation?

LEWIS: Yes. That is transaction based manipulation. The trade had no investment purpose. It was designed to plant a false price that would mislead the fund's investors about its performance.

HEART: The traps for Standard two B. Trap one treats any trade that moves a price as manipulation. Without intent to mislead, there is no violation, even in a thin market. Trap two assumes no harm was done because the price did not move. Inflating volume to create a false impression violates the Standard on its own. Trap three mixes up the two forms. False words are information based. Misleading trades are transaction based.

LEWIS: The takeaway for Standard two B. Do not distort prices or inflate volume to mislead. Intent separates manipulation from honest trading, and manipulation comes in two forms, false information and misleading trades.

## Module 91.4, Guidance for Standards III(A) and III(B)

HEART: Standard three, Duties to Clients, has five parts, and this module covers the first two. Standard three A, Loyalty, Prudence, and Care. And Standard three B, Fair Dealing.

LEWIS: Standard three A, Loyalty, Prudence, and Care. In plain words, you owe your clients loyalty, you must apply reasonable care and sensible judgment, and you must act for their benefit. When their interests collide with your employer's or your own, theirs come first. Client, then employer, then you.

HEART: Why does it exist? A client hands you control of money, or relies on your advice, precisely because you know more than they do. That gap creates the temptation to serve yourself, and this Standard closes it.

LEWIS: One subtle point. The Standard does not, by itself, make you a fiduciary where the law has not already done so. But it still requires you to act in your clients' best interests and to recommend investments that suit their objectives and risk tolerance. So an answer saying that loyalty to clients applies only to fiduciaries is wrong.

HEART: Prudence has a specific meaning here. It is the care, skill and diligence that someone in a similar role, who knows the subject, would bring to the same circumstances. A trustee of a child's education fund and the manager of an aggressive hedge fund are both held to that test, but what a prudent person does in each seat is very different.

LEWIS: Several duties follow. Judge each investment decision by its effect on the client's whole portfolio, not in isolation. Diversify, unless the client's situation says otherwise. When you manage a pool of client money, such as a trust or an account under an investment management agreement, follow the terms of the governing documents. And tell clients about any limits on the advice you can give, for example if you can only offer a short menu of approved products.

HEART: The first exam question under this Standard is often, who is the client? For an individual account, it is the person. For a pension plan, the duty runs to the plan's participants and beneficiaries, the workers and retirees whose money it is, not to the company that sponsors the plan or the officers who hired you. For a fund run to a stated mandate, the duty is to manage it according to that mandate. And for an analyst publishing research, the client may be the investing public as a whole.

LEWIS: A case. Paloma manages the pension plan of a regional trucking company. The company is short of cash, so its finance chief asks her to have the plan build a warehouse and rent it to the company well below market. Would agreeing violate Standard three A?

HEART: Yes. Her duty runs to the plan's beneficiaries, not to the sponsor. The cheap rent helps the company at the plan's expense and ties the workers' savings even closer to their employer. The executives who hired her are not the client for this purpose.

LEWIS: Next, client brokerage. The commissions paid on a client's trades belong to the client. Soft dollars, also called soft commissions, are the practice of steering those commissions to brokers in exchange for goods and services, usually research. The rule is simple. Client brokerage must benefit the client. Research that helps manage that client's money can qualify. Paying the firm's own running costs with it does not.

HEART: A case. Keith runs a small advisory firm. He sends client trades to a broker that charges above market commissions, and in return the broker pays the partners' country club dues and the firm's holiday party. Violation?

LEWIS: Yes. The dues and the party serve the firm, not the clients whose commissions paid for them. Clients are paying higher costs for no benefit of their own.

HEART: Related to that is best execution. On every client trade, aim for the best overall result, counting price, commission and market impact together, not just the cheapest commission. And a client may direct you to send its trades to a particular broker. That is allowed, because the commissions are the client's asset. But if that broker may not deliver best execution, you should tell the client so.

LEWIS: Then proxy voting. When a client owns shares, the right to vote on matters such as mergers, board seats and executive pay has economic value, so it is a client asset too. Vote proxies in an informed and responsible way, in the best interests of clients and the ultimate beneficiaries. But you do not have to vote every one. If a cost benefit view shows that voting on a routine matter would cost more than it could ever be worth to the client, skipping it can be fine. Firms should also tell clients how they vote proxies.

HEART: A case. Hana's firm votes every proxy with company management by default. That includes a contested merger that would dilute shareholders, at a company that is a major investment banking client of the firm. Violation?

LEWIS: Yes. On an important vote, automatic support for management is not informed or responsible voting, and here it serves the firm's banking relationship rather than the clients who own the shares.

HEART: The recommended procedures for Standard three A. If you hold client assets, send each client a detailed statement at least every three months, listing what you hold for them and every movement in the account. And push your firm to write down how it meets its duties to clients: obeying the law and treating clients fairly, setting goals and judging suitability across the whole portfolio, diversifying, seeking best execution, voting proxies, keeping confidences, and disclosing conflicts and how it gets paid.

LEWIS: The traps for Standard three A. Trap one says the duty runs to whoever hires you or pays the fee. For a pension plan, it runs to the beneficiaries. Trap two says the employer comes first because it pays your salary. Client, employer, you, in that order. Trap three says soft dollars are always forbidden. They are fine when they buy research that benefits the client. Trap four says every proxy must be voted. A cost benefit view can justify skipping one.

HEART: The takeaway for Standard three A. Know who the client is, put the client first, act with the care of a knowledgeable professional, and remember that commissions and proxy votes belong to the client.

LEWIS: Standard three B, Fair Dealing. In plain words, be fair and objective with every client in all your professional work, from research and advice to the trades you place. No client may be quietly shortchanged when recommendations go out or trades get done.

HEART: Why does it exist? Research and trades are worth the most to whoever acts first. If some clients reliably get the news first or the best fills, the rest are paying for that advantage.

LEWIS: The key phrase is that fairly does not mean identically. Emails arrive a few seconds apart, and some clients pick up the phone before others. That is ordinary business. And firms may offer different levels of service, such as a premium tier with more meetings and more detailed reporting for a higher fee.

HEART: But premium service comes with three conditions. It cannot leave any other client worse off. Every client and prospect must be told that it exists. And anyone willing to pay the price can have it. Think of an airline. First class gets a wider seat and a better meal, but the plane does not take off earlier for them. Premium clients can get more service, but they cannot get a recommendation before everyone else.

LEWIS: Now dissemination, meaning the order in which recommendations go out. When a recommendation is new or changes, every client who has shown interest in the security, or for whom it fits, gets it at the same moment. Nobody gets a head start. Only after it has gone to all of them may you discuss it one on one with particular clients.

HEART: Fairness also covers different kinds of clients. Institution or individual, every client is treated even handedly. The bigger account does not get the earlier call.

LEWIS: A case. A firm's research team cuts its rating on a utility stock from buy to sell. The firm sends the change to its institutional clients right away through its electronic system, and its individual clients learn about it in the next morning's newsletter. Violation?

HEART: Yes, of Standard three B. The institutions got most of a day to act before individual clients heard a word. Every client who holds the stock, or has shown interest in it, should get the change at the same time, whatever their size.

LEWIS: Another case. Two days after that downgrade, Nadia, an adviser at the same firm, takes a call from a client who missed the news and wants to buy more of the utility. Nadia enters the order without mentioning the change. Violation?

HEART: Yes. Any client who has missed a change should hear about it before an order goes in. Telling him takes ten seconds, and he can still buy if he wants to.

LEWIS: Then trades. When one order is placed for many accounts, called a block trade, every account should get the same price and the same commission. When an order is only partly filled, the shares should be shared out in proportion to each account's order, which is called pro rata allocation, or by another fair method set in advance. Orders should be time stamped and handled in the sequence they arrive. And disclosing an unfair method does not make it fair.

HEART: A case. Cyrus's firm gets only part of what it asked for in a popular new bond issue. He fills the orders for his two largest fee paying accounts in full and divides what is left among the smaller ones. Violation?

LEWIS: Yes. Partial fills should be shared pro rata, or by another fair method set in advance, not according to which clients pay the most. Hot new issues add one more rule. Do not take scarce shares for your own account, or for family accounts that are not clients, so that more reach clients. But a family member who is an ordinary fee paying client is treated like any other client, neither favored nor shut out.

HEART: The recommended procedures for Standard three B. As an individual, push your firm for written rules on fair dissemination, and keep track of which clients own what, so every holder hears about a change. As a firm, keep the circle that knows about a coming change small and the gap before release short, and forbid anyone in that circle from talking about it or trading on it early. Write down and disclose how trades get allocated, disclose the service levels on offer, and review accounts regularly for favoritism.

LEWIS: The traps for Standard three B. Trap one says fair means identical. It means no one is disadvantaged. Trap two says a premium tier is automatically unfair. It is fine with disclosure and open access. Trap three says premium clients may hear first. They may not. Trap four says the biggest accounts get filled first. Partial fills are shared fairly, usually pro rata.

HEART: The takeaway for Standard three B. Fair, not identical. Everyone eligible gets every recommendation at the same time, partial fills are shared fairly, and premium service means more service, never earlier access.

## Module 91.5, Guidance for Standards III(C), III(D), and III(E)

LEWIS: Standard three C, Suitability. It applies in two settings. In the first, you advise a client directly. Before any advice or action, you make a reasonable inquiry into how much investing the client has done, what return they want, how much risk they can take and what limits their money faces, and you keep that picture current. Each investment must then fit the client's finances and the goals and limits written down for them, judged across everything the client owns rather than one holding at a time.

HEART: In the second setting, you manage a portfolio to a stated mandate, strategy or style, such as an index fund or a small company value fund. There you do not judge suitability investor by investor. You take only the actions that fit the portfolio's stated objectives and constraints.

LEWIS: Why does it exist? You cannot know whether something fits until you know the person it is meant to fit. A tailor who never measures you is guessing.

HEART: The tool is the investment policy statement, a written document drawn up at the start of the relationship. It sets out the type of client, and whether there are separate beneficiaries, as there are with a trust or a pension plan. It states the return and risk objectives. It lists the constraints: liquidity needs and expected cash flows, the time horizon, taxes, and legal and regulatory circumstances. And it names the benchmarks for measuring performance.

LEWIS: Risk tolerance deserves a word. It is not just how a client feels about risk, but what the client's needs and circumstances can bear. And part of the inquiry is whether leverage, meaning borrowed money, is suitable for that client at all.

HEART: Then the total portfolio. A single holding can look wild on its own and still make the whole portfolio steadier. A small slice of a volatile commodity fund, for example, can lower overall risk if it tends to zig when the rest of the portfolio zags. Suitability is judged by the whole, not by the piece.

LEWIS: Updating is where many violations hide. The Standard says reassess regularly, and the recommended practice is to review the policy statement at least once a year, before any major change to the portfolio, and whenever something big happens, such as a new job, a marriage, a divorce, a new child, an inheritance or a move toward retirement. An old policy statement for a changed life is like an old map of a city that has since been rebuilt.

HEART: A case. Wesley's client, a nurse in her fifties, tells him she has decided to retire in two years rather than in twelve. Wesley notes it in her file, then leaves her aggressive, stock heavy allocation untouched for the next eighteen months. Violation?

LEWIS: Yes, of Standard three C. Her time horizon and her need for income just changed sharply. Suitability is not a one time check. He had to reassess her objectives and constraints and revisit the allocation.

HEART: Now unsolicited trade requests, a favorite exam topic. A client asks you to buy something you know does not fit the policy statement. Step one is the same in every case. Hold the order, walk the client through exactly why it does not fit, and have the client acknowledge that conversation, including that the trade goes against your advice.

LEWIS: Then the path depends on size. If the trade would barely change the risk and return of the whole portfolio, you may follow your firm's own rules on unsuitable trades, but that acknowledgment is required whatever those rules say.

HEART: If the trade would change the portfolio's risk in a material way, one route is a new policy statement, with the client accepting a changed risk profile that makes room for the trade. If the client will not agree to that, your firm's policy may allow the trade in a separate account the client directs, outside your advice. And if nothing else works, you may need to ask whether the relationship should continue at all.

LEWIS: A case. Diego's client, a retired teacher with low risk tolerance, wants to move half her savings into a single regional airline stock. Diego explains why it does not fit, she insists, and Diego simply places the trade. Violation?

HEART: Yes. The effect on her portfolio would be material, and simply placing the trade skips the steps that size calls for. Diego needed to raise updating the policy statement. If she would not accept a changed risk profile, the remaining choices were a separate account she directs herself, if firm policy allows one, or reconsidering the relationship.

LEWIS: The second setting, the mandate. A case. Lucas manages a fund marketed as a short term, high quality bond fund. Yields are low, so he quietly buys long dated, high yield corporate bonds to lift returns. Violation?

HEART: Yes, of Standard three C. Investors chose the fund for its stated mandate, and he acted outside it. The fund now carries interest rate risk and credit risk that its investors never signed up for.

LEWIS: The recommended procedures for Standard three C come down to two habits. Write every client's situation and goals down in a policy statement, and come back to it on a schedule, so it keeps pace with the client's life.

HEART: The traps for Standard three C. Trap one judges a holding in isolation instead of within the whole portfolio. Trap two treats the policy statement as something you write once and file away. Trap three says an unsolicited request should simply be executed, or simply turned down. The answer is to discuss it first, then follow the path the size of the trade calls for. Trap four expects a fund manager to check suitability for each investor. A mandate manager sticks to the mandate.

LEWIS: The takeaway for Standard three C. Know the client before you act, judge fit across the whole portfolio, update as life changes, and if you run a fund to a mandate, stay inside it.

HEART: Standard three D, Performance Presentation. In plain words, any performance you show must be, as far as you can reasonably make it, fair, accurate and complete. No twisting your own results or your firm's, no misleading picture of past or likely future returns, and never say or hint that you can repeat past returns.

LEWIS: Why does it exist? Track record is how most clients choose a manager, and it is also the easiest thing in the business to flatter. Pick the best account, drop the bad years, quietly mix hypothetical results in with real ones, and almost anyone can look brilliant.

HEART: It applies to anyone who communicates performance, whether in a pitch book, a client letter, a website or a summary of an analyst's past calls. And it applies whether or not the firm claims compliance with the Global Investment Performance Standards. Those global standards are the best known way to meet this Standard, and firms are encouraged to follow them, but following them is voluntary.

LEWIS: The center of the recommended procedures is the composite. A composite is a group of portfolios managed to the same strategy, with their returns combined and weighted by size. Show the composite, not a single account picked to stand for the whole. Think of a restaurant judged on every plate it served last year, not on the one dish it photographed for the menu.

HEART: A few more pieces. Keep terminated accounts in the history, along with the date each one left. Otherwise the history contains only survivors, and survivors tend to be the winners. And include every disclosure a reader needs to understand the numbers, such as whether any results are hypothetical rather than real, and whether returns are shown before or after fees. Also consider the audience. A pension committee with its own consultants can handle detailed statistics. A retail client needs plainer information that is still complete. And keep the data and calculations behind every number you show.

LEWIS: A case. Celeste runs twelve accounts in a dividend strategy. Her pitch book shows the returns of the three best accounts and labels them the strategy's track record. Violation?

HEART: Yes, of Standard three D. That is cherry picking. Fair, accurate and complete means a weighted composite of all twelve, not a hand picked few.

LEWIS: A case. Omar's firm website says its growth fund has doubled clients' money over five years, and adds that clients can look forward to more of the same. The five year figure is accurate. Violation?

HEART: Yes. An accurate history does not save it. Promising, or even implying, that past returns will repeat is a violation in its own right.

LEWIS: And a case with a different verdict. Tessa hands a prospect a one page fact sheet showing her composite's annual returns, with a note that it is a summary and an offer of the full presentation on request. Violation?

HEART: No. A brief presentation does not have to include everything, as long as it flags that the information is limited and makes the full details available on request.

LEWIS: The traps for Standard three D. Trap one says the Standard applies only to firms that claim compliance with the global standards. It applies to everyone. Trap two says a true number cannot mislead. A true number from a hand picked account, or a true number paired with a promise about the future, can. Trap three drops terminated accounts because those clients have left. They stay in the history, with their dates.

HEART: The takeaway for Standard three D. Fair, accurate and complete. Show the composite, keep the departed accounts, disclose what a reader needs, and never imply the past will repeat.

LEWIS: Standard three E, Preservation of Confidentiality. In plain words, client information stays private, for current, former and prospective clients alike. There are only three exceptions. The information concerns illegal activity by the client or prospect. The law requires disclosure. Or the client or prospect gives permission.

HEART: Why does it exist? Clients tell advisers things they tell almost no one else: their debts, their health, their marriages, their plans to sell a business. If that leaks, clients stop telling the truth, and the advice gets worse for everyone.

LEWIS: Three features of the scope. It covers what you learn through the relationship, while doing the client's business. It covers former clients, so ending the relationship does not end the duty. And it covers prospects who never became clients at all.

HEART: A case. Marisol meets a prospect who explains that he is in the middle of a divorce and needs to restructure his finances. He never signs with her firm. Months later, at a neighborhood barbecue, Marisol mentions his divorce and his money worries to a mutual friend. Violation?

LEWIS: Yes, of Standard three E. Prospects are covered, the information came to her through the prospective relationship, and none of the three exceptions applies.

HEART: Now the exceptions in practice. If the information concerns the client's illegal activity, you may disclose it, and you may even be obliged to report it to the authorities. Laws differ from place to place, so when you are unsure, speak to compliance or outside counsel before disclosing anything.

LEWIS: A case. Leo's client mentions, almost proudly, that he runs large amounts of business income through his investment account without reporting it to the tax authorities. After talking to his compliance department, Leo reports it to the authorities, as the law requires. Violation?

HEART: No. The information concerns illegal activity by the client, and the law required disclosure, so the duty of confidentiality gives way.

LEWIS: One more point that is really a clarification. Members and candidates are expected to cooperate with investigations by the CFA Institute Professional Conduct Program, so sharing client information with the program in that setting is not a violation, and the program takes reasonable care to keep what it receives confidential.

HEART: The recommended procedures for Standard three E. Pass client details only to colleagues who are cleared for them and are working for that same client. Follow your firm's rules for storing and sending electronic data, and if there are none, urge the firm to write some. You do not have to be a technology expert, but you do have to follow the safeguards.

LEWIS: The traps for Standard three E. Trap one says the duty ends when the client leaves. It does not. Trap two says prospects are not covered. They are. Trap three says confidentiality blocks cooperation with the Professional Conduct Program. It does not. Trap four says you may share client details with any colleague. Only with authorized colleagues who work for that client.

HEART: The takeaway for Standard three E. Client secrets stay secret, for current, former and prospective clients alike, unless the client acted illegally, the law requires disclosure, or the client gives permission.

## Recall check

LEWIS Q: A day before announcing a big product recall, a company posts the news on a password protected website open only to its largest shareholders. An analyst with access sells the stock for clients that afternoon. Is the news public because the company published it?

HEART: Answer. No, it is still material nonpublic information, and selling violates Standard two A. Here is why. A big recall would clearly move the price, so it is material. A website open only to a chosen few is not release to the marketplace in general, so it is still nonpublic. The analyst should not trade or advise on it, and should encourage the company to make it public.

HEART Q: An analyst combines public shipping data, job postings and a supplier's offhand remark that orders feel steady, concludes that a manufacturer's earnings will beat forecasts, and upgrades the stock. Violation?

LEWIS: Answer. No, this is the mosaic theory. Here is why. Each piece is either public or nonpublic but not material. The material conclusion comes from the analyst's own work, not from a leak, and that is what research is for. The analyst should keep documentation showing how the conclusion was reached.

LEWIS Q: A firm that trades a stock every day learns material nonpublic information about the company. Why might halting all of its own trading in that stock be a mistake, and what can it do instead?

HEART: Answer. A sudden halt can itself signal the market, so the firm can go passive and only take the opposite side of unsolicited customer trades. Here is why. Other traders notice when a regular participant suddenly disappears, and they may guess why. By filling only the orders customers bring in on their own, the firm keeps a normal presence without taking any position based on what it knows.

HEART Q: Two traders at different firms agree to trade the same thinly traded stock back and forth all week at matching prices, so that it looks popular. The price barely moves. Violation?

LEWIS: Answer. Yes, it violates Standard two B, Market Manipulation. Here is why. These are transaction based trades with no genuine buying or selling interest behind them, designed to create a false impression of activity. The Standard covers artificially inflating volume with intent to mislead, so no price move is needed for a violation.

LEWIS Q: The executives of a company ask the manager of its pension plan to make an investment that helps the company but adds risk for the plan. Whose interests govern under Standard three A?

HEART: Answer. The interests of the plan's participants and beneficiaries. Here is why. For a pension plan, the client is the people whose retirement money it is, not the sponsor or the officers who hired the manager. A request that serves the company at the beneficiaries' expense must be declined.

HEART Q: A manager pays normal commissions on client trades to a broker that supplies research the manager relies on to run those same clients' portfolios. Violation?

LEWIS: Answer. No, that is an acceptable use of soft dollars. Here is why. Client brokerage belongs to the client and must be spent for the client's benefit. Research that helps manage the portfolios whose trades paid for it does exactly that. Paying the firm's own running costs with it would violate Standard three A.

LEWIS Q: A firm discloses in its brochure that premium clients receive each new recommendation an hour before everyone else. Does the disclosure make it acceptable?

HEART: Answer. No, it violates Standard three B, Fair Dealing. Here is why. Premium service can buy more contact and more detail, but it must not disadvantage other clients. An early look at a recommendation lets premium clients trade before everyone else, at the others' expense. Disclosure does not cure an unfair practice.

HEART Q: An adviser wrote a client's investment policy statement four years ago. Since then the client has sold her business and taken in an elderly parent, and the adviser has not revisited it. Is there a problem?

LEWIS: Answer. Yes, under Standard three C, Suitability. Here is why. Suitability must be reassessed regularly and whenever the client's circumstances change. A business sale and a dependent parent can change the time horizon, the liquidity needs and the ability to bear risk, so recommendations based on the old statement may no longer fit.

LEWIS Q: A client insists on a trade that would materially change his portfolio's risk, but he refuses to update his investment policy statement. What are the adviser's options?

HEART: Answer. Firm policy may allow the trade in a separate account the client directs himself, and failing that, the adviser may need to reconsider the relationship. Here is why. The adviser should not place an unsuitable trade before discussing it, and for a material change the natural fix is to update the policy statement. If the client will not accept a changed risk profile, the trade cannot simply go into the account the adviser manages under the old one.

HEART Q: A manager shows a strategy's track record but leaves out accounts that were terminated after poor years, reasoning that those clients are gone. Violation?

LEWIS: Answer. Yes, it violates Standard three D, Performance Presentation. Here is why. Dropping the departed accounts leaves only the survivors, which flatters the record. Fair, accurate and complete performance includes terminated accounts in the history, along with when they were terminated.

LEWIS Q: The CFA Institute Professional Conduct Program asks a member under inquiry for a former client's account statements. Does handing them over violate Standard three E?

HEART: Answer. No. Here is why. Members and candidates are expected to cooperate with Professional Conduct Program investigations, and the program takes reasonable care to keep what it receives confidential. The duty to former clients still applies everywhere else, so the same statements could not be shared with a friend or a new employer.

## Key takeaways

HEART: First, a few memory hooks.

LEWIS: For Standard two A, two keys must both turn: material and nonpublic. When both turn, no trading and no tipping, in anything the news would move. And a room is not the market. For the mosaic theory, public tiles plus trivial private tiles, assembled by skill, make a legal picture. One material private tile spoils it.

HEART: For the firewall: the watch list is kept quiet, the restricted list says stop, and the rumor list says look closer. And when the firm holds inside information, do not go dark, go passive.

LEWIS: For Standard two B: moving a price is fine, meaning to mislead is not. Lies are information based. Fake trades are transaction based.

HEART: For Standard three A: client, then employer, then me. For a pension plan, the client is the people the money is for. Soft dollars buy research for the client, never the firm's own bills. Proxies get informed votes, though not necessarily every one.

LEWIS: For Standard three B: fair is not identical. Premium buys more service, never a head start. Everyone eligible hears at once, and partial fills are shared fairly, usually in proportion.

HEART: For Standard three C: measure before you tailor, and measure again when life changes. For an unsuitable request, talk first and get an acknowledgment. Minor means firm policy decides. Material means a new policy statement, a separate account the client directs, or parting ways. And a fund manager sticks to the mandate.

LEWIS: For Standard three D: fair, accurate and complete. Show the whole team, not the star player. Departed accounts stay in the history. And the past is not a promise.

HEART: For Standard three E: secrets outlive the relationship and cover prospects too. Three keys unlock them: the client's illegal activity, the law, and the client's permission. And the Professional Conduct Program is not a leak.

LEWIS: Now, how you would say the big ideas to a senior leader.

HEART: On inside information: if we know something market moving that the market does not, we do not trade on it or pass it on. Research pieced together from public facts and trivial private details is fine, and deal teams stay walled off from traders. The jargon is material nonpublic information, the mosaic theory and the firewall.

LEWIS: On manipulation: we never trade or talk to fake a price or fake demand. The jargon is market manipulation, information based and transaction based.

HEART: On loyalty: the client's interest beats the firm's and ours, and the commissions and votes we control belong to clients. The jargon is loyalty, prudence and care, soft dollars, best execution and proxy voting.

LEWIS: On fairness: every eligible client gets our calls at the same time and a fair share of scarce trades, and paying more buys service, not speed. The jargon is fair dealing, dissemination and pro rata trade allocation.

HEART: On suitability: we know each client's goals and limits in writing, judge every move against the whole portfolio, and refresh it when their life changes. The jargon is suitability and the investment policy statement.

LEWIS: And on reporting and privacy: we show all our results, not our best ones, and client secrets stay secret unless they involve the client's illegal acts, the law demands disclosure, or the client agrees. The jargon is performance presentation, composites and preservation of confidentiality.
