LEWIS: This episode covers Standard four, Duties to Employers, on loyalty to the firm, outside pay and the duties of supervisors, and Standard five, Investment Analysis, Recommendations, and Actions, on doing the research properly, communicating clearly with clients and keeping records.

## Module 91.6, Guidance for Standard IV

HEART: Standards two and three looked outward, to the market and to clients. Standard four turns to the firm you work for. Its title is Duties to Employers, and it has three parts. Standard four A, Loyalty. Standard four B, Additional Compensation Arrangements. And Standard four C, Responsibilities of Supervisors.

LEWIS: Think of a job as a trade. The firm pays you, and it also hands you things it cannot easily take back: its clients, its research, its systems and its plans. Standard four is your side of that trade. Give the firm your skills honestly, do not turn what it gave you against it, and if you manage people, make sure they follow the rules too.

HEART: Standard four A, Loyalty. In plain words, while you work for a firm, you are on its side in everything to do with the job. It gets your full effort and talent, its secrets stay secret, and you do nothing that hurts it, such as damaging the business or quietly costing it profit.

LEWIS: Why does it exist? A firm can only trust its people with clients and secrets if it can count on them not to walk off with those things or compete behind its back. Without that trust, firms would share less, and clients would get worse service.

HEART: Notice the scope. This duty is owed by employees. A contractor's obligations are whatever the contract says, so both sides should spell them out clearly at the start, and the contractor then sticks to them.

LEWIS: And loyalty has limits, which is where many exam questions sit. First, clients still come first. Loyalty to your employer never justifies harming clients, and it never requires you to break the law or the Code and Standards. Within those limits, you should still weigh what your actions do to the firm's integrity and its long run health.

HEART: Second, loyalty does not swallow your private life. Your employer does not automatically outrank your family or your other personal commitments. When the two pull against each other, the answer is an honest conversation with your employer and a workable balance. A manager who leaves early on Thursdays to care for a parent, by agreement with her boss, is not being disloyal.

LEWIS: Now the applied topics. First, independent practice, which means outside work for pay that competes with your employer. It is allowed, with conditions. Before you start, give your employer a notice that fully describes the arrangement: the services, the pay, how long it will run and the nature of the work. Then wait until the employer consents to all of those terms. Only then can you begin.

HEART: A case. Tobias is a financial planner at a regional wealth firm. A neighbor who runs a small dental practice asks him to design retirement plans for the practice's staff, for a monthly fee, on evenings and weekends. Planning is exactly what Tobias's employer sells. He takes the work and plans to mention it at his next annual review. Violation?

LEWIS: Yes, of Standard four A. He is competing with his employer for pay without first describing the arrangement and getting consent to its terms. Doing the work on his own time does not change that. The fix was a full notice to his employer before taking the work, and consent to every term before starting.

HEART: Next, leaving an employer, the most heavily tested part of Loyalty. The core rule is that your duty lasts until your resignation takes effect.

LEWIS: That does not mean you cannot plan. Preparing to compete is allowed, as long as the preparations themselves do not breach your duty. You can look for a new job, arrange financing, find office space, or set up a new company on your own time. What you cannot do before you leave is the list of classic violations. Taking trade secrets, such as the firm's proprietary models or methods. Misusing confidential information. Soliciting the firm's clients. Self dealing, which means using your position to benefit yourself at the firm's expense, such as quietly steering a piece of business the firm was pursuing toward your own new venture. And taking client lists.

HEART: A case. Delia, a portfolio manager, decides to open her own advisory firm. While still employed, and on her own time, she arranges a business loan, signs a lease on a small office and hires a lawyer to draft client agreements for the new firm. She tells no clients and takes nothing from her employer. Violation?

LEWIS: No. Those are preparations, not competition. Now change one fact. A week before she resigns, Delia announces her new firm on her professional networking profile, where dozens of her clients are among her contacts, and invites them to move their accounts. That is a violation of Standard four A. She is still an employee, and the post solicits her employer's clients.

HEART: And even after you give notice, follow the firm's procedures for telling clients about your departure. Urging them to follow you, or promoting your new firm, waits until you are gone.

LEWIS: Records are the next trap. Firm records stay firm property wherever they sit, in a desk drawer, on a laptop at home or in a personal cloud account, and that includes files you created yourself. So taking them without permission is a violation in itself. Picture a departing analyst who forwards the firm's client contact spreadsheet to his personal email the night before he resigns and never opens it again. Still a violation. The breach is taking the records, whether or not they are ever used.

HEART: Once you have actually left, the picture changes. The names you carry in your head are generally not a secret, and neither is your know how. The experience and skill you built at the old firm come with you. So after your last day you may contact former clients, as long as you find them through public sources rather than the old firm's records, and you do not break a valid agreement you signed, such as a noncompete.

LEWIS: One wrinkle. Some firms sign agreements with each other that let a departing broker take limited client information, typically names and contact details. In the United States, many brokerage firms belong to one called the Protocol for Broker Recruiting. Acting within the terms of an agreement like that is not a violation.

HEART: Social media has its own rules. Whatever your firm says about contacting clients online binds you, and it matters most on the way out, because the firm decides how and when clients hear that someone is leaving. The cleanest habit is two accounts from day one, one personal and one for work.

LEWIS: Now whistleblowing. Normally, handing the firm's confidential information to an outsider would breach loyalty. In rare cases, though, going against the firm is the right call, when the aim is to protect clients or keep the market honest and you stand to gain nothing from it.

HEART: A case. Farah works in fund accounting at a real estate fund manager. She finds that the firm has been billing its investors for property appraisals that were never carried out. She raises it internally and nothing changes, so she copies the invoices and sends them to the securities regulator. Violation?

LEWIS: No. She broke the usual confidentiality, but to protect clients from fraud, with nothing in it for herself. Now picture a colleague who finds the same invoices and tells management he will keep quiet in exchange for a larger severance package. That is not whistleblowing. He is using the firm's secrets for personal gain, which breaches loyalty.

HEART: Many firms run a channel where staff can report wrongdoing without giving their names, and for some firms it is a legal requirement. Know how yours works, and push your firm toward good practice if it falls short.

LEWIS: The recommended procedures for Standard four A are short. A good habit is handing your employer a copy of the Code and Standards, so the firm knows the rules you follow. And on the firm side, pay plans should never make cutting ethical corners the profitable choice. A bonus based only on how much client money someone moves into the firm's own products is the kind of design that invites trouble.

HEART: The traps for Standard four A. Trap one says any planning to leave is disloyal. Preparing is fine, while soliciting, taking records and misusing confidential information are not. Trap two says the records were never used, so there is no harm. Taking them is the violation. Trap three says you can never contact former clients. After you leave, you can, through public sources and absent a noncompete.

LEWIS: Trap four says outside work on your own time is your own business. If it competes with the firm for pay, it needs notice and the employer's consent to every term before it begins. And trap five says loyalty means doing whatever the employer asks. Clients, the law and the Code come first, and whistleblowing to protect clients is not a breach.

HEART: The takeaway for Standard four A. Work for your employer's benefit until your last day. Prepare to compete if you like, but take nothing and solicit no one before you go, and never let loyalty override clients, markets or the law.

LEWIS: Standard four B, Additional Compensation Arrangements. In plain words, if someone other than your employer offers you something of value, whether pay, a perk or a gift, and it could compete with your employer's business or reasonably be expected to pull you into a conflict with it, you need written consent from all parties involved before you take it.

HEART: Why does it exist? Your employer pays you for your time and your judgment. If someone else is also paying you, your incentives can drift, toward that person's account or toward work the firm is not paying for. The employer can only judge that risk if it knows the terms.

LEWIS: Three details are tested. First, the word compensation is broad. It includes direct and indirect pay from a client and benefits from any third party, and it does not have to be cash. Free travel, a club membership or shares in a company all count. Second, consent has to be in writing, and email counts. A nod in the hallway does not. Third, consent comes from all parties involved, and that always includes your employer, not just the person making the offer.

HEART: Now the distinction that decides most questions here, because it links back to Standard one B. Picture two notes from the same client. The first says, thank you for a great year, here is a gift. That rewards past performance. It is a gift, governed by Standard one B, Independence and Objectivity, and the rule is disclosure to your employer.

LEWIS: The second note says, if my account beats its benchmark next year, I will give you something. That depends on future performance. It is an additional compensation arrangement under Standard four B, and you need written consent before you accept. The reason for the difference is incentives. A reward for what already happened is far less likely to change how you work. A reward for what happens next can tilt your effort toward that one account at other clients' expense, or tempt you to take more risk than the client should be taking.

HEART: A case. Ivan manages money for a client who recently sold her family business. She tells him that if her portfolio beats its benchmark this year, she will personally pay him a cash bonus equal to a small slice of the extra gain. Ivan mentions it to his manager in a quick phone call, the manager says it sounds fine, and Ivan accepts. Violation?

LEWIS: Yes, of Standard four B. The reward depends on future performance, so it is additional compensation, and a casual spoken okay is not written consent. An email laying out the offer, and a written reply from the firm approving it, would have done the job.

HEART: The recommended procedures for Standard four B. The moment an outside offer appears, put it in writing to your employer: what you would be paid, what you would do for it, roughly how much it is worth and how long it would run. If you are hired part time, raise any outside work that could compete at the hiring stage, and then respect whatever limits the employer sets.

LEWIS: And on the firm side, whoever is offering the extra pay should confirm its terms, performance incentives included, so the employer knows exactly what it is approving.

HEART: The traps for Standard four B. Trap one says telling your boss is enough. It must be written consent. Trap two says only cash counts. Benefits of any kind count. Trap three mixes up the two client offers. Past performance means a gift and disclosure under Standard one B. Future performance means additional compensation and written consent first under Standard four B.

LEWIS: The takeaway for Standard four B. If outside pay or perks could compete with your employer or create a conflict with its interest, get written consent from everyone involved before you accept, and email counts.

HEART: Standard four C, Responsibilities of Supervisors. In plain words, if people report to you or act under your authority, you must make reasonable efforts to keep them within the law and within the Code and Standards.

LEWIS: Why does it exist? Rules only work if someone checks that they are followed, and a firm whose managers only react after the damage is done invites the next scandal. And the duty reaches everyone you supervise, whether or not they are CFA Institute members or candidates.

HEART: Reasonable efforts have two halves. Prevention, meaning systems that stop violations before they happen. And detection, meaning monitoring that would actually catch them when they do. The compliance system behind both has to be adequate, which means it meets industry standards, regulatory requirements and the Code and Standards.

LEWIS: Here is a point that surprises people. If you supervise with a reasonable system that is actually enforced, and someone still breaks the rules, you have not automatically violated Standard four C. Determined people can get around good controls. The test is whether your efforts were reasonable, not whether the outcome was perfect, although violations that keep happening can be a sign the system is not adequate after all. You may also hand supervisory tasks to others, but you must still make sure they know how to prevent and detect violations, so delegating does not end your own responsibility.

HEART: Now, what to do when a violation turns up. Respond promptly. Investigate thoroughly. And while the investigation runs, step up supervision of the person involved or limit what they are allowed to do, so the problem cannot continue. What never satisfies the Standard is taking the person's word that it will not happen again.

LEWIS: A case. Clive heads a team of client advisers. Firm policy says advisers may send clients text messages only through an approved system that keeps a copy. Clive learns that one adviser, Wade, has been texting clients trade ideas from his personal phone. Wade apologizes and promises to stop. Clive takes him at his word and moves on. Violation?

HEART: Yes, of Standard four C. A promise is not an investigation. Clive should have looked promptly into what Wade sent and to whom, limited or closely watched his client contact in the meantime, and checked how the firm's controls missed it. Those texts are also records the firm is supposed to keep, which links ahead to Standard five C.

LEWIS: Now the other classic situation. What if you are asked to supervise inside a firm whose compliance system is inadequate, or missing altogether? Two steps. First, bring the problem to senior management and recommend corrective action. That part is not optional. Second, if the gaps mean you cannot supervise properly, decline the supervisory responsibility until the firm adopts adequate procedures, and put that refusal in writing so there is a clear record of what you turned down and why.

HEART: A case. Hazel is asked to take charge of the advisers at a small branch her firm has just bought. She finds no written rules on personal trading, no review of what advisers send to clients, and no compliance officer covering the branch. She writes to senior management listing the gaps and the fixes she recommends, and she declines in writing to take on supervision of the branch until those procedures are adopted. Violation?

LEWIS: No. That is exactly the path. The tempting wrong answer is to accept the role and do your best inside a broken system.

HEART: The recommended procedures for Standard four C come in two sets. For members, recommend that your employer adopt a code of ethics, and encourage it to share that code with clients. Once a program is running, the supervisor's job is to keep it alive: make sure the right people have it, refresh it as rules change, train and remind staff, build an ethics review into performance reviews, and watch what people actually do so that violations surface.

LEWIS: For firms, keep the code of ethics and the compliance procedures as separate documents. The code states broad principles. The procedures are the detailed rules that put them into practice. Mixing the two tends to dilute the ethical message, because the principles get lost among operational detail.

HEART: What do adequate procedures look like? Plain language that anyone at the firm can follow. A named compliance officer who knows exactly what powers the job carries. Checks and balances, so no one person polices themselves. A clear statement of what the rules cover and what behavior is allowed. A known route for reporting violations, and known penalties. And pay that never rewards bad conduct.

LEWIS: The traps for Standard four C. Trap one says a violation by someone you supervise proves you failed. Not if your system was reasonable and enforced. Trap two says a promise or a warning is enough. It takes a prompt investigation, with tighter supervision or limits in the meantime. Trap three says a supervisor facing an inadequate system should simply do the best they can. Raise it with management, recommend fixes, and decline supervision in writing until adequate procedures exist. Trap four says a code of ethics is enough. A code without detailed procedures, training and monitoring is not a compliance system.

HEART: The takeaway for Standard four C. Supervisors must make reasonable efforts to prevent and detect violations, investigate promptly while tightening supervision, and when the system is inadequate, report it, recommend fixes and decline the role, in writing, until it is fixed.

## Module 91.7, Guidance for Standard V

LEWIS: Standard five is about the work itself, and its title is Investment Analysis, Recommendations, and Actions. It has three parts, and a simple way to hear them is think, tell and keep. Standard five A, Diligence and Reasonable Basis, is about thinking carefully. Standard five B, Communication with Clients and Prospective Clients, is about telling clients honestly. And Standard five C, Record Retention, is about keeping the proof.

HEART: Standard five A, Diligence and Reasonable Basis. In plain words, two duties. First, do the work properly, carefully, thoroughly and with your own independent judgment, whether you are analyzing, recommending or acting. Second, every analysis, recommendation and action needs a reasonable and adequate basis, which means real research and investigation behind it.

LEWIS: Why does it exist? A client cannot see inside your work. They see a recommendation and a confident tone. This Standard is the promise that real thinking sits behind both.

HEART: How much diligence is enough? It depends. It depends on your firm's investment philosophy, on your role in the decision, and on the resources and support your employer gives you. An analyst building the case from scratch carries a different load from a portfolio manager who leans on the firm's research team. And it depends on the product. Researching a large utility's bonds is a different job from evaluating a private credit fund.

LEWIS: Before you recommend or act, work from the outside in. The economy, at home and around the world. The business itself, meaning its results, its track record and where it stands in the business cycle. If it is a mutual fund, what it charges and how it has done. Where any model you lean on can break. And whether the peer group you are valuing it against is a fair comparison at all. A cruise line valued against hotel chains may look cheap or expensive for reasons that have nothing to do with its own prospects.

HEART: Now relying on someone else's research, which is common and allowed. You may use research from a broker, an outside provider or a colleague, but you have to make reasonable and diligent efforts to confirm it is sound. Four questions do most of the work. Are its assumptions reasonable? How rigorous was the analysis? Is it current? And is the source objective and independent, or does it have a reason to be cheerful? If you have reason to suspect the research is not sound, you cannot rely on it.

LEWIS: A case. Garrett runs a small advisory firm. He subscribes to a newsletter he found through a social media post. Its author promises huge winners in tiny companies, will not explain his methods, and will not say who pays him. Garrett buys the newsletter's top pick for every client account, with no research of his own. Violation?

HEART: Yes, of Standard five A. He had no way to judge the newsletter's assumptions, rigor or independence, and secrecy about who pays the author is a reason for suspicion in itself. Material found online and on social media deserves more scrutiny, not less.

HEART: Next, quantitative models. Many recommendations now come out of a model, and a model is only as good as its inputs and assumptions. Before you rely on one, understand what goes in, what it assumes and where it breaks. Test it across a range of scenarios, including bad ones and conditions the historical data never showed. Someone who builds a model carries a heavier burden than someone who only uses it, but users still have to understand its assumptions and limits.

LEWIS: A case. Saul, a credit analyst at a private credit fund, adopts a vendor's model that scores small business loans. The model was built on data from a long economic expansion, and Saul never asks how it behaves in a recession. When a member of the investment committee asks what drives the scores, he cannot say. Violation?

HEART: Yes. He relied on a model without understanding its inputs and assumptions, and without testing it against the kind of downturn that matters most for credit. The same discipline applies when a bank or credit union runs its balance sheet through interest rate shocks larger than anything in recent history. The scenario the past never showed is often the one that hurts.

LEWIS: The same care applies when you hire outside managers, called external advisers or subadvisers, to run part of a portfolio. Review them as you would any investment, with consistent and objective criteria. Look at their code of ethics, their compliance procedures and internal controls, the quality of the returns they report, and whether they actually follow the strategy they claim. Then review them again on a regular schedule, not just once.

HEART: Now group research, a favorite trap. Many firms reach recommendations through a committee. If the committee's process gave a recommendation a reasonable and adequate basis, a member who disagrees does not have to step away from it. Reasonable people differ on the same evidence. She may ask to have her name taken off, but she is not required to. Stepping away becomes the question only if you believe the process itself lacked a reasonable and adequate basis.

LEWIS: A case. Priya sits on a five person research committee that spent weeks on a hotel chain, with property visits, a debt review and a full valuation, and voted to rate it a buy. Priya thought the valuation ignored a coming wave of new hotel supply and voted hold. She asks that her dissent be noted in the committee's file, and her name stays on the published report. Violation?

HEART: No. The process had a reasonable and adequate basis, and she made her view known in writing. She did not have to take her name off the report. Disagreeing with a sound group decision is not the same as lending your name to an unsupported one.

HEART: The recommended procedures for Standard five A. Members should push their firms to grade outside research on a regular cycle, using those same four questions. Firms, in turn, should insist that every report can show its basis is reasonable and adequate, put their research and due diligence process in writing, and grade research quality with measurable yardsticks that feed into analysts' pay.

LEWIS: They should also write down a minimum level of stress testing for computer models, covering how wide the scenarios go, how well the model has tracked reality over time, and how much its cash flows move when an assumption or input changes. Data vendors get checked for reasonableness and accuracy on a set cycle. And external advisers are judged against set criteria, again on a set schedule.

HEART: The traps for Standard five A. Trap one says you may never rely on someone else's research. You may, after reasonable checks, unless you have reason to doubt it. Trap two says a reasonable basis means being right. A well researched call that loses money is not a violation, and a lucky guess that wins is not diligence. Trap three says a dissenter must take her name off a group report. Not when the group's process was sound. Trap four says a model's output speaks for itself. You have to understand its assumptions and test it.

LEWIS: The takeaway for Standard five A. Be diligent, independent and thorough, have a reasonable and adequate basis for every call, check other people's research and models before you lean on them, and know that you can dissent from a sound group decision without stepping away from it.

HEART: Standard five B, Communication with Clients and Prospective Clients. In plain words, five duties. One, tell clients and prospects what services you provide and what those services will cost them. Two, describe the basic shape of your investment process, and tell them promptly about any change that could materially affect it. Three, point out the significant limitations and risks of that process. Four, exercise reasonable judgment about which factors really drive your analysis or recommendation, and make sure clients hear about them. Five, keep fact and opinion clearly apart.

LEWIS: Why does it exist? Clients choose an adviser partly for how the adviser invests and what it costs. If the process changes quietly, or the costs are buried, the client is no longer getting what they signed up for, and they cannot tell.

HEART: The Standard covers every way you communicate with clients, not only research reports and other written material. A phone call, a meeting, a video update and a text message all count.

LEWIS: Start with services and costs, the part added in twenty twenty four. If you deal with clients directly, tell them what you will do for them and what it will cost them, both when the relationship begins and again whenever the service or the price changes.

HEART: You do not have to quote exact dollar figures. A fee set as a share of assets cannot be known in advance anyway. What you owe is a reasonably detailed picture of every cost the client will bear, including charges from outside parties whose services the client ends up paying for.

LEWIS: A case. Joanna signs up a new client at a wealth firm. She explains her advisory fee clearly. She does not mention that the client will also pay the running expenses of the funds she uses, or a quarterly fee charged by the outside platform that holds the assets. Violation?

HEART: Yes, of Standard five B. The disclosure covers the client's cost for the services, including third party costs, not just the adviser's own fee. She did not need exact figures, but she did need reasonable detail about every layer.

LEWIS: Next, the investment process. Clients do not need every formula, but they need the basic shape: what you look for, how you choose, and how you build the portfolio. When something changes that could materially affect that process, tell them promptly. Material changes include handing stock selection to an outside subadviser, starting to use leverage or derivatives, or a change in who makes the decisions.

HEART: A case. Daphne runs a global bond fund that has always hedged its foreign currency exposure back to dollars, and clients bought it for that steadiness. She decides to stop hedging and start taking active currency positions to add return. She tells investors in the annual report, eight months later. Violation?

LEWIS: Yes. Dropping the hedge changes the fund's risk in a way that could materially affect what clients own, so it needed prompt disclosure. Mentioning it eight months later in an annual report is not prompt. And good results in the meantime would not cure it.

HEART: Then risks and limitations. Point out the significant risks and limitations of your process. With structured products, allocation strategies and other nontraditional investments, spell out the risks peculiar to that kind of product. And always frame outcomes in total return terms, gains and losses together, so the client sees the whole result. A note paying a generous coupon can still lose money overall if its principal falls.

LEWIS: Two limitations get named specifically. Liquidity is whether you can get your money out quickly without taking a real hit on price or fees. Capacity is how big a fund or strategy can grow before new money starts to hurt its returns. Think of a strategy that buys tiny companies. It may work beautifully with modest assets and stall once it grows, because it can no longer buy enough of each idea without pushing up the price.

HEART: Two more duties sit here. If an investment or a strategy becomes meaningfully riskier, or risky in a new way, clients hear about it. And changes to the process, including risks and limits you have only just spotted, go into regular updates.

LEWIS: Models come up again. If you give a client a projection from a quantitative model, explain the model's assumptions and limits. Without that context, the client cannot judge how uncertain the projection really is, and leaving it out can be a violation in itself.

HEART: Next, the important factors. Deciding which factors belong in a report is a judgment call. You are not expected to include everything, but you must include what matters to the conclusion, and a research report should always describe the basic characteristics of the security being analyzed. A short format, such as a one page list of rated stocks, can be fine if clients know fuller analysis is available. Because the choice is a judgment call, keep notes on what research you did, so you can give a client more on request.

LEWIS: Finally, fact versus opinion, which is heavily tested. Earnings forecasts, price targets and anything produced by statistical modeling are expectations, not facts. Label them as views.

HEART: A case. Otto, a biotech analyst, writes in a client note that a company's new drug will be approved next spring and that the shares will double. Violation?

LEWIS: Yes, of Standard five B. Approval and the price move are his opinions, written as facts. Had he written that he expects approval next spring and sets a price target of twice the current price, the same view would be properly labeled as opinion.

HEART: The traps for Standard five B. Trap one says cost disclosure means the adviser's own fee. It means the client's costs for the service, including third party costs, in reasonable detail rather than exact dollars. Trap two says process changes can wait for the next annual review. Material changes are disclosed promptly. Trap three says only written reports are covered. Every means of communication is. Trap four says a model's output is a fact. It is an expectation, and its assumptions and limits need explaining.

LEWIS: The takeaway for Standard five B. Tell clients what they get and what it costs, how you invest and when that changes, what the risks and limits are, which factors matter, and which parts are fact and which are opinion.

HEART: Standard five C, Record Retention. In plain words, leave a trail. Every analysis, recommendation and action, and every other investment conversation with clients and prospects, should be backed by records you create and keep.

LEWIS: Why does it exist? A reasonable basis means little if nobody can see it later. Records let you, your firm, your clients and regulators rebuild why a decision was made. Think of them as the receipts for your reasoning.

HEART: Three points carry most questions. First, the research records behind your conclusions and actions are the property of the firm, not of you. Second, the format does not matter. Anything you send a client, whether a letter, an email or a text message, has to be kept.

LEWIS: Third, when you change firms, the old support stays behind, and memory is no substitute for it. Rebuild the support at the new firm from public sources or from the company itself. Your expertise goes with you. The documentation behind a specific recommendation does not.

HEART: A case. Cedric covered a group of railroad stocks for five years at one firm and moves to a rival. In his first week, he types his old earnings model back in from memory, adds no new research, and reissues his buy rating under the new firm's name. Violation?

LEWIS: Yes, of Standard five C. His recommendation needs support created at the new firm, from public filings, information from the companies and fresh analysis, not a rebuild of the old firm's work from memory.

HEART: How long should records be kept? Follow the law and your firm's policy. When neither sets a period, the recommended minimum is seven years.

LEWIS: And who keeps them? Keeping the records is generally the firm's job. But you still have to create the support for your own work and hand it over, so the firm has something to keep. In capital planning work, the same habit means keeping the assumptions behind a funding or capital forecast so that someone else can rebuild it later.

HEART: The traps for Standard five C. Trap one says your research belongs to you because you built it. It belongs to the firm. Trap two says only formal reports count. Emails, texts and every other client communication count. Trap three says you may rebuild your old analysis from memory at the new firm. You must recreate it from public or company information. And trap four guesses a number of years. When no law or firm rule applies, the answer is seven.

LEWIS: The takeaway for Standard five C. Keep the support for every analysis, recommendation, action and client communication, in any medium. It belongs to the firm, it stays when you leave, and when no other rule applies you keep it at least seven years.

## Recall check

LEWIS Q: An analyst who plans to start a rival firm spends her evenings meeting investors to fund it and interviewing people from outside the firm to hire later. She contacts no clients and takes nothing. Has she violated Standard four A?

HEART: Answer. No. Here is why. Preparing to compete is allowed while you are still employed, as long as the preparations do not breach the duty of loyalty. Soliciting clients, taking records or misusing confidential information before leaving would be violations, and she did none of those.

HEART Q: The week before she leaves, a portfolio manager copies the client presentations she designed herself onto her personal laptop, reasoning that her own work belongs to her. Violation?

LEWIS: Answer. Yes, of Standard four A, Loyalty. Here is why. Work you produce on the job belongs to the firm in any format, even when you created it yourself, so taking it without permission is the breach, whether or not it is ever opened again. After she leaves, she may still contact former clients she finds through public sources, unless she signed a noncompete.

LEWIS Q: An employee finds that her firm is overcharging clients, and after internal complaints go nowhere, she gives copies of the billing files to the regulator. Is that a breach of loyalty?

HEART: Answer. No. Here is why. Loyalty to the employer ranks below protecting clients and the integrity of the market. Breaking confidentiality is justified in isolated cases when the purpose is to stop wrongdoing that harms clients or markets. It would not be justified if she were using the information for personal gain.

HEART Q: A research analyst wants to manage a few private accounts for a fee on weekends, offering the same service his firm sells. What must he do first?

LEWIS: Answer. Give his employer a notice fully describing the services, the pay, the duration and the nature of the work, and wait for consent to all the terms before starting. Here is why. Paid outside work that competes with the employer is independent practice under Standard four A. It is allowed only when the employer knows exactly what is involved and agrees before it begins.

LEWIS Q: A client says she will pay for her adviser's evening finance course if her account beats its benchmark next year. What must the adviser do, and under which Standard?

HEART: Answer. Get written consent from all parties involved, including the employer, before accepting, under Standard four B, Additional Compensation Arrangements. Here is why. The perk depends on future performance, so it could change how the adviser treats that account compared with others. A thank you gift for a year already finished would instead be a gift under Standard one B, handled by disclosure to the employer.

HEART Q: A supervisor learns that a trader made personal trades without the approval firm policy requires. The trader apologizes and promises it will not happen again. Is the supervisor's duty met?

LEWIS: Answer. No. Here is why. Standard four C requires a prompt and thorough investigation, with tighter supervision or limits on the trader's activities while it runs. A promise neither tells the supervisor what happened nor stops it from continuing. Reasonable efforts mean finding out and controlling the risk.

LEWIS Q: Someone offered a supervisory role finds the firm's compliance procedures inadequate. What does Standard four C call for?

HEART: Answer. Bring the gaps to senior management, recommend corrective action, and decline the supervisory responsibility until adequate procedures are adopted, putting that refusal in writing. Here is why. A supervisor cannot make reasonable efforts to prevent and detect violations without an adequate system. Accepting the role anyway and hoping for the best is the tempting wrong answer.

HEART Q: Before relying on a broker's research, what four qualities should an analyst check?

LEWIS: Answer. Its assumptions, the rigor of the analysis, how timely it is, and the objectivity and independence of the source. Here is why. Standard five A lets you use other people's research only after reasonable and diligent efforts to confirm it is sound. Those four checks show whether it can bear the weight of your recommendation. Any reason to doubt its soundness means you cannot rely on it.

LEWIS Q: An analyst disagrees with her committee's buy rating, but the committee's process was thorough and well supported. Must she remove her name from the report?

HEART: Answer. No, she may keep her name on it and note her dissent. Here is why. A group conclusion with a reasonable and adequate basis satisfies Standard five A even when one member sees it differently. Stepping away is called for only if she believes the process itself lacked a reasonable and adequate basis.

HEART Q: An adviser discloses her own advisory fee but not the platform fee and fund expenses the client will also pay. Violation?

LEWIS: Answer. Yes, of Standard five B, Communication with Clients and Prospective Clients. Here is why. The cost disclosure covers what the client pays for the services, including costs from third parties, not just the adviser's own fee. Exact dollar amounts are not required, but reasonably detailed information about each cost is, at the start and whenever costs change.

LEWIS Q: A research note says a company will double its profit next year. What is the problem, and how is it fixed?

HEART: Answer. It states an opinion as a fact, which violates Standard five B, and it is fixed by labeling the forecast as the analyst's expectation. Here is why. Earnings forecasts and model outputs are expectations, not facts. Clients need to know which statements are verified facts and which are judgments that may prove wrong.

HEART Q: An analyst joins a new firm and wants to keep recommending a stock she covered before. How must she support the recommendation, and when no law or firm rule applies, how long should records be kept?

LEWIS: Answer. She must recreate the support at the new firm from public information or information from the company, and records should be kept for at least seven years. Here is why. Research records belong to the old firm, so her files stay behind, and memory is no substitute for documented support. Seven years is the recommended minimum when no regulation or firm policy sets a period.

## Key takeaways

HEART: First, a few memory hooks.

LEWIS: For Standard four A: loyal until the last day. Prepare, do not poach. Soliciting clients, taking records, misusing secrets and self dealing are poaching. After you leave, the names in your head are fine, and the firm's files are not.

HEART: For independent practice: describe it, then wait for a yes. Services, pay, duration and nature, and the employer consents to every term before you start. For whistleblowing: protect the clients, not your pocket.

LEWIS: For Standard four B: a reward for the past is a gift, a reward for the future needs paper. The gift is disclosed under Standard one B. The future reward needs written consent from everyone involved, and email counts.

HEART: For Standard four C: prevent, detect, investigate. A promise is not an investigation. And no system, no supervision. Raise the gaps, recommend fixes, and decline in writing until they are fixed.

LEWIS: For Standard five: think, tell, keep.

HEART: For Standard five A, check outside research with four questions: sound assumptions, real rigor, still current, truly independent. Understand a model before you trust it, and test it in the storm, not just the sunshine. A sound committee can outvote you without pushing you out. You may note your dissent and keep your name on.

LEWIS: For Standard five B, five things to tell: services and costs, the process and its changes, risks and limits, the key factors, and fact versus opinion. Costs means every layer the client pays, in reasonable detail. Material process changes go out promptly. Forecasts are opinions. Liquidity is getting out quickly and cheaply. Capacity is room for more money without hurting returns.

HEART: For Standard five C: receipts for your reasoning. The firm owns them, emails and texts included, they stay when you leave, and seven years is the floor when no other rule applies.

LEWIS: Now, how you would say the big ideas to a senior leader.

HEART: On departures: people can plan their exit, but until they leave they work for us, and our client lists and files stay here. The jargon is the duty of loyalty, solicitation and misappropriation of client lists.

LEWIS: On outside pay: if anyone else pays our people in a way that could conflict with their work for us, especially for future results, we approve it in writing first. The jargon is additional compensation arrangements and written consent.

HEART: On managing people: supervisors need real systems to stop and catch problems, and when something goes wrong they investigate rather than accept a promise. The jargon is responsibilities of supervisors, reasonable efforts and an adequate compliance system.

LEWIS: On research quality: every recommendation needs homework we can show, including checks on outside research and stress tests of our models. The jargon is diligence and reasonable basis, third party research and scenario testing.

HEART: On talking to clients: they hear what they pay, how we invest, what can go wrong and when any of that changes, and opinions are labeled as opinions. The jargon is communication with clients, cost disclosure and distinguishing fact from opinion.

LEWIS: And on documentation: if the reasoning is not in the file, we cannot defend it, and the file belongs to the firm. The jargon is record retention.
